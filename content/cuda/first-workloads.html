Workload 1: a battery heat simulation

Engineers who design cooling for electric scooter and car batteries need to know how heat spreads through a battery pack. Lecture 1 showed a student project that simulated this on a CPU and a GPU.

The heat equation
∂T/∂t = α (∂²T/∂x² + ∂²T/∂y²) + Q
T is temperature, α the thermal diffusivity (how fast heat spreads), Q the heat the battery makes itself.
2-D update rule (5 points)Exam favourite
Tnew = T + α (up + down + left + right − 4T) + βQ
New temperature = old temperature + heat from the four neighbours + heat made inside.
1-D version
Tnew = T + α (left + right − 2T) + βQ
The same idea along a rod.
How much work
cells = height × width
updates = cells × time steps
A 1024 × 1024 grid has 1,048,576 cells per step.

The bracket is the finite difference version of the second derivative (the Laplacian). It says how much hotter or colder the four neighbours are than the cell itself. If all neighbours equal the cell, nothing changes.

Worked example from the workbook
  1. A cell at 25 degrees has neighbours up 20, down 30, left 25, right 35. α = 0.1 and βQ = 0.5.
  2. Neighbour sum = 20 + 30 + 25 + 35 = 110. Four times the cell = 100.
  3. Difference 110 − 100 = 10, times α = 1.
  4. Tnew = 25 + 1 + 0.5 = 26.5 degrees

Why this is perfect GPU work. Each cell needs only its own old value and four neighbours. No cell depends on another cell's new value in the same step. So one thread per cell can update the whole grid at once.

  3. Line 7int i = row * n + col;Border cells are held fixed. Threads outside the grid or on the border do nothing.

  5. Line 9- 4.0f * Told[i];The four neighbours minus four times the cell: the discrete Laplacian.
  6. Line 11}The update rule from the lecture.
Common mistake
A GPU heat code must keep two buffers. If threads wrote into the same grid they read from, some would read a neighbour's new value and some its old value, and the answer would depend on timing.
Going deeper: stability: why α must be small

Rewrite the 2-D rule as Tnew = (1 − 4α)T + α(sum of neighbours). Every weight must be zero or positive or the scheme amplifies errors. So 1 − 4α ≥ 0, α ≤ 0.25 in 2-D (and α ≤ 0.5 in 1-D, α ≤ 1/6 in 3-D). With α = 0.3 the centre weight is −0.2 and the simulation blows up. This is the explicit scheme stability limit, and in physical units α Δt ÷ h² must stay below the same bound.

Workload 2: image recognition and convolution cost

Lecture 1's second example was an image recognition pipeline: a camera frame goes through convolution layers, pooling and dense layers to a label. Almost all of the time is spent in convolutions, which are millions of multiply adds that do not depend on each other. That again is GPU work.

Normal convolutionExam favourite
mults = H × W × Cin × Cout × K × K
Every output pixel of every output channel looks at a K × K patch of every input channel.
Depthwise separable
depthwise = H W Cin K²
pointwise = H W Cin Cout
First one K × K filter per input channel, then a 1 × 1 convolution that mixes channels. MobileNet uses this.
Saving ratioExam favourite
separable ÷ normal = (K² + Cout) ÷ (K² × Cout)
For many output channels this tends to 1 ÷ K², so at most 9 times fewer for 3 × 3 filters.
Worked example: K = 3, 64 to 128 channels, 32 × 32 output
  1. Normal: 32 × 32 × 64 × 128 × 9 = 75,497,472.
  2. Depthwise: 32 × 32 × 64 × 9 = 589,824.
  3. Pointwise: 32 × 32 × 64 × 128 = 8,388,608.
  4. Separable total = 8,978,432.
  5. Ratio = (9 + 128) ÷ (9 × 128) = 137 ÷ 1152 ≈ 0.119, about 8.4 times fewer
Real world scenario: why MobileNet runs on your phone

Your phone camera can recognise faces and food in real time because networks like MobileNet use depthwise separable convolutions. In the lecture study, MobileNetV2 had more layers than a plain CNN but ran faster, because each layer needs far fewer multiplications.

Remember
Fewer multiplications does not always mean proportionally faster on a GPU. Depthwise layers do little work per byte loaded, so they are often limited by memory, not maths (chapter 4.2).
Lesson 3 of 3

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

To find tomorrow's temperature of a spot, look at its neighbours today. Every spot can do this at the same moment, so thousands of helpers can work together.

Explicit finite difference: Tnew = T + α(neighbours − 4T) + βQ, stable for α ≤ 0.25 in 2-D. Convolution cost H W Cin Cout K², separable saving (K² + Cout) ÷ (K² Cout).

Both workloads are stencils: each output reads a small fixed neighbourhood. Neighbouring threads read overlapping inputs, so loading a tile with a halo into shared memory cuts global reads (chapters 5.3 and 5.4). That is why the "Shared GPU" column of the battery table exists.

Production codes use implicit or multigrid solvers for stiff heat problems, temporal blocking to reuse data across time steps, and for CNNs the convolution is turned into a matrix multiply (im2col) or computed with Winograd or FFT methods on tensor cores.

Work it out: a rod with fixed ends (workbook P1.5.4)
  1. Start [0, 0, 100, 0, 0], α = 0.25.
  2. Step 1: middle 100 + 0.25(0 + 0 − 200) = 50. Its neighbours 0 + 0.25(0 + 100 − 0) = 25. Rod [0, 25, 50, 25, 0].
  3. Step 2: middle 50 + 0.25(25 + 25 − 100) = 37.5. Neighbours 25 + 0.25(0 + 50 − 50) = 25.
  4. [0, 25, 37.5, 25, 0]
