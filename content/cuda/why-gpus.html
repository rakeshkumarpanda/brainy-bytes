Lesson 1 of 5

Two ways to build a processor

A computer chip can spend its area on two kinds of things. It can spend it on clever control and big caches, so that each instruction finishes as soon as possible. Or it can spend it on many small arithmetic units, so that a huge number of simple instructions finish together. The first choice gives a CPU. The second gives a GPU.

Part of the chipCPUGPU
Control logic (decides what runs next)Large and clever: branch prediction, out of order executionSmall and simple, shared by a group of threads
Cache (fast memory on the chip)Large per coreSmall per core, plus a shared L2
ALUs (the units that add and multiply)A few, powerfulThousands, small and energy efficient
ClockHigh, about 2 to 5 GHzModerate, about 0.8 to 2 GHz
Built forLow latency: one task, fastHigh throughput: many tasks per second
Real world scenario: the school bus and the race car

A race car takes one child to school in 5 minutes. A bus takes 20 minutes but carries 50 children. For one child the car wins. For a whole school the bus wins by a mile. A CPU is the race car. A GPU is a fleet of buses. Neither is "faster" in general. It depends on whether you move one child or a thousand.

Real world scenario: the gardener (from the lecture)

One 25 year old gardener picks up any single leaf faster than a 10 year old. But leaves are scattered everywhere, and 25 children can each grab a different leaf at the same moment. The children clear the garden first. Each child is slower, the team is faster. That is a GPU.

Common mistake
GPU does not mean "always faster". A GPU wins only when there is enough independent work to keep thousands of small units busy. For a single small task the CPU usually wins.

Teachers in the class asked if a GPU is SIMD and a CPU is SISD. Broadly yes. SISD means a single instruction works on a single piece of data. SIMD means one instruction works on many pieces of data at once. NVIDIA calls its version SIMT, single instruction multiple threads: 32 threads (a warp) run the same instruction together, but each thread has its own registers and can take its own branch. Chapter 2.3 shows this in detail.

Lesson 2 of 5

Latency, throughput and frames per second

Latency and throughputExam favourite
Latency = time to finish ONE job
Throughput = jobs finished ÷ time taken
A CPU is built for low latency. A GPU is built for high throughput.
Frames per second
FPS = 1000 ÷ (ms per frame)
ms per frame = 1000 ÷ FPS
There are 1000 milliseconds in one second.
Time for a batchExam favourite
T = ⌈N ÷ C⌉ × (time per round)
N jobs, C jobs done together in one round. ⌈ ⌉ means round up, because a part full round still costs a whole round.
Pipeline
latency = sum of stage times
throughput = 1 ÷ (slowest stage)
Once the line is full, one result comes out every time the slowest stage finishes.
Worked example from the workbook: who clears 1000 images first?
  1. CPU: 10 ms per image, one at a time. GPU: 15 ms per round, 1000 images per round.
  2. One image: CPU 10 ms, GPU 15 ms. The CPU has lower latency.
  3. Pile of 1000: CPU needs 1000 × 10 = 10,000 ms = 10 s.
  4. GPU needs ⌈1000 ÷ 1000⌉ = 1 round = 15 ms.
  5. Throughput: CPU 1000 ÷ 10 = 100 images per second. GPU 1000 ÷ 0.015 ≈ 66,667 images per second.
  6. The GPU has about 667 times the throughput, even though each image takes longer
Real world scenario: a toll plaza on a highway

One toll booth with a very fast operator still makes a long queue at rush hour. Twenty booths with slower operators clear the queue far sooner. Each car still waits about as long at its own booth, so latency per car does not drop, but cars per minute go up twenty times.

The inference study in the lecture. Ten small models were timed on an Intel Xeon CPU, an NVIDIA T4 GPU and a custom circuit on an FPGA board. Model M1 (three convolution layers) ran at 13.39 FPS on the CPU, 15.07 on the GPU and 1464.05 on custom hardware. The GPU gain was only about 1.13 times because M1 is tiny and the T4 had too little parallel work. Custom hardware has no instruction fetch or decode at all, which is why it won by about 97 times. The tailor analogy from class: ready made clothes (CPU, GPU) fit everyone, a tailored suit (custom hardware) fits one person perfectly.

Lesson 3 of 5

Clock speed, power and peak work

Cycle time
tcycle = 1 ÷ f
1 GHz = 10⁹ ticks per second, so one tick lasts 1 ns. At 800 MHz a tick lasts 1.25 ns.
Dynamic powerExam favourite
Pdyn ∝ α · C · V² · f
α is how often the circuit switches, C its capacitance, V the voltage (squared), f the clock. A faster clock needs a higher voltage, so power grows much faster than speed.
Peak work per secondExam favourite
peak = (FLOPs per tick per core) × f × cores
One FMA, a × b + c, counts as 2 FLOPs.
Why GPUs add cores instead of chasing clock speed (workbook P1.2.3 and P1.2.4)
  1. Running 20 percent faster needs about 10 percent more voltage.
  2. Power factor = 1.1² × 1.2 = 1.21 × 1.2 = 1.452. Power rises 45 percent for only 20 percent more speed.
  3. Now go the other way: halve both voltage and clock. One slow core uses (½)² × ½ = ⅛ of the power.
  4. So 8 slow cores fit in the power budget of 1 fast core.
  5. 8 cores × ½ speed = 4 times the work for the same power, if the work can be split
Going deeper: the power wall

Until about 2005 every new chip shrank its transistors and lowered the voltage at the same time (Dennard scaling), so clocks rose for free. Below about 1 volt the transistors leak current, the voltage could not keep falling, and power per chip hit the limit a fan can cool (about 100 to 300 W). Clock speeds stopped near 3 to 5 GHz. Since then extra transistors have been spent on more cores. GPUs push this to the extreme.

Remember
Peak FLOP/s is a ceiling, not a promise. Real kernels usually reach a fraction of it, because they wait for memory. Chapter 4.2 (the roofline) shows how to predict that fraction.

CPU, GPU, TPU, NPU, FPGA and ASIC

PlatformStrengthWeaknessExamples from the lecture
CPUMost flexible, cheapest, runs any programLimited parallelismArm Cortex-M, Raspberry Pi
GPUMassive parallelism, good programmabilityPower hungryNVIDIA Jetson, T4, A100, Arm Mali
FPGACustom circuits, low latency, good work per watt, can be reprogrammedSmall on chip memory, needs hardware design skillXilinx Zynq, Intel Arria 10
ASIC (TPU, NPU)Lowest power, highest performance for one taskVery costly to design, long time to market, fixedGoogle Edge TPU, Ascend 310

Moving from CPU to GPU to FPGA to ASIC trades flexibility for efficiency. TPU is Google's name for its own neural accelerator, and other makers call theirs an NPU. A smartwatch that checks your heartbeat the same way all day is a perfect job for a tiny NPU. A GPU is kept because its work keeps changing: a heat simulation today, image processing tomorrow, a language model next week.

Host and device
Host = CPU (starts the program, owns the operating system)
Device = GPU (runs the parallel parts you send it)
Every GPU system is driven by a CPU. CUDA is the library and language extension that lets the host hand work to the device.
In the exam
A GPU never runs alone. The CPU boots the machine, runs the serial parts and launches GPU work. This is why the time to copy data between them matters so much (chapter 1.2).
Lesson 5 of 5

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

A CPU is one very fast worker. A GPU is thousands of slower workers. If the job can be split into many small pieces that do not depend on each other, the many workers finish first.

Latency is time per job, throughput is jobs per second. Peak = FLOPs per tick × clock × cores. Power ∝ V²f, so GPUs use many cores at a moderate clock. Use ⌈N ÷ C⌉ rounds for batch time.

GPUs hide the long latency of each operation by keeping thousands of threads in flight and switching between them every cycle at no cost. The design only pays off when the program exposes enough parallelism, which Little's law turns into a number in chapter 2.3.

Modern accelerators mix the ideas: GPUs gained tensor cores (fixed function matrix units), and research chips explore near memory and in memory computing, systolic arrays (the TPU) and dataflow designs. Picking hardware is now a question of matching arithmetic intensity, precision and memory bandwidth to the workload.

Work it out: the T4 peak (workbook P1.2.5)
  1. Cores = 40 SMs × 64 = 2560.
  2. FLOPs per tick = 2560 × 2 = 5120 (one FMA each).
  3. Per second = 5120 × 1.59 × 10⁹ = 8.1408 × 10¹².
  4. Peak ≈ 8.14 TFLOP/s, and the T4 data sheet says 8.1
Going deeper: why the GPU took over deep learning

Training a neural network is mostly matrix multiplication, millions of identical multiply adds. That is exactly the kind of work a GPU was built for in graphics. In 2012 AlexNet was trained on two consumer GPUs, and since then GPU makers have added hardware for the exact number formats AI uses (chapter 8.1).
