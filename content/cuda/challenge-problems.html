Problem 1: when does batching pay off?

Problem. (a) An H100 SXM does 989 TFLOP/s in BF16 and moves 3.35 TB/s. From which batch is a 4096 × 4096 fully connected layer math limited? (b) Repeat for a 1024 × 1024 layer on an A100 (312 TFLOP/s, 1.555 TB/s). (c) Explain why the smaller layer needs a bigger batch.

Solution (a)
  1. Ridge = 989 ÷ 3.35 = 295.2 FLOP/byte.
  2. Need 4096 B ÷ (4096 + 2B) > 295.2.
  3. (4096 − 590.4) B > 295.2 × 4096, so B > 344.9.
  4. B ≥ 345
Solution (b) and (c)
  1. For an n × n layer, AI = n B ÷ (n + 2B). With n = 1024 and ridge 200.6: 1024 B > 200.6 (1024 + 2B).
  2. 622.7 B > 205,455, so B > 329.9.
  3. Fewer weights means less reuse to spread the activation traffic over.
  4. B ≥ 330 for the 1024 layer, against 223 for the 4096 layer

Problem 2: the occupancy puzzle

Problem. A kernel on an A100 uses 256 threads per block, 40 registers per thread and 20 KB of shared memory per block. Which limit binds, and what is the occupancy? What is the cheapest change that reaches 100 percent?

Solution
  1. Registers: 40 × 256 = 10,240 per block. 65,536 ÷ 10,240 = 6.4, so 6 blocks.
  2. Threads: 2048 ÷ 256 = 8 blocks. Block limit: 32.
  3. Shared memory: 20 KB + 1 KB reserved = 21 KB. 164 ÷ 21 = 7.8, so 7 blocks.
  4. The smallest is 6 (registers): 6 × 8 = 48 warps of 64.
  5. 75 percent, limited by registers

To reach 8 blocks the kernel needs at most 65,536 ÷ (8 × 256) = 32 registers per thread (for example with __launch_bounds__(256, 8)), and at most 164 ÷ 8 − 1 = 19.5 KB of shared memory per block. Forcing registers down can cause spills to local memory, so measure before and after.

Problem 3: banks, strides and padding

Problem. Thread t of a warp reads s[t × k] from a float array in shared memory (32 banks of 4 bytes). (a) Give the conflict degree for k = 1, 2, 6, 8, 32. (b) Prove the rule. (c) A 32 × 32 tile is read by column. Show why padding each row to 33 floats removes the conflict.

Conflict degree for stride kExam favourite
degree = gcd(k, 32)
Thread t goes to bank (t k) mod 32.
Solution
  1. Threads t and u share a bank when (t − u) k is a multiple of 32, which means (t − u) is a multiple of 32 ÷ gcd(k, 32).
  2. So each bank is hit by gcd(k, 32) threads: k = 1 gives 1, k = 2 gives 2, k = 6 gives 2, k = 8 gives 8, k = 32 gives 32.
  3. Column read of a 32 × 32 tile: element (t, c) sits at 32 t + c, stride 32, a 32 way conflict.
  4. With rows of 33: address 33 t + c, stride 33, and gcd(33, 32) = 1.
  5. Padding to 33 makes every column read conflict free

Problem 4: work against steps in scan

Problem. An inclusive scan of n = 1024 values. (a) How many additions do Hillis and Steele and Blelloch each do? (b) How many parallel steps? (c) With 1024 threads, which one finishes first and which one is better on a GPU with far fewer lanes than n?

Hillis and Steele
adds = n log₂ n − (n − 1), steps = log₂ n
Step d updates n − 2d elements.
BlellochExam favourite
adds = 2 (n − 1), steps = 2 log₂ n
Up sweep then down sweep.
Solution
  1. Hillis and Steele: 1024 × 10 − 1023 = 9217 additions in 10 steps.
  2. Blelloch: 2 × 1023 = 2046 additions in 20 steps.
  3. With enough lanes, Hillis and Steele wins on steps.
  4. With few lanes the total work decides, and Blelloch does 4.5 times less.
  5. Short arrays in one block: Hillis and Steele. Large arrays: Blelloch, which is work efficient

Problem 5: choose the tile under a budget

Problem. A FP32 matmul block keeps a BM × 32 strip of A and a 32 × BN strip of B in shared memory, double buffered, within 48 KB. (a) What is the largest BM + BN? (b) Which square tile maximises AI, and what AI does it reach? (c) At 2 TB/s, what speed can it reach from DRAM traffic alone?

Solution
  1. Shared memory = 2 buffers × (BM + BN) × 32 × 4 bytes ≤ 49,152.
  2. So BM + BN ≤ 192.
  3. For a fixed sum, BM × BN is largest when BM = BN = 96.
  4. AI = (2 ÷ 4) × 96 × 96 ÷ 192 = 24 FLOP/byte.
  5. 24 × 2 TB/s = 48 TFLOP/s ceiling from DRAM traffic

In practice tiles are powers of two (64 or 128) for simple indexing, so 64 × 128 (AI 21.3) or a smaller BK are chosen. That is the kind of trade off the matmul ladder makes at every step.

Problem 6: divergence, overlap and scaling

Problem A. In one warp, threads with tid < 8 run path A of 20 instructions, the others path B of 10. What is the warp time and the lane efficiency? How would you rearrange the data to remove the loss?

Solution A
  1. Both paths run one after the other: 20 + 10 = 30 instruction slots.
  2. Useful lane slots: 8 × 20 + 24 × 10 = 400.
  3. Available: 32 × 30 = 960.
  4. 41.7 percent. Sort or group the work so each warp takes one path

Problem B. 8 chunks pass through copy in (4 ms), kernel (6 ms) and copy out (2 ms), one engine each. Serial and overlapped time?

Solution B
  1. Serial: 8 × 12 = 96 ms.
  2. The kernel is the slowest stage: 4 ms to fill, 8 × 6 ms of kernels, 2 ms to drain.
  3. 4 + 48 + 2 = 54 ms.
  4. Speedup 96 ÷ 54 ≈ 1.78

Problem C. A program is 95 percent parallel. What speedup do Amdahl and Gustafson predict on 1024 cores, and why do they differ?

Solution C
  1. Amdahl (fixed problem): 1 ÷ (0.05 + 0.95 ÷ 1024) = 19.6.
  2. Gustafson (problem grows with the machine): 0.05 + 0.95 × 1024 = 972.9.
  3. Amdahl keeps the work fixed, so the serial 5 percent dominates.
  4. Gustafson grows the parallel part, which is how GPUs are really used

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Hard problems are several easy ones joined together. Find the pieces first.

Write down the limit formulas you need: ridge, occupancy, gcd for banks, scan work, tile AI, pipeline time, Amdahl.

Always check with a second method: a small Python loop, a widget, or a limiting case such as n = 1.

Research papers on GPU kernels are built from exactly these models, then confirmed with Nsight measurements.
