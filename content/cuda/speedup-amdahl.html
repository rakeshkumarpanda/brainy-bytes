Lesson 1 of 4

Speedup and the trip over PCIe

SpeedupExam favourite
S = old time ÷ new time
S = 10 means the new version is ten times faster. With FPS, use new FPS ÷ old FPS.
Total GPU timeExam favourite
TGPU = Tcopy in + Tkernel + Tcopy out
The GPU is worth it only if TGPU < TCPU.
Copy time
Tcopy = bytes ÷ bandwidth
PCIe 3.0 x16 gives about 12 to 16 GB/s in practice. 12 GB/s = 12 × 10⁹ bytes per second.

The GPU has its own memory. Data lives in the CPU's memory first, so it must travel over the PCIe bus to the GPU, and results must travel back. That trip can cost more than the computing.

Real world scenario: a calculator friend across town

Your friend adds numbers 100 times faster than you, but lives a 30 minute bus ride away. For one sum you do it yourself. For ten thousand sums you take the bus. The bus ride is the PCIe copy.

Common mistake
Keep data on the GPU across many kernel launches. Copy in once, run all time steps, copy out once. Copying every step can turn a 158 times speedup into 12 times (workbook P1.3.5).
Lesson 2 of 4

Amdahl's law

Split a program into a part that can run in parallel (fraction P of the time on one processor) and a part that cannot (1 − P). With N processors the parallel part shrinks to P ÷ N, the serial part stays.

Amdahl's lawExam favourite
S(N) = 1 ÷ [ (1 − P) + P ÷ N ]
P is the parallel fraction measured on one processor, N the number of processors.
The ceilingExam favourite
Smax = 1 ÷ (1 − P)
Let N grow without limit. 95 percent parallel can never beat 20 times.
Working backwards
P = (1 − 1/S) ÷ (1 − 1/N)
From a measured speedup S on N processors.
Efficiency
E = S ÷ N
How much of each processor is really used.
Speeding up one part s times
S = 1 ÷ [ (1 − p) + p ÷ s ]
The same law for any part p that gets s times faster, for example a kernel.
Derivation in four lines
  1. Time on one processor: T₁ = (1 − P)T₁ + PT₁.
  2. On N processors only the parallel part is divided: TN = (1 − P)T₁ + PT₁ ÷ N.
  3. Speedup S = T₁ ÷ TN. The T₁ cancels.
  4. S(N) = 1 ÷ [(1 − P) + P ÷ N], and as N → ∞, S → 1 ÷ (1 − P)
Lecture 5 quiz: 1 percent serial, 61 cores
  1. 1 − P = 0.01 and P ÷ N = 0.99 ÷ 61 ≈ 0.016230.
  2. Denominator 0.01 + 0.016230 = 0.026230.
  3. S = 1 ÷ 0.026230 ≈ 38.125.
  4. Efficiency 38.125 ÷ 61 = 62.5 percent. Ceiling 1 ÷ 0.01 = 100
Real world scenario: cooking for a wedding

Fifty cooks can chop vegetables together, but the biryani must still simmer for 40 minutes in one pot. Adding cooks shortens the chopping, never the simmering. That 40 minutes is the serial part.

Common mistake
P is a fraction of the time on one processor, not a fraction of the lines of code. A tiny loop that runs for 90 percent of the time gives P = 0.9.
Lesson 3 of 4

Gustafson's law and scaling (beyond the lecture)

Amdahl keeps the problem size fixed. In practice people buy a bigger GPU to solve a bigger problem in the same time: a finer heat grid, a larger batch, a longer video. Then the parallel work grows with N while the serial part stays about the same.

Gustafson's law (scaled speedup)Exam favourite
Sscaled = N − (1 − P)(N − 1)
Here P is the parallel share of the time measured on the N processor run. The speedup grows almost in a straight line with N.

Example: P = 0.9 on 64 processors
  1. Amdahl: S = 1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.9 ÷ 64) = 1 ÷ 0.1140625 ≈ 8.77.
  2. Gustafson: S = 64 − 0.1 × 63 = 64 − 6.3.
  3. = 57.7. Both are right. They answer different questions
In the exam
In an exam, Amdahl is the default unless the question says the problem grows with the number of processors.
Lesson 4 of 4

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Extra helpers only speed up the part of a job that can be shared. The part that must be done by one person sets the limit.

S = 1 ÷ ((1 − P) + P ÷ N), ceiling 1 ÷ (1 − P). Count copy in plus kernel plus copy out. Use P = (1 − 1/S) ÷ (1 − 1/N) to measure how parallel a program is.

Amdahl applies to every part of a pipeline: a 10 times faster kernel inside an 8.66 ms frame where the kernel is 1.65 ms gives only about 1.21 times overall (workbook P1.4.7). Profile first, then speed up the biggest part.

Hill and Marty extended Amdahl to multicore chip design (few big cores against many small ones), and asymmetric chips like CPU plus GPU systems follow from it. Overlapping copies with compute (chapter 6.2) and unified memory with prefetching attack the serial copy term directly.

Work it out: the frame that will not speed up (workbook P1.4.7)
  1. Frame 8.66 ms, kernel 1.65 ms, so p = 1.65 ÷ 8.66 ≈ 0.1905.
  2. Kernel gets s = 10 times faster: S = 1 ÷ (0.8095 + 0.01905).
  3. S ≈ 1.21. The copies are the real bottleneck
Going deeper: break even size for a GPU

If the CPU takes 2 ns per element and the GPU takes a fixed 50 µs plus 0.2 ns per element, the GPU wins when 2n > 50,000 + 0.2n, so n > 27,778 elements. At 10 million elements the speedup is 20 ms ÷ 2.05 ms ≈ 9.76 (workbook P1.3.6). Every GPU decision has a break even size like this.
