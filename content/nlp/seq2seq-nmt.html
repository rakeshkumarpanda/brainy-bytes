Lesson 1 of 5

Neural machine translation

Machine translation turns a sentence x in a source language into a sentence y in a target language, for example x: "I like deep learning" and y: "我喜欢深度学习". Neural machine translation (NMT) does it with a single neural network trained end to end. The architecture is called sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) and uses two RNNs.

PartWhat it does
Encoder RNNreads the source sentence "il a m’ entarté" and produces an encoding of it
Encodingthe last encoder state, used as the initial hidden state of the decoder
Decoder RNNa language model that writes the target "he hit me with a pie", conditioned on the encoding
At test timeeach decoder output (the argmax word) is fed back in as the next input until <END>
Encoder
hienc = RNNenc(hi−1enc, xi),   i = 1 ... n
Reads the source word by word.
DecoderExam favourite
st = RNNdec(st−1, yt−1),   s0 = hnenc
Starts from the encoding and reads its own previous output.
Output
P(yt | y<t, x) = softmax(Wo st)
A distribution over the target vocabulary.
Real world scenario: translating a hotel review

A travel site shows a French review to an English reader. The encoder reads "le petit déjeuner était excellent", the decoder starts from its final state and writes "the", then "breakfast", then "was", then "excellent", then <END>. Every word choice depends both on the French sentence and on the English words already written.

Lesson 2 of 5

A conditional language model

The seq2seq model is a conditional language model. It is a language model because the decoder predicts the next word of the target y. It is conditional because every prediction also depends on the source x. This extra conditioning is the key difference from the RNN language model of chapter 3.1.

NMT probabilityExam favourite
P(y | x) = ∏t=1T P(yt | y1, ..., yt−1, x)
Each factor conditions on the target words so far and on the entire source sentence.
Training lossExam favourite
J = 1T ∑t=1T −log P(ytgold | y<tgold, x)
Teacher forcing: the decoder reads the gold previous word.
Score a three word translation
  1. The decoder gives the gold words probabilities 0.5, 0.4 and 0.9.
  2. P(y | x) = 0.5 × 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.18
  3. Losses: −ln 0.5 = 0.6931, −ln 0.4 = 0.9163, −ln 0.9 = 0.1054.
  4. J = (0.6931 + 0.9163 + 0.1054) ÷ 3 = 0.5716

How to train an NMT system: get a big parallel corpus, sentence pairs in both languages, and backpropagate the loss through decoder and encoder together. Real systems stack several RNN layers: the hidden states of layer i are the inputs of layer i + 1 (Sutskever et al. 2014 used 4 layers, Luong et al. 2015 stacked LSTMs as well).

In the exam
In the exam, name both parts: "language model" because it predicts the next target word, "conditional" because it conditions on the whole source x.

Seq2seq is versatile

The general idea is an encoder-decoder model. One neural network takes the input and builds a neural representation. Another network produces the output from that representation. When both input and output are sequences, it is a seq2seq model.

TaskInput sequenceOutput sequence
Summarisationlong textshort text
Dialogueprevious utterancesnext utterance
Parsinginput textthe parse written as a sequence
Code generationnatural languagePython code
Speech recognitionaudio frameswords
TransliterationHindi in Devanagari lettersthe same words in Latin letters
Parsing as a sequence
  1. Input: "John has a dog".
  2. A tree can be written with brackets: (S (NP John) (VP has (NP a dog))).
  3. Treat every bracket and label as a token.
  4. The decoder writes the tree token by token, so a seq2seq model can parse
Remember
Encoder and decoder do not need to be RNNs. The encoder can be a CNN over an image (image captioning) and both can be transformers.
Lesson 4 of 5

The bottleneck problem

Without attention the decoder sees the source only through one vector, the last encoder state. A long sentence must be squeezed into that one vector and then reconstructed into another long sentence, so information is lost. This is the bottleneck problem, and it is why translation quality of plain seq2seq drops for long sentences.

How tight is the bottleneck?
  1. A 50 word sentence with 256-dimensional embeddings holds 50 × 256 = 12,800 input numbers.
  2. The encoder must summarise them in a hidden state of 256 numbers.
  3. 256 ÷ 12,800 = 2 percent of the input size for the whole sentence
  4. The same 256 numbers must also hold the first word for as long as the decoder needs it.

Early fixes reversed the source sentence (Sutskever et al. 2014), which brings the first source words close to the first target words. The real fix is attention (chapter 4.2): let every decoder step look at all encoder states directly.

Going deeper: quality against sentence length

Cho et al. (2014) and Bahdanau et al. (2015) plotted BLEU against sentence length. The plain encoder-decoder was competitive up to about 20 words and then fell steadily, while the attention model stayed nearly flat up to 50 to 60 words. That plot is the clearest evidence for the bottleneck.

Low-resource NMT and the parameter question

NMT needs a big parallel corpus, which most of the world’s 7,000 languages do not have. The lecture lists six ways to cope.

ApproachIdea
Transfer learningtrain on a high-resource pair first, then fine-tune on the low-resource data
Multilingual NMTone model for many languages, low-resource languages share representations
Back translationtranslate monolingual target text back into the source to make synthetic parallel data
Data augmentationparaphrasing, noise injection, word replacement
Pivot translationA to English to B when direct A to B data is scarce
Unsupervised NMTonly monolingual data, no parallel corpus at all

Most of the parameters sit in the embeddings and the output layer, about 37.8 million of 38 million. The RNNs themselves are tiny. Variants change the total: concatenating the context to the decoder input adds 128 × 256 = 32,768, and a decoder with the same size as the encoder needs no bridge or projection.
One idea, four depths

A translation model reads the whole sentence in one language, then writes it word by word in the other, each time looking at what it already wrote.

Seq2seq models P(y | x) as a product of next word probabilities. It is trained on parallel sentences with teacher forcing and the average cross-entropy, and decodes by feeding outputs back in.

The single context vector is a bottleneck. Parameter counts are dominated by |V| × d matrices, which is why subword vocabularies (BPE) and tying input and output embeddings matter in practice.

Back translation (Sennrich et al. 2016) and multilingual models with a target language token (Johnson et al. 2017, zero-shot translation) are standard. Massively multilingual models such as NLLB-200 cover 200 languages with one network.
