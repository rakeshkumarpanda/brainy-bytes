Lesson 1 of 4

Two kinds of zero

Question 3 of the activity asked for the probability of S3 = Sam I do like linguistics. The first four bigrams are fine. Then the model needs P(linguistics | like), and linguistics never occurred in training. One zero factor makes the whole product 0, the log −∞ and the perplexity infinite. A model that does that cannot be compared with any other.

Out of vocabulary wordUnseen n-gram of known words
Example"linguistics" was never seen at all"am do": both words were seen, never together
Even the unigram is0fine
FixMap it to an <UNK> tokenSmoothing, backoff or interpolation

Sparsity is the normal state

The slide showed bigram counts from 9,222 sentences of the Berkeley Restaurant Project. The table is mostly zeros: "i to" and "i chinese" never occur, even though "I want to eat Chinese food" is a fine sentence. The toy corpus shows the same effect in miniature.

Why more context means more zeros
  1. With V = 10,000 words a unigram model has about 10⁴ parameters, a bigram 10⁸ and a trigram 10¹².
  2. A corpus of 10⁶ tokens contains at most 10⁶ different trigrams.
  3. So at least 99.9999% of possible trigrams are never seen.
  4. The toy trigram gives P(I | I do) = 0 for "Sam I do I like", although both bigrams "I do" and "do I" were seen. The bigram model gave 0.0096.
Going deeper: Should a zero always be smoothed away?

A student asked why we back off when we really want the trigram. The professor's answer used "John eats blue". That trigram is missing because the sentence is nonsense, and its absence is informative. Backing off to the frequent bigram "John eats" would give the nonsense a sizeable probability. The same goes for "I eats". Some zeros are accidental (the corpus is too small), some are structural (the grammar rules them out), and counts alone cannot tell them apart. Better discounting, class based models and neural models reduce the damage.

Lesson 2 of 4

<UNK>, Laplace and add-k

Step one: add a special token <UNK> to the vocabulary and map every unseen test word to it. "Sam I do like linguistics" becomes "Sam I do like <UNK>". The vocabulary grows from 6 to V = 7. But <UNK> has count 0 in training, so the MLE still gives it 0. Step two is smoothing.

Laplace (add-one)Exam favourite
PLap(w) = C(w) + 1N + V,   PLap(w | h) = C(h w) + 1C(h) + V
V counts the types the model can predict: here 7, with </s> and <UNK> but not <s>.
Add-k
P(w | h) = C(h w) + kC(h) + kV
A smaller k, such as 0.5 or 0.01, steals less from the seen words. Choose k on a dev set.

The bag of fruit

The professor's analogy: a bag holds 2 bananas and 2 apples. Someone asks for an orange and there is none. You want an orange in the bag, but the bag should keep its proportions. So if you add one orange, you also add one apple and one banana. That is why we add 1 to every count and grow the denominator by V.

Proof that Laplace still sums to 1
  1. Unigram: Σw (C(w) + 1) = Σ C(w) + Σ 1 = N + V.
  2. Divide by N + V: the total is (N + V) ÷ (N + V) = 1.
  3. Bigram, fixed history h: Σw (C(h w) + 1) = C(h) + V, using Σw C(h w) = C(h).
  4. Σw PLap(w | h) = (C(h) + V) ÷ (C(h) + V) = 1
  5. The same argument with k gives C(h) + kV. Adding 1 only to <UNK> would leave the others unnormalised.
Laplace bigram for S3
  1. Factors: (3+1)/(5+7) = 1/3, (3+1)/(5+7) = 1/3, (1+1)/(5+7) = 1/6, (1+1)/(2+7) = 2/9.
  2. P(<UNK> | like) = (0+1)/(3+7) = 1/10, and P(</s> | <UNK>) = (0+1)/(0+7) = 1/7.
  3. P = 1/3 × 1/3 × 1/6 × 2/9 × 1/10 × 1/7 = 1/17010 = 5.8789 × 10⁻⁵
  4. log₁₀ P = −4.23070, PP = 5.0713, PP1 = 7.0168.
  5. The Laplace unigram gives 4.3576 × 10⁻⁶ and PP 7.8245, so the bigram still wins once both are smoothed.
Common mistake
The answer sheet got this one wrong twice. It wrote P(linguistics | like) = 0.01 instead of 1/10. Then it multiplied in the unigram values 0.034 and 0.207 in brackets, which is neither a Laplace bigram nor a proper backoff, and does not sum to 1. SRILM's vocabulary also contains <s> (its file lists 8 unigrams), so its add-one numbers can differ slightly. In the exam, state your V.

Add-one has a cost. Rare words gain a lot (2 → 3 is +50%) while frequent words lose (5/22 = 0.227 drops to 6/29 = 0.207). With a real vocabulary of 50,000 types, add-one hands most of the probability to words never seen. That is why better methods exist.

Lesson 3 of 4

Better smoothing: discounting

Add-k gives every unseen word the same share. The better methods take a little from each seen count and give the freed mass to unseen words in proportion to a lower order model. The slide named four.

Absolute discountingExam favourite
P(w | h) = max(C(h w) − d, 0)C(h) + λ(h) P(w),   λ(h) = d · N₁₊(h •)C(h)
Subtract a fixed d of about 0.75 from every seen count. N₁₊(h •) is the number of different words seen after h.
Good-Turing
c* = (c + 1) Nc+1Nc,   P(unseen) = N₁N
Nc = number of types seen exactly c times. Things seen once tell you how much is still unseen.
Witten-Bell
λ(h) = N₁₊(h •)N₁₊(h •) + C(h)
A history followed by many different words is likely to be followed by a new one.
Kneser-NeyExam favourite
Pcont(w) = |{v : C(v w) > 0}|number of bigram types
Absolute discounting, but the lower order model counts how many different words come before w, not how often w occurs.
Problem D10: Kneser-Ney after "like" with d = 0.75
  1. Different words before each token: I 3, Sam 3, </s> 3, do 2, like 2, am 1. There are 14 bigram types.
  2. Pcont: I = Sam = </s> = 3/14, do = like = 2/14, am = 1/14. Compare the MLE unigram, where am and do tie at 2/22.
  3. like occurs 3 times, followed by </s> twice and Sam once, so λ = 0.75 × 2 ÷ 3 = 0.5.
  4. PKN(</s> | like) = (2 − 0.75)/3 + 0.5 × 3/14 = 0.4167 + 0.1071 = 0.5238
  5. PKN(Sam | like) = 0.0833 + 0.1071 = 0.1905. Unseen: I 0.1071, do and like 0.0714, am 0.0357. Total 1.

The textbook example for Kneser-Ney is "San Francisco". Francisco is a frequent word, but it almost only follows San. In a new context it should get a low backoff probability, and the continuation count gives it exactly that, while a plain unigram would rate it highly.

In the exam
Chen and Goodman (1998) compared all of these. Modified Kneser-Ney with interpolation was the most accurate classic n-gram smoother, and it is still the default in toolkits such as KenLM. In assignment 2 it also won on the Brown corpus.
Lesson 4 of 4

Backoff, interpolation and choosing λ

When the trigram has no evidence, use the bigram, and if that has none, the unigram. There are two ways to combine the levels.

Backoff (Katz 1987)
PBO(w | h) = P*(w | h) if C(h w) > 0, else α(h) PBO(w | shorter h)
Non linear: use the highest order with evidence. P* is discounted and α hands exactly the freed mass to the lower order, so the result sums to 1.
Linear interpolationExam favourite
P̂(w | h) = λ₃ P(w | u v) + λ₂ P(w | v) + λ₁ P(w),   Σ λ = 1, λ ≥ 0
Always mix all levels. Proof of normalisation: Σw P̂ = λ₃ · 1 + λ₂ · 1 + λ₁ · 1 = 1.
Problem E9: Katz backoff after "do" with d = 0.5
  1. do is followed once by I and once by like, so P*(I | do) = P*(like | do) = (1 − 0.5)/2 = 0.25. Left over β = 0.5.
  2. Unseen followers: Sam, am, do and </s>, with unigram mass (5 + 2 + 2 + 5)/22 = 14/22.
  3. α(do) = 0.5 ÷ (14/22) = 11/14 = 0.7857
  4. P(Sam | do) = P(</s> | do) = α × 5/22 = 0.1786, P(am | do) = P(do | do) = 0.0714. Total 1.
  5. Without α the sum would be 0.5 + 14/22 = 1.136, not a distribution.
Problem E4: interpolating S3, λ = 0.7
  1. Each factor = 0.7 × MLE bigram + 0.3 × Laplace unigram.
  2. Sam | <s>: 0.7 × 0.6 + 0.3 × 0.2069 = 0.4821. The same for I | Sam. do | I: 0.1710. like | do: 0.3914.
  3. <UNK> | like: 0.7 × 0 + 0.3 × 1/29 = 0.0103. </s> | <UNK>: 0.7 × 0 + 0.3 × 6/29 = 0.0621.
  4. P ≈ 9.99 × 10⁻⁶, log₁₀ P = −5.0005, PP = 6.814
  5. Nothing is zero any more, and the seen part keeps most of its bigram strength.

Choosing λ

The professor's answer to "what will the λ values be?" was the standard machine learning recipe: split the data into train, dev and test. Train the counts, try λ values on dev, keep the one with the lowest dev perplexity, and report the test perplexity once.

Grid search works for one or two values. A finer search puts the minimum near λ = 0.48 with PP ≈ 6.43. For many λ values, such as one per bucket of history counts in Jelinek-Mercer smoothing, the EM algorithm maximises held out likelihood and never makes it worse from one step to the next.

Going deeper: Stupid backoff

Brants and colleagues (2007) built translation systems on web scale data with a shortcut: use the relative frequency if the n-gram was seen, otherwise 0.4 times the score of the shorter history, with no discounting. For "do I am Sam" with a trigram it gives 0.2 × 1 × (0.4 × 0.4) × 0.5 × 1 = 0.016. It is not a probability, since a row can sum to more than 1, so no true perplexity can be computed. With enough data it ranks candidates well.

One idea, four depths

Never let a model say "impossible" about a sentence that could happen. Borrow a little probability from what was seen and share it with what was not.

<UNK> handles unknown words. Add-k is simple but crude. Discounting methods (absolute, Good-Turing, Witten-Bell, Kneser-Ney) take a little from seen counts. Backoff uses the highest order with evidence. Interpolation mixes all orders with λ tuned on dev data.

Every smoother must keep each conditional distribution normalised: Laplace by adding kV to the denominator, backoff through α, interpolation through Σλ = 1. Kneser-Ney's continuation count is the marginal constraint that makes lower order models predict well in new contexts.

Kneser-Ney is a limiting case of a hierarchical Pitman-Yor process (Teh 2006). Neural models avoid zeros with a softmax, and research still uses n-gram statistics for retrieval, data deduplication and checking test set contamination.
