Lesson 1 of 5

Words have parts

Look at play, plays, played, playing, player and replay. A person sees one idea dressed six ways. A computer that compares strings sees six unrelated words, so evidence about one form never helps the others. Stemming and lemmatisation exist to undo this, and both rest on morphology, the study of how words are built from parts.

Morphemes, a quick reminder

Chapter 1.1 met the morpheme, the smallest piece that carries meaning or a grammar job. A free morpheme stands alone (help, build). A bound morpheme must attach to something (un-, -ful, -ing). The lecture worked problem split four words.

Two kinds of suffix

This table decides what the two tools should do. A lemmatiser removes inflection only: walked becomes walk, but teacher stays teacher because it is a separate dictionary word. A stemmer cuts both kinds without asking, so national and nation can both become nation.

Vafter ≤ Vbefore,   reduction = 1 − VafterVbefore
Merging forms can only lower the number of types. Above, 5 types become 1, a reduction of 80 percent.

c(connect*) = c(connect) + c(connected) + c(connecting) + …
Evidence that was split over five rare types is pooled into one common type. Chapter 4 shows why fewer, larger counts give better probabilities.
Real world scenario: searching a news archive

A reader searches an Indian news archive for "flooding in Assam". Articles say "floods", "flooded" and "flood". With exact matching most relevant stories are missed. With stemming, flooding, floods and flooded all become flood, and the search finds them. The cost is that "flood of complaints" about a phone launch may also match.

Remember
Hindi, Turkish, Finnish and Tamil pack many more endings into one word than English does. One Turkish verb can have hundreds of forms, so normalisation matters even more for them.
Lesson 2 of 5

Stemming: chop with rules

A stemmer removes endings using a fixed list of rules and no dictionary. The piece that is left is the stem. It is fast and needs no data, but the stem does not have to be a real word. The professor's own examples: gaming gives game, which happens to be a word, while leaves gives leav, which is not.

Python with NLTK, as run in the class notebook
from nltk.stem import PorterStemmerps = PorterStemmer()stem_words = ["game", "gaming", "gamed", "games", "leaves", "cared", "children",              "sportingly", "fairly", "saw", "loving", "boy's"]for word in stem_words:    print(ps.stem(word))

Read the output carefully, because each line teaches something.

  • game, gaming, gamed and games all become game. Four forms now share one count.
  • leaves becomes leav. The rule removed -es and did not know that the singular is leaf.
  • children is left alone. No suffix rule turns an irregular plural into child.
  • sportingly becomes sportingli and fairly becomes fairli. Porter rewrites a final y as i so that happy and happiness meet at happi.
  • saw is unchanged. A stemmer cannot know that saw can be the past of see.

The measure m

Porter only removes a long ending when enough of the word is left. He measured "enough" by writing the word as runs of consonants (C) and vowels (V). The letters a, e, i, o and u are vowels, and y is a vowel when it follows a consonant.

Porter's word shapeExam favourite
[C] (VC)m [V]
Square brackets mean optional. m counts how many times a vowel run is followed by a consonant run.
Examples from Porter (1980)
m = 0: tree, by
m = 1: trouble, oats, ivy
m = 2: troubles, private
tr-ee is C V, so m = 0. tr-ou-bl-e is C V C V, so m = 1.

A rule such as "(m > 1) EMENT → nothing" turns replacement into replac (m of replac is 2) but leaves cement alone, because the stem c has m = 0. The condition stops the stemmer from cutting short words to nothing.

In the exam
In an exam, write the C and V pattern under the letters first, then count VC pairs. Most mistakes come from forgetting that y after a consonant is a vowel.

Inside the Porter algorithm

Martin Porter published the algorithm in 1980 for search engines. It has five steps, applied in order. Each step holds a list of rules of the form (condition) SUFFIX → REPLACEMENT, and within a step only the longest matching suffix is used.

StepJobExample rulesExamples
1aPluralsSSES → SS, IES → I, SS → SS, S → nothingcaresses → caress, ponies → poni, cats → cat
1bPast tense and -ing(m > 0) EED → EE, (*v*) ED → nothing, (*v*) ING → nothing, then tidy upplastered → plaster, motoring → motor, hopping → hop, sing → sing
1cFinal y(*v*) Y → Ihappy → happi, sky → sky
2Double suffixes(m > 0) ATIONAL → ATE, IZATION → IZE, FULNESS → FULrelational → relate, vietnamization → vietnamize
3More endings(m > 0) ICATE → IC, FUL → nothing, NESS → nothingtriplicate → triplic, hopeful → hope, goodness → good
4Long suffixes(m > 1) AL, ANCE, ENCE, ER, IC, ABLE, IBLE, ANT, EMENT, MENT, ENT, ION, ISM, ATE, IVE … → nothingrevival → reviv, adjustable → adjust, adoption → adopt
5Tidy the end(m > 1) E → nothing, (m > 1 and double L) → single Lprobate → probat, rate → rate, controll → control

The symbol *v* means "the stem contains a vowel". It is why sing keeps its -ing: removing it would leave s, which has no vowel.

Trace: hopefulness with NLTK PorterStemmer
  1. Step 1a and 1b: no plural, past or -ing ending. hopefulness stays.
  2. Step 2: FULNESS → FUL needs m > 0 on the stem hope. m(hope) = 1, so hopefulness → hopeful.
  3. Step 3: FUL → nothing needs m > 0 on hope. It applies, so hopeful → hope.
  4. Step 4: no long suffix matches hope.
  5. Step 5: E → nothing needs m > 1 on the stem hop, but m(hop) = 1. The other branch (m = 1 and the stem hop does not end in consonant vowel consonant) fails too, because hop does end in c v c. So the e stays.
  6. hopefulness → hope
Going deeper: NLTK mode against the original paper

NLTK's PorterStemmer runs in NLTK_EXTENSIONS mode by default. It adds a few fixes that the 1980 paper does not have. One is that a four letter word ending in ies keeps ie, so ties becomes tie in NLTK but ti in the paper. Another is a small exception list that maps forms such as dying and lying to die and lie. When an exam asks you to apply "the Porter rules", use the paper rules and say so.

Lesson 4 of 5

Lemmatisation: look it up

A lemma is the dictionary form of a word, the form you would look up. children has the lemma child, leaves (the noun) has the lemma leaf, and better (the adjective) has the lemma good. A lemmatiser always returns a real word, because it uses a dictionary rather than chopping blindly.

The part of speech matters

The same spelling can have different lemmas. saw as a verb is the past of see, but saw as a noun is a tool. leaves can be the plural of leaf or a form of the verb leave. So a lemmatiser needs to know the part of speech, and NLTK's WordNetLemmatizer assumes a noun when you do not tell it.

Python with NLTK, as run in the class notebook
lemma = nltk.wordnet.WordNetLemmatizer()print(lemma.lemmatize('loving', 'v'))     # told it is a verbfor word in stem_words:    print(lemma.lemmatize(word))          # no POS given, so noun is assumed

How WordNet's lemmatiser works

  1. Look the word up in an exception list for that part of speech (children → child, geese → goose).
  2. Otherwise try the detachment rules for that part of speech, such as -ies → -y, -es → nothing and -s → nothing for nouns.
  3. Keep a result only if it is a real entry in WordNet.
  4. If nothing works, return the word unchanged.
Common mistake
Run a POS tagger first and pass each tag to the lemmatiser. Without the tag, most verbs and adjectives come back unchanged, exactly as in the notebook output above.
Real world scenario: a voice assistant booking cabs

A rider says "I booked a cab yesterday, cancel the bookings." The intent system has one entry for the action book and one for the object booking. A lemmatiser with POS tags maps booked (verb) to book and bookings (noun) to booking, which keeps the action and the object apart. A stemmer would turn both into book and blur the two.

Stem or lemma: choosing well

The professor said this question will appear in the exam: "What is a lemma and what is a stem? Answer with examples." Here is a complete answer.

StemLemma
DefinitionWhat is left after rule based suffix strippingThe dictionary (citation) form of the word
Real wordNot always: leaves → leav, studies → studiAlways: leaves → leaf, studies → study
NeedsOnly a rule listA dictionary and the part of speech
Irregular formsMissed: children, saw, better unchangedHandled: child, see (verb), good (adjective)
SpeedVery fastSlower (tagging plus lookup)
Typical useSearch engines, quick text classificationQuestion answering, chatbots, linguistics

Two kinds of mistake

  • Over stemming merges words that mean different things. university, universe and universal all become univers in Porter.
  • Under stemming fails to merge forms of the same word. alumnus becomes alumnu but alumni stays alumni, so the two never meet. children and child never meet either.

The notebook exercise tested both stemmers on thirteen words. They agreed on twelve and differed only on alumnus (Porter alumnu, Snowball alumnus). Neither joined alumni with alumnus.

Python with NLTK, as run in the class notebook
stem_words = ["alumnus", "running", "alumnu", "alumni", "alumna", "alumnae", "cats",              "catty", "cat", "chocolates", "chocolatey", "choco", "children"]print([snowball.stem(w) for w in stem_words])
Output recorded in the notebook
['alumnus', 'run', 'alumnu', 'alumni', 'alumna', 'alumna', 'cat', 'catti', 'cat', 'chocol', 'chocolatey', 'choco', 'children']

Measuring the effect in search

A search engine is judged by precision (the share of returned documents that are relevant) and recall (the share of relevant documents that are returned). Stemming usually raises recall, because more forms match, and can lower precision, because over stemming lets wrong documents in.

Precision and recallExam favourite
P = relevant returnedall returned     R = relevant returnedall relevant
Both lie between 0 and 1.
F1 score
F1 = 2 P RP + R
The harmonic mean. It is high only when both P and R are high.
One idea, four depths

Stemming cuts the end off a word with simple rules, so playing and played both become play. Sometimes the result is not a real word, like leav. Lemmatising looks the word up and gives the dictionary word, like leaf.

A stemmer applies ordered suffix rules with conditions on the measure m. A lemmatiser uses an exception list, detachment rules and a lexicon, and needs the POS tag. Stemming conflates inflection and derivation. Lemmatisation removes inflection only.

Normalisation trades recall for precision in retrieval and reduces sparsity for count based models. Over stemming and under stemming are measured with conflation based indices (Paice, 1994). For morphologically rich languages, finite state transducers (two level morphology, Koskenniemi 1983) analyse words into lemma plus features, such as walked → walk +V +PAST.

Neural models mostly replace explicit normalisation with subword tokens (chapter 2.1), which learn shared pieces like walk and ##ed from data. Lemmatisation remains useful for low resource languages, for interpretable features and for evaluation. Research studies neural morphological analysers and shared tasks such as SIGMORPHON reinflection.
