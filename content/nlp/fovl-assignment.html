Step 0. Read the paper and make a plan

The paper fixes four hard rules: only IndicCorpV2 for training, exactly 300 dimensions, no pre-trained vectors, and no pair may be dropped because of an unseen word. The test sets of Akhtar et al. (2017) exist for 7 languages, so the models must cover Gujarati, Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu. The plan was: look at the data, pick a model that can build vectors for unseen words, tune it on the public sets, then write the files in the exact submission format.

Step 1. Look at the data

IndicCorpV2-5k holds 118,243 lines in 24 languages, 5,000 lines each (Kashmiri has 3,243). After cleaning, each of our 7 languages has between 166,263 and 357,737 word tokens. That is tiny next to the billions of words used for published embeddings, so every choice below aims at squeezing meaning out of little text.

Common mistake
The Hindi test file is comma separated, the others are tab separated with Windows line ends. In the Marathi file 149 of the 253 pairs have no human score. They are kept in the .npy file (the paper asks for every pair in order) but Spearman for Marathi uses the 104 scored pairs. The Tamil file has one blank word.

Step 2. Clean the text

Each line is put in Unicode NFC form so that one letter is always stored one way, then every punctuation mark, symbol, separator, digit and ASCII character becomes a space, and the line is split on spaces.

Common mistake
The regular expression \w looks natural for splitting words, but it does not count the vowel signs of Indian scripts (ा, ि, ो) as word characters. It cut किताब into pieces and gave Hindi only 5,426 word types. Checking the Unicode category of each character gave 24,245 real word types.

Step 3. Choose the model: FastText skip-gram

Word2Vec and GloVe give no vector at all to a word they never saw, and between 10 and 30 percent of the test words never appear in our small corpus. FastText (Bojanowski et al., 2017) learns a vector for every character n-gram as well as for every word, so it can build a vector for any word from its pieces.

FastText word vectorExam favourite
vw = zw + ∑g ∈ G(w) zg
G(w) is the set of character n-grams of w with the marks < and >, here n = 3 to 6.
Skip-gram with negative sampling
J = −log σ(vw·uc) − ∑k=1..K log σ(−vw·unk)
c is a real context word, nk are K = 10 random words.
Cosine similarity
s(wi, wj) = vi · vj‖vi‖ ‖vj‖
1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated.
Macro average
Smacro = 1|L| ∑ℓ ∈ L ρℓ
Each language counts the same, whatever its number of pairs.

Step 4. OOV handling

The function get_vector never drops a word. A word in the vocabulary uses its trained vector. An unseen word gets the sum of its n-gram vectors, which were learned from other words with the same pieces. A test entry with two words, such as कुटुंब नियोजन (family planning), is cleaned like the training text and the word vectors are averaged.

Real world scenario: a word the model never read

कीबोर्ड (keyboard) does not occur in the 5,000 Hindi lines. Its pieces such as बोर्ड and र्ड> do. FastText adds them up and the nearest words of the new vector are बिलबोर्ड (billboard), डेशबोर्ड (dashboard) and बोर्ड (board). The meaning "some kind of board" survives even though the word itself was never seen.

Three lessons came out of the table. Skip-gram beats CBOW by a wide margin, because it gives every rare word its own updates. Keeping every word (min_count 1) and a wider window (8) both help a small corpus. One shared model for all languages, even with every Indic script mapped to Devanagari so that cognates share pieces, was clearly worse, because short pieces mean different things in different languages.

The first 100 different test words of each language go from 300 numbers to 2 with t-SNE (perplexity 15, PCA start, random_state 42). Words with the same stem or the same loan word sit together, for example the computer words in Hindi and Punjabi. Some groups share spelling more than meaning, which is the weak side of n-grams. Step 11. Limitations

  • 5,000 lines per language is the main limit. A larger slice of IndicCorpV2 would raise every score.
  • The test sets have only 97 to 235 pairs, so one pair can move Spearman noticeably, and the private test may differ.
  • N-gram vectors capture spelling as well as meaning, which misleads on names and loan words.
  • Choosing settings on the public sets can overfit them a little. The settings are general (window, min_count) rather than fitted per pair, which limits that risk.
