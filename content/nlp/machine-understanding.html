Lesson 1 of 5

Marr's three levels and the robot that flies

The lecture frames the course with one question: can machines understand language? Language is linked to thought, thought to thinking, and on the working view of cognitive science, thinking is information processing. If minds and computers both process information, we can ask whether a machine can carry out the same processing. That is why vision and language share one goal: reverse engineering human perception and language.

Level (Marr, 1982)QuestionWhat it specifies
1. ComputationalWhat and why?The goal of the computation and why it is the right goal
2. Algorithmic or representationalHow?The representation of input and output, and the algorithm that turns one into the other
3. ImplementationalWhere?The physical substrate: neurons, gears, silicon

The professor's summary: level 1 suggests level 2 and level 2 predicts level 3 when you design. When you validate, level 3 confirms level 2 and level 2 explains level 1.

Class activity: make a robot fly
  1. Computational: the goal is flight, so produce an upward force greater than the weight (more than 9.8 newtons for every kilogram).
  2. Algorithmic: flap the wings to make thrust, keep a streamlined body, run a control loop of ground, climb, balance, descend, ground.
  3. Implementational: wings, motors, light materials and sensors.
  4. Design runs top down. "Flap" in level 2 already predicts that level 3 needs a wing. Validation runs bottom up: a wing that flaps confirms the algorithm and the goal.

Marr's own textbook example is a cash register. Its goal is addition (level 1), done by column addition with carrying (level 2), in gears or transistors (level 3). An abacus and a chip both add with very different algorithms and hardware, which is why you cannot understand a system just by staring at its hardware.

Common mistake
The professor calls jumping straight to hardware and algorithms "the rush that all computer engineers make". A purely data driven language model is tuned at levels 2 and 3 without a clear level 1 answer to what understanding is.
Lesson 2 of 5

The Turing test and what it misses

Definitions of AI fall into a two by two grid, the scheme of Russell and Norvig's textbook.

Like humansRationally
ThinkingSystems that think like humans (Haugeland, Bellman)Systems that think rationally (Charniak and McDermott, Winston)
ActingSystems that act like humans (Kurzweil, Rich and Knight)Systems that act rationally (Schalkoff, Luger and Stubblefield)

Alan Turing (1950) replaced "can machines think?" with an operational question. A judge chats by text with a hidden person and a hidden machine. If the judge cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine passes. This defines intelligence by behaviour, not by inner experience.

Two problems the lecture lists

  1. It is not reproducible. The result depends on which judge asks and on the setup.
  2. There is no physical interaction. It tests only text, never seeing or acting.

A student added that behaviour leaves out subjective experience. Philosophers call this qualia, the "what it is like" of seeing red. Nagel's bat (1974), Jackson's Mary who knows all the physics of colour but has never seen red (1982), and Chalmers's hard problem (1995) all point at the same gap.

The Total Turing Test

Stevan Harnad's Total Turing Test asks the machine to perceive and act in the world, not just chat. To pass it a computer needs seven capabilities.

CapabilityWhy
Natural language processingTalk with the examiner
Knowledge representationStore what it is told
Automated reasoningAnswer questions and draw conclusions
Machine learningAdapt and find patterns
VisionRecognise the examiner's actions and objects
Motor controlAct on objects when asked
Other sensesHearing, touch, smell

This is why the course is called Foundations of Vision and Language. Even a vending machine joins vision (reading the menu), action (the purchase) and, with speech, language.

Lesson 3 of 5

ELIZA and SHRDLU: talking against understanding

Two famous systems from MIT give opposite answers to the question "if a machine fools you, does it understand?"

Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) imitated a Rogerian therapist who reflects what the client says. It found keywords, swapped pronouns and pasted your own words into a template: "my boyfriend made me come here" became YOUR BOYFRIEND MADE YOU COME HERE. Users still confided in it, an effect now named the ELIZA effect. The slide says it in blue: producing an appropriate looking response is not necessarily the same as understanding.

Terry Winograd's SHRDLU (around 1970) lived in a small simulated world of blocks, pyramids and a box. It parsed English commands, kept a model of the scene, reasoned and moved a simulated arm. It asked "I DON'T UNDERSTAND WHICH PYRAMID YOU MEAN" when a phrase fit two objects, resolved "it" to the right block, answered "how many blocks are not in the box?" from its world model, and said "I CAN'T" when asked to stack two pyramids.

ELIZA (1966)SHRDLU (about 1970)
CreatorJoseph Weizenbaum, MITTerry Winograd, MIT
PipelineLanguage → pattern match → responseLanguage → world model → reasoning → action → response
GroundingNone, text in and text outIn a simulated micro world

LessonFluent is not understandingLanguage can be procedure tied to knowledge and action, at small scale
Remember
SHRDLU's "OK" matters because it reports that a grounded action was carried out, closing the loop between language and behaviour.
Lesson 4 of 5

NLP, CL and NLU, and how systems are measured

Students kept asking how the three names differ. They differ in what drives the work, not in subject.

FieldOrientationGoal
NLPEngineering, application drivenBuild tools: search, speech to text, translation, sentiment
Computational linguisticsScience, theory drivenModel the human cognitive system for language
NLUThe shared end goalA system that really understands, built from description to representation to evaluation

The building blocks

  1. Corpus and annotation. Text (or speech and video) with labels, such as part of speech tags for "John sat near the bank" or the root "run" for "running". Universal Dependencies gives one scheme across many languages. There are also child language, learner and eye tracking corpora.
  2. Model. Hand built grammars such as the English Resource Grammar, or statistical and neural models learned from data.
  3. Validation with performance metrics and cognitive metrics.
Precision, recall, F1Exam favourite
P = TPTP + FP, R = TPTP + FN, F1 = 2PRP + R
Precision: how much of what you found is right. Recall: how much of the truth you found.
BLEUExam favourite
BLEU = BP · exp(Σn wn log pn)
pn is clipped n-gram precision, BP = 1 if c > r, else e1 − r/c. Papineni et al. 2002.
Surprisal
S(w) = −log2 P(w | context)
Surprising words take longer to read (Hale 2001, Levy 2008).
Dependency length
DL = Σi |i − head(i)|
Human sentences keep related words close (Gibson 2000).

Cognitive metrics ask whether a model behaves like people, not just whether it scores well. "Apples and oranges" is expected, so it has low surprisal. "Apples and coconut" is surprising and carries more information.

Lesson 5 of 5

Can form alone give meaning?

Is NLP just a giant knowledge engineering project, solved by enough data? The lecture gives the case for and against.

The case for data

  • Firth (1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." The distributional hypothesis behind word2vec, GloVe and the embeddings inside BERT.
  • Shannon (1948): information depends on probability, not meaning, I(x) = −log2 P(x). Next word prediction and cross entropy loss come straight from this.

The case against form alone

  • Huge models need data and compute most languages do not have, which splits the world into high and low resource languages.
  • The octopus test (Bender and Koller, 2020). An octopus taps a cable between two islanders and learns to imitate one perfectly. When a bear attacks and help is needed to build a weapon from sticks, it can only produce plausible text, because it never had access to sticks or bears. Form alone does not give meaning.
  • Ettinger (2020) ran psycholinguistic tests on BERT and found it largely insensitive to negation ("a robin is a ___" and "a robin is not a ___" get similar completions) and weak at predicting roles in events.
  • Symbol grounding (Harnad, 1990): symbols defined only by other symbols are like a dictionary in a language you cannot read. The Chinese room (Searle, 1980): following a rulebook to produce fluent output need not involve understanding.
  • Rare events carry the most information, yet frequency based learning handles them worst.

The NLP trinity

Every NLP problem sits on three axes: the language (Hindi, Marathi, English, French), the problem in rising difficulty (morphology, POS tagging, chunking, parsing, semantics, discourse and coreference) and the algorithm (HMM, MEMM, CRF, RNN). A tagger built for one point does not move to another for free.

One idea, four depths

A parrot can repeat words without knowing what they mean. A computer that copies patterns from text may also sound clever without understanding. To understand, words must connect to real things and real goals.

Processing maps input text to output text with learned statistics. Understanding also links symbols to referents and intentions. ELIZA processes. SHRDLU understands a little, inside its blocks world.

The debate maps onto Marr: large models are optimised at the algorithmic level with a proxy goal (next token prediction) whose relation to the computational level goal (meaning, intent) is unclear. Diagnostic suites such as Ettinger's test specific competences rather than overall scores.

Open questions include whether distributional signals implicitly encode reference, how multimodal and embodied training changes grounding, how to learn from rare but informative events, and how to evaluate understanding when benchmarks leak into training data.
