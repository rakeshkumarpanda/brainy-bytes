Lesson 1 of 5

What language is

Ask a class "what is language?" and you get many true but partial answers: a medium of communication, a reflection of thought, words in a grammatical order, a script of symbols. The lecture puts the pieces together into one working definition that the whole course uses.

Working definition from the lectureExam favourite
Language is a systematic, symbolic and structured system that (1) starts as thought in the mind, (2) organises chaotic ideas into structure, and (3) lets those ideas be sent out, carried and rebuilt in another mind.
Every later topic in this course, from tokens to parse trees, is a way of recovering some part of this structure from text.

From an idea to an utterance

The professor traced every sentence back to the mind with a simple chain: an idea forms, you conceptualise it, you articulate it, and you say it as a form (a sound, written letters or a sign). Keep this chain in mind. The lecture uses it three times, for one word, for a full conversation and for the origin of speech.

A window to the mind

Language does two jobs at once. It reflects the patterns of our thought, so studying how people build sentences gives indirect evidence of how they think. That is why linguistics is treated as a cognitive science. It also organises thought. Close your eyes for a minute and your raw thoughts overlap and drift. Language imposes order on them, and structure is what turns a pile of words into a claim that can be true or false.

Remember
Structure creates meaning. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same three words. A bag of words model that ignores order cannot tell them apart, which is why later chapters bother with tags, n-grams and parse trees.

Two questions you can ask about language

QuestionWhat it needsWhere it leads
How do I manipulate this string of words?String operations, patterns, counts and probabilitiesStatistical NLP: n-gram models, classifiers and today the next word prediction of large language models
What kind of system could produce this behaviour?A theory of the representations and rules inside a mind or machineSymbolic NLP, formal grammars and the cognitive science side of linguistics

The modern twist is that large language models are trained only on the first question, predicting the next token, yet they show behaviour that looks like the second. Whether that means they understand is one of the open questions of this course.

The six levels of linguistic structure

This is the backbone of the course. Each level combines the units of the level below it. Sounds combine into words, words into sentences, sentences carry a literal meaning, and context decides what the speaker meant.

LevelUnit studiedQuestion it answersExample
PhoneticsRaw speech soundsHow are sounds produced, carried and heard?[k] is made by stopping air at the back of the mouth with no voice
PhonologyPhonemes, the sound systemWhich sound differences change meaning in this language?/p/ against /b/ turns "pat" into "bat"
MorphologyWords and word partsHow are words built from meaningful parts?dis + taste = distaste
SyntaxPhrases and sentencesHow do words combine into sentences?"The cat jumped" is subject then verb
SemanticsLiteral meaningWhat does the sentence mean on its own?"The cat jumped" names a jumping event done by a cat
PragmaticsMeaning in contextWhat does the speaker intend here and now?"I sat near a bank": river or money?

Phonetics against phonology

The lecture used the two words almost as one, but exams like to separate them. Phonetics is the physics and biology of speech sounds and is the same for every language. Phonology is the mental sound system of one language: which differences matter. English treats the breathy p in "pin" and the plain p in "spin" as one phoneme. Hindi treats प and फ as two different phonemes, so the same physical difference changes meaning in one language and not in the other.

Morphology in more detail

  • A morpheme is the smallest unit that carries meaning or a grammatical job. It is not a syllable: "cats" has two morphemes (cat + s) but one syllable.
  • A free morpheme stands alone (taste, cat, run). A bound morpheme must attach to something (dis-, -s, -ing, -ness).
  • Affixes by position: prefix before the root (dis-taste), suffix after (taste-ful), infix inside (common in Tagalog, rare in English), circumfix on both sides at once (German ge-lob-t, praised).

Stemming and lemmatisation, which you meet in chapter 2.2, are computer approximations of exactly this analysis. The small program above shows both the idea and its weakness: the length rules stop it from cutting "ring" to "r", but a fixed list of affixes still cuts "bless" to "bles", and spelling changes like happy to happi need extra rules.

One idea, four depths

Language is built in layers, like letters into words and words into sentences. Sounds make words, words make sentences, and sentences mean something. On top of that, the situation tells you what the person really wanted to say.

The six levels are phonetics, phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and pragmatics. A useful test: if a change in sound alters meaning, that is phonology. If the problem is word order, it is syntax. If the sentence is well formed but the meaning is odd, it is semantics. If you need the situation, it is pragmatics.

NLP systems map onto these levels. Speech recognition works at the phonetic and phonological levels, tokenisers and stemmers at the morphological level, taggers and parsers at the syntactic level, semantic role labellers and entailment models at the semantic level, and dialogue systems at the pragmatic level. Errors propagate upward, which is why pipeline systems were replaced by joint models.

The levels are not sealed. Prosody (a phonological property) changes pragmatic meaning, morphology and syntax trade off across languages (rich case marking allows free word order, as in Hindi), and neural models learn representations whose layers loosely track these levels, which probing studies try to measure. Whether such models need explicit linguistic structure is an open research question.

Lesson 3 of 5

The symbolic function: words stand for concepts

The lecture splits what language does into two functions. The symbolic function lets words stand for objects, events and ideas, even when those things are not present. The interactive function lets two minds coordinate, which is the next lesson.

Symbols come in many sizes: a bound morpheme like dis-, a whole word like cat, or a full proposition such as "If it rains tomorrow, then I will not go to class", which can itself be agreed with or denied.

The key claimExam favourite
Language does not directly represent the external world. It represents our conceptualised experience of the world.
Say "leopard" in a classroom. No leopard appears, yet everyone understands, because the word calls up a concept that speaker and listener already share.

World, percept, concept, meaning, form

For one word the lecture traces this pipeline. A real cat exists. Your senses register its shape and colour (percept). Your brain matches that to a stored concept of a small four legged pet (concept). The concept links both ways to a meaning your community agreed on (linguistic meaning). Finally that meaning is packed into a form, the sound [kæt] or the letters c, a, t. The brackets mark the International Phonetic Alphabet, where each symbol stands for exactly one sound.

The diagram has a name: the semiotic triangle of Ogden and Richards (1923). Its bottom edge is dashed on purpose. A word is linked to the world only through a concept in a mind. The $ sign means money only because a community agreed on that pairing, a convention, not because its shape looks like money.

Four kinds of reality that grammar can encode

RealityExample from the lectureHow grammar marks it
PresentThe cat is sleeping.Present tense with progressive aspect (is + verb + ing)
PastThe cat was sleeping.Past tense with progressive aspect (was + verb + ing)
ImaginedImagine a cat flying over the moon.Irrealis mood, signalled by "imagine"
AbstractJustice is important.A property turned into a noun with no physical referent

Linguists call this machinery TAM: tense (when), aspect (ongoing or completed) and mood (fact or hypothesis). A word like unicorn has meaning without a real referent because its concept is built from parts that do have referents. Frege called this having a sense without a reference.

A sentence as a logical symbol

Write P = "it rains tomorrow" and Q = "I will not go to class". The sentence has the form P → Q. The promise is broken only when it rains and you go to class anyway.

Common mistake
Listeners usually also infer "if it does not rain, I will go", which the logic does not say. This gap between logical form and everyday meaning is one reason formal logic alone could not solve language understanding.
Lesson 4 of 5

The interactive function: from one mind to another

"Let's meet tomorrow!" packs a time limit, a proposal and a request for agreement into three words. The lecture calls this quick and effective expression, and it is the interactive function at work. For any message you need two roles: a speaker who encodes an idea and a hearer who decodes it.

The communication chainExam favourite
Thought → linguistic representation → signal → linguistic representation → reconstructed thought
The first and last boxes are different. Communication never copies a thought. It only gives the hearer enough to rebuild an approximation.

The professor called this a two body problem. A speaker has to model not only their own idea but also what the listener already knows. The same picture appears in engineering as the Shannon Weaver model (source, transmitter, channel with noise, receiver, destination). Shannon wrote that meaning was irrelevant to his engineering problem. A course on language understanding has to put meaning back in.

The effort against clarity trade off

Speakers save effort with short phrases, listeners need enough detail to understand. Two classic ideas formalise this balance.

Zipf: least effort
f(r) ≈ Crα, α ≈ 1
A few short words such as "the" are used constantly. Thousands of rare words are kept for precision. Chapter 3.1 measures this on real text.
Grice: the cooperative principle
Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner
Say enough but not too much, be truthful, be relevant, be clear. Breaking a maxim on purpose creates an implied meaning.
Real world scenario: a customer support chat

A user types "my order hasn't arrived". A good reply follows the maxims: it gives the tracking status (quantity), does not invent a delivery date (quality), talks about this order and not a sale (relation) and avoids jargon (manner). A reply that repeats "I understand your frustration" three times breaks quantity and relation, and users feel they were not understood even though every sentence is polite.

In the exam
In an essay, link Zipf to the speaker side (minimise effort) and Grice to the shared contract between both sides.
Lesson 5 of 5

Recursion and the Pirahã debate

Lecture 2 opened by finishing a debate from lecture 1. Linguists define recursion precisely: a clause can contain another clause of the same kind, which can contain another, with no fixed limit.

Building a recursive sentence, as in class
  1. Matrix clause only: [John said that Mary had left.]
  2. Embed a clause: [John said that [Mary had left before Tom arrived]].
  3. Embed again: [John said that [Mary had left before [Tom, who was late, arrived]]].
  4. Each bracket is a clause with its own subject and verb, sitting inside a bigger clause. That self similar nesting is recursion.

Why it caused a fight

In 2002, Hauser, Chomsky and Fitch proposed that recursion may be the single core property that makes human language unique. The linguist Daniel Everett, who lived with the Pirahã people of the Brazilian Amazon, claimed in 2005 that their language has no embedded clauses, no exact number words and no fixed colour terms. He explained this by an immediacy of experience principle: speakers talk about what they or someone they know witnessed directly. If one human language truly lacks recursion, recursion cannot be a universal that every language must have. Nevins, Pesetsky and Rodrigues (2009) re examined Everett's own data and argued it does not show that. The question is still open.

Why an NLP course cares

  • Most NLP systems are built on high resource languages such as English. Assumptions that look universal may only be English habits.
  • A language with few speakers has little digital text, so word counts are tiny and a statistical model has almost nothing to learn from. This is the low resource problem.
  • Computers store words as numbers: build a vocabulary, give each word an index, then count. A classmate described exactly this, which is the subject of chapter 2.
Going deeper: recursion in formal grammar

A context free rule such as S → NP VP with VP → V S lets a sentence appear inside a verb phrase, so the grammar generates sentences of unbounded depth. Finite state machines (chapter 5.2) cannot handle unbounded centre embedding like "the rat the cat the dog chased bit died", which is one classic argument that natural language is not a regular language. People struggle with more than two levels of centre embedding, so the formal capacity and real use are different things.
