Lesson 1 of 4

From surprise to perplexity

Chapter 3 measured how surprising a single event is. A sentence is a chain of events, so its surprise is the sum of the surprises of its words. The lecture built perplexity from that idea in three small steps.

Pointwise conditional entropy
−log₂ m(wi | hi)
The surprise of one word given its history.
Total surprise
Htotal = −∑i log₂ m(wi | hi) = −log₂ m(w₁ … wN)
Logs turn the chain rule product into a sum.
PerplexityExam favourite
PP = 2Ĥ,   Ĥ = HtotalN,   so   PP = m(w₁ … wN)−1/N
Lower perplexity means the model found the text less surprising.
The board derivation: why 2 to the average entropy is the N-th root
  1. Start from PP = 2Ĥ with Ĥ = Htotal ÷ N.
  2. Substitute Htotal = −log₂ m(w₁ … wN): Ĥ = −(1/N) log₂ m.
  3. Move the factor inside the log: a log x = log xa, so Ĥ = log₂ (m−1/N).
  4. PP = 2log₂ (m−1/N) = m−1/N = ᴺ√(1 ÷ m)
Worked example: He put the coffee on
  1. Model probabilities: P(He) = 0.2, P(put | He) = 0.1, P(the | put) = 0.25, P(coffee | the) = 0.05, P(on | coffee) = 0.4.
  2. Product: 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.25 × 0.05 × 0.4 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴.
  3. Surprises in bits: 2.322, 3.322, 2.000, 4.322, 1.322. Total 13.288 bits.
  4. Average Ĥ = 13.288 ÷ 5 = 2.658 bits per word.
  5. PP = 22.658 = 6.31, and the root form (10⁻⁴)−1/5 = 100.8 = 6.31 agrees

Perplexity is a branching factor

If a model spreads its probability evenly over k words at every step, each factor is 1/k, the product is (1/k)N, and PP = ((1/k)N)−1/N = k. So a perplexity of 109 means the model is as uncertain as if it had to pick fairly among 109 words at each position. For a real model it is an effective number of choices, not a literal count.

Remember
Base 2 gives bits, base e gives nats, base 10 gives hartleys. Perplexity does not depend on the base as long as you raise the same base you took the log with: 2Ĥ in bits = 10Ĥ in hartleys. That is why SRILM can print log₁₀ values and still match the textbook.
Going deeper: Are perplexity and entropy the same thing?

They move together but answer different questions. Entropy is in bits and adds: one extra bit means twice the choices. Perplexity counts the choices and multiplies. Going from 7 bits to 6 bits halves perplexity from 128 to 64. The professor called perplexity "almost proportional" to entropy. Strictly it is exponential in entropy. Ranking models by either gives the same order.

Lesson 2 of 4

PP, PP1 and whole files

SRILM prints two perplexities for every sentence and for the whole file. Both use the same log probability. They differ only in what they divide by.

pplExam favourite
ppl = 10−logprob ÷ (words − OOVs − zeroprobs + sentences)
Counts one </s> per sentence: how hard is it to predict every token, including where the sentence ends?
ppl1Exam favourite
ppl1 = 10−logprob ÷ (words − OOVs − zeroprobs)
Words only. The cost of predicting the end is charged to the words, so ppl1 ≥ ppl always.
The file line of the SRILM output
  1. Sentence totals from the activity: log₁₀ P(S1) = −2.01773 with 5 words, log₁₀ P(S2) = −1.14267 with 3 words.
  2. File logprob = −2.01773 + (−1.14267) = −3.16040.
  3. Tokens: 8 words + 2 end tags = 10 for ppl, 8 for ppl1.
  4. ppl = 103.16040/10 = 2.07033,   ppl1 = 103.16040/8 = 2.48342
  5. This is not the average of 2.169 and 1.930. Perplexity is a geometric mean over tokens, so it is computed from the totals.
In the exam
For long documents PP and PP1 converge, since there is one end tag per twenty or more words. For three word toy sentences they differ a lot (1.93 against 2.40). In an exam, say which N you used and never compare PP from one model with PP1 from another.

OOVs and zero probabilities in the toolkit

In the demo SRILM did not print −∞ for the sentence with "linguistics". The professor put it this way: the model "kicked the culprit out". It reports the word as 1 OOV and computes logprob and perplexity over the rest. A known word with probability 0 is reported as a zeroprob and skipped in the same way. So a perplexity with OOVs is not comparable with one without. Always read the OOV and zeroprob counts on the line.

Lesson 3 of 4

Evaluating language models

How do we know one language model is better than another? There are two ways, and both appear in the slides.

ExtrinsicIntrinsic
WhatPut the model inside an application and measure the applicationMeasure the model alone on held out text
ExamplesThe London word order task, translation, speech recognition, spelling correctionLikelihood, log likelihood, perplexity on a test set
StrengthMeasures what we really care aboutCheap, fast and repeatable
WeaknessSlow and expensiveA lower perplexity usually, but not always, gives a better application
The three intrinsic measuresExam favourite
likelihood m(test),   log likelihood log m(test),   PP = m(test)−1/N
Higher likelihood, higher log likelihood and lower perplexity all mean the same thing, on the same test set and vocabulary.

Why logs: underflow

A double precision number cannot hold values below about 10⁻³⁰⁸. A test set of 1,000 words with average probability 0.001 has P = 10⁻³⁰⁰⁰, which becomes exactly 0. Its log₁₀ is simply −3000, no problem at all. Toolkits add log probabilities for this reason.

The WSJ table

ModelPerplexityBits per word
Unigram9629.91
Bigram1707.41
Trigram1096.77

Trained on 38 million words of the Wall Street Journal and tested on 1.5 million (Jurafsky and Martin). Each extra word of context cuts the effective number of choices. The gains shrink, and on finite data they eventually reverse: a 6-gram has so many unseen histories that, without very good smoothing, test perplexity rises again.

Three rules from class

  • Never train on the test set. An MLE model trained on the test sentence gives it the highest probability any model of its kind can, so its perplexity says nothing about new text. Tuning λ or k on the test set is the same cheating. Use a dev set.
  • Compare perplexities only with identical vocabularies. The professor flagged this as a likely exam question. A model that maps most words to <UNK> finds the text easy because the task changed.
  • Report everything. Log probability, PP and PP1, the number of words, sentences and OOVs.
The vocabulary trick in numbers
  1. A lazy model keeps only Sam and </s>. I, am, do and like all become <UNK> with count 5 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 12.
  2. S1 becomes Sam <UNK> <UNK> <UNK> <UNK> </s>, so P = (5/22)(12/22)⁴(5/22) = 0.00457.
  3. PP = 0.00457−1/6 = 2.45, lower than the honest unigram's 5.58
  4. Yet the lazy model cannot tell I from like. The lower number comes from the easier task, not a better model.
Real world scenario: a spelling app update

An app team reports that the new keyboard model has perplexity 40 against the old one's 65. Before celebrating, the reviewer asks two questions. Were both scored on the same held out messages? Do both use the same vocabulary and tokeniser? If the new model splits words into smaller pieces, its perplexity is per piece, and the two numbers measure different things.

Lesson 4 of 4

Perplexity as cross entropy

What a model measures on test data is not the true entropy of the language but a cross entropy: the average surprise of real text under the model's probabilities.

Test set cross entropyExam favourite
Ĥ = −1N log₂ m(w₁ … wN) ≈ H(p, m) = H(p) + D(p ‖ m)
For long text this tends to the cross entropy of the truth p with the model m.
Problem F8: perplexity can never beat the truth
  1. True next word distribution p = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25). The model q is uniform, 1/3 each.
  2. H(p) = 0.5 × 1 + 2 × 0.25 × 2 = 1.5 bits, so the best possible perplexity is 21.5 = 2.828.
  3. H(p, q) = −Σ p log₂ (1/3) = log₂ 3 = 1.585 bits, so the model's perplexity is 3.
  4. KL = 1.585 − 1.5 = 0.085 bits ≥ 0, by Gibbs' inequality
  5. Test perplexity estimates 2H(p,q) ≥ 2H(p). The gap is the KL divergence from chapter 3.3.

How do people predict each other?

Students answered "context, shared interest, past conversations". Underneath that, the professor said, we speak the same language, and language is built from predictable units: short frequent words, "the" followed by a noun. As context grows, the number of plausible next words falls for both speaker and listener. Psycholinguists find that reading time grows roughly in line with surprisal (Hale 2001, Levy 2008, Smith and Levy 2013), which suggests the listener runs something like a language model.

Remember
Adding another source of information, such as tone of voice or a facial expression, can only lower conditional entropy on average: H(Y | X, Z) ≤ H(Y | X). Sarcasm is the classic case where the text misleads and the voice resolves it.
One idea, four depths

Perplexity says how many words the model is effectively choosing between at each step. Smaller is better.

PP = 2Ĥ = P−1/N. ppl counts </s>, ppl1 does not. Compare only on the same test set and vocabulary, after tuning on a dev set.

Test perplexity is the exponential of cross entropy, which equals entropy plus KL, so it bounds the true entropy from above. Minimising it is maximum likelihood. Per token perplexities of models with different tokenisers must be converted to bits per character before comparing.

Perplexity correlates imperfectly with downstream quality, especially for large models. Research studies calibration, bits per byte across tokenisers, test set contamination in web scale training data, and surprisal as a predictor of human reading times.
