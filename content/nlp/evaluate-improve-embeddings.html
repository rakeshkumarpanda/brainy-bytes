Intrinsic evaluation: test the vectors on their own

After training, how do we know the embedding is good? NLP has two kinds of evaluation. Intrinsic evaluation checks the vectors on a small subtask. Extrinsic evaluation plugs them into a real system and measures that system.

IntrinsicExtrinsic
Evaluated ona specific intermediate subtaska real task such as NER or translation
Speedfast to computecan take a long time
Strengthhelps to understand the embeddingtells you if it really helps
Weaknessnot clear it helps unless it correlates with a real taskunclear whether the embedding or another part is the problem

Cluster purity
purity = 1N ∑clusters maxclass |cluster ∩ class|
Each cluster votes for its majority class.
Purity by hand
  1. k-means gives cluster 1 = {dog, cat, apple} and cluster 2 = {orange, banana, horse}.
  2. Cluster 1 majority class: animal, 2 words. Cluster 2 majority class: fruit, 2 words.
  3. purity = (2 + 2) ÷ 6 = 0.667
  4. A perfect embedding would give purity 1.
In the exam
A Spearman question gives two lists of scores. Rank both lists first, then use the formula. Pearson uses the raw scores instead.
Lesson 2 of 5

Extrinsic evaluation: use the vectors in a real system

Extrinsic evaluation asks whether embeddings help a downstream task: sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, machine translation or question answering. It reports the end metric, such as accuracy, F1 or BLEU. If replacing exactly one subsystem with another raises the score, the new embedding wins.

Way to use pre-trained vectorsProsCons
Fixed (frozen)fewer parameters, less overfittingcannot adapt to task specific meaning
Fine-tunedadapts to the taskmay forget general knowledge
Concatenated [pre-trained, learned]general and task specific information togetherbigger model, more computation
Concatenation
xw = [epre(w), etask(w)] ∈ ℝd₁ + d₂
A 300-dimensional GloVe vector plus a learned 100-dimensional vector gives 400 inputs.
Trainable embedding parameters
frozen: 0,   fine-tuned: V × d
With V = 10,000 and d = 300, fine-tuning trains 3 million extra numbers.
Count the parameters of a concatenated input
  1. Vocabulary V = 10,000. Pre-trained part 300 dimensions, frozen. Learned part 100 dimensions.
  2. Trainable numbers in the learned table: 10,000 × 100 = 1,000,000.
  3. Frozen numbers: 10,000 × 300 = 3,000,000, which do not change.
  4. Input size per word = 300 + 100 = 400, trainable embedding parameters = 1,000,000
Real world scenario: choosing vectors for a hospital chatbot

A team builds a chatbot for patient questions with only 5,000 labelled messages. Training embeddings from scratch on so little text gives poor vectors, so they start from pre-trained vectors. Freezing them protects against overfitting the small set, and a small learned part captures hospital words such as "OPD" and "discharge summary". They compare each choice by the F1 score of the final intent classifier, which is an extrinsic evaluation.

The lecture's conclusion from Schnabel and others: no embedding wins on every metric. SGNS wins some columns, GloVe others. Be aware of the trade offs and pick the best one for your task.

Remember
Pre-trained vectors help most when the labelled data is small. With a huge labelled set, vectors trained on the task can catch up.

Subword embeddings and fastText

Word2vec and GloVe treat each word form as a separate symbol. "dog" and "dogs" get two unrelated vectors, and they do not know that -s marks a plural. Rare words get poor vectors, and a word never seen in training (out of vocabulary, OOV) gets none at all.

Three fixes from the slides break a word into smaller pieces, give each piece a vector, and combine them.

PiecesExampleCombined with
charactersc, a, t, sa BiLSTM or CNN over the characters
morphemesun + fortunate + lyan RNN or a sum
character n-grams (fastText)<wh, whe, her, ere, re>, ...a sum or average
fastText word vectorExam favourite
vw = zw + ∑g ∈ G(w) zg
G(w) is the set of character n-grams of w (usually n = 3 to 6) with boundary marks < and >.
Number of n-grams
∑n = lohi (L + 2 − n + 1)
L is the word length. The marks add 2 characters.
Training
skip-gram score s(w, c) = ∑g ∈ G(w) zg·uc
Same skip-gram or CBOW as word2vec, but the update goes to every n-gram vector of the word.
Count the n-grams of "where" for n = 3 to 6
  1. With marks the string is <where>, 7 characters.
  2. n = 3: 7 − 3 + 1 = 5. n = 4: 4. n = 5: 3. n = 6: 2.
  3. 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 14 n-grams, plus the whole word
  4. The trigram <her> of the word "her" differs from her inside "where", thanks to the marks.
Common mistake
The slide lists the trigrams of "playing" as <pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>: 7 trigrams. The last one is ng>, the suffix shared by every -ing word.
Going deeper: hashing the n-grams

There are far too many distinct n-grams to store, so fastText hashes each one into a fixed number of buckets, 2 million by default. Two n-grams can share a bucket. This bounds memory and still gives any new word, even a typo, a reasonable vector from its pieces.

Lesson 4 of 5

Knowledge, languages and alignment

Embeddings capture statistics of text, not facts. "whale" sits near "fish" because both appear near "ocean", not because whales are fish. Embeddings trained separately for English and Spanish are not aligned either: "dog" and "perro" may end up far apart, because each training run picks its own orientation.

The fix is a mapping from one space to the other. Faruqui and Dyer (2014) used a bilingual dictionary. The surprising later result is that no dictionary is needed, because co-occurrence patterns are similar in every language, so the two point clouds have about the same shape.

MethodAnchorHow the mapping is found
Faruqui and Dyer 2014a bilingual dictionaryproject both spaces into a shared space (CCA)
Artetxe et al. 2017identical strings: numbers, names, "COVID", "2025", "UN"learn a linear map W from the anchors, then refine with mutual nearest neighbours
Zhang et al. 2017noneadversarial training: a generator W against a discriminator that guesses the language
Linear mappingExam favourite
minW ∑i ‖W xi − yi‖²
xi is a source word, yi its translation.
Orthogonal Procrustes
W = U VT where U Σ VT = SVD(Y XT)
Forcing WTW = I (a rotation) keeps distances, and this closed form solves it.
Map one word with a known rotation
  1. Suppose the Spanish space is the English space turned by 90 degrees: W = 0−110.
  2. English "cat" is x = (0.8, 0.6).
  3. W x = (0 × 0.8 − 1 × 0.6, 1 × 0.8 + 0 × 0.6) = (−0.6, 0.8)
  4. The nearest Spanish vector to (−0.6, 0.8) should be "gato". Length stays 1, so the rotation kept distances.
Remember
The adversarial method stops when the discriminator cannot tell a mapped English vector from a real Spanish one. At that point the two spaces are statistically indistinguishable.

Interpretable and fairer embeddings

Dense vectors spread information over every dimension, so no axis has a clear meaning. We can say king − man + woman ≈ queen, but we cannot point at a "gender" or "royalty" axis. Sparse embeddings add a penalty that pushes most values to 0, so each active dimension tends to mean one feature, such as "is animal" or "is a place".

Sparsity penaltyExam favourite
Jsparse = J + λ ∑i ‖wi‖₁
‖w‖₁ = ∑ |wk|. A bigger λ gives more zeros.

Embeddings also learn the biases in the text they read. In early word2vec vectors trained on Google News, the analogy man : computer programmer :: woman : ? returned homemaker. Bolukbasi and others (2016) reduce this in three steps.

StepWhat it does
1. Identifypick definitional pairs such as he and she, man and woman
2. Directioncompute the bias direction g from their differences (the top principal component)
3. Neutraliseremove the g part of words that should be neutral, such as programmer or nurse
(Equalise)make each pair equally far from every neutral word
NeutraliseExam favourite
v′ = v − (v·g) g
g is a unit vector. After this v′·g = 0.
Neutralise a word with a tilted direction
  1. g = (0.6, 0.8), a unit vector. Word vector v = (2, 1).
  2. v·g = 2 × 0.6 + 1 × 0.8 = 2.0.
  3. v′ = (2 − 2.0 × 0.6, 1 − 2.0 × 0.8) = (0.8, −0.6)
  4. Check: v′·g = 0.48 − 0.48 = 0, so the bias part is gone.
One idea, four depths

Computers learn word meanings from books and the web. If the text often says "he is an engineer" and "she is a nurse", the computer learns that too. We can find the he to she direction and remove it from job words.

Evaluate embeddings intrinsically (similarity, analogy, clusters) and extrinsically (task scores). Improve them with subwords, cross-lingual mapping, sparsity and debiasing by projection.

Each fix is linear algebra: n-gram sums, an orthogonal map from Procrustes, an L1 penalty, and a projection onto the complement of g. The analogy test itself assumes relations are parallel offsets, which holds only roughly.

Gonen and Goldberg (2019) showed that projection debiasing hides bias rather than removing it: neutralised words still cluster by their old gender association. Measuring bias with tests such as WEAT and debiasing contextual models remain open problems.

Common mistake
Perfect debiasing remains hard. The slide says embeddings become more balanced, not unbiased. A balanced or filtered corpus is the other approach.
