Lesson 1 of 4

Do words have an average frequency?

The lecture opened with two ways to describe a language. A grammar says what is allowed: English puts the verb before the object, Hindi after it. Statistics say what is likely: after "apples and" you expect "oranges" more than "bananas". This step is about the second view, and it starts with a plain question. In a page of text, do most words occur about an average number of times?

Many things in life follow the bell shaped normal curve: heights of children in a class, marks of 100 students, the sum of three dice. Most values sit near the middle and extremes are rare. For three dice there are 6 × 6 × 6 = 216 outcomes. Only one gives a sum of 3, while 27 give a sum of 10, so the middle wins.

Three dice
P(sum = 3) = 1216 ≈ 0.005,   P(sum = 10) = 27216 = 0.125
Counts for sums 3 to 18: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 25, 27, 27, 25, 21, 15, 10, 6, 3, 1.
Words
c(the) = 69,971,   types seen once = 16,540
Brown corpus, lower cased. One word fills about 7% of the text while thousands of words appear once. There is no typical value.

Word counts look nothing like a bell. A few words are used constantly and a huge number are rare. The notebook already showed this: "the" alone was 5.40% of the 1,161,192 Brown tokens counted case sensitively (69,971 once The and the are merged).

Remember
Why no bell curve? A normal curve appears when a value is the sum of many small independent effects. Word frequency is not a sum. It comes from people choosing words, and a few grammatical words are chosen in almost every sentence.
Lesson 2 of 4

Zipf's law

In 1912 the French stenographer Jean-Baptiste Estoup noticed a regularity in word counts. In 1932 the Harvard linguist George Kingsley Zipf made it famous, with students counting the words of books by hand. The method is the one used for ranking students by marks: count every word, sort from most to least frequent, give the top word rank 1, the next rank 2, and plot frequency against rank.

Zipf's lawExam favourite
f = kr   or   f × r ≈ k
f is the frequency of the word of rank r and k is a constant for the corpus. The frequency falls as one over the rank.
Ratio ruleExam favourite
fafb = ba
The top word is about twice the second, three times the third. The 50th word is three times the 150th.

Why a straight line on log axes

Proof: a power law is a straight line on a log log plot
  1. Start from the general power law f = k · r−a. Zipf is the case a = 1.
  2. Take logarithms of both sides: log f = log k + log r−a.
  3. The power comes down: log f = log k − a · log r.
  4. Write Y = log f and X = log r. Then Y = log k − a X, a straight line with slope −a.
  5. Zipf predicts slope −1. Brown gives about −1.08 for ranks 10 to 10,000.
In the exam
The class question "should the 50th word occur three times as often as the 150th?" has answer yes: f₅₀ ÷ f₁₅₀ = 150 ÷ 50 = 3. On Brown the real ratio is 2,139 ÷ 610 = 3.51, a little steeper, as the slope of −1.08 suggests.
Lesson 3 of 4

How much text do the top words cover?

If Zipf's law held exactly, we could work out how much of all text the most common words make up. The answer uses the harmonic numbers.

Share of the top n wordsExam favourite
share(n) = HnHV,   Hn = 1 + 12 + 13 + … + 1n
V is the vocabulary size. Total tokens are k HV and the top n words have k Hn of them.
Approximation
Hn ≈ ln n + γ,   γ ≈ 0.5772
γ is the Euler Mascheroni constant. The sum of 1/r grows like the area under 1/x.
Coverage for a 50,000 word vocabulary
  1. H50000 ≈ ln 50,000 + 0.5772 = 10.820 + 0.577 = 11.397
  2. Top 1 word: 1 ÷ 11.397 = 8.8% (Brown's "the" is 7.1%).
  3. Top 10: H10 = 2.929, so 2.929 ÷ 11.397 = 25.7% (Brown: 24.8%).
  4. Top 100: H100 = 5.187, so 45.5% (Brown: 48.5%).
  5. Top 1,000: H1000 = 7.485, so 65.7% (Brown: 70.1%).
  6. About 100 words make up half of everything we write.

Why language is shaped like this

The professor's answer is the principle of least effort. Words we use often should be quick to say, so frequent words are short. But short words are easy to mishear and carry little information, while long words are robust and informative. Language balances the speaker's effort against the listener's need to recover the message despite noise. In Brown the ten most frequent words average 2.4 letters, while words ranked beyond 10,000 average 8.1. This is Zipf's law of abbreviation.

Going deeper: Mandelbrot and Heaps

Benoit Mandelbrot (1953) proposed f = k ÷ (r + b)a. The shift b flattens the top few ranks and a near 1 controls the tail. With k = 100,000, a = 1 and b = 2.7, rank 1 gets about 27,000 instead of 100,000. Heaps' law is the companion law for vocabulary growth: V ≈ K Nβ with β between about 0.4 and 0.6. The vocabulary never stops growing, which is why every language model meets unseen words and needs smoothing.

Real world scenario: sizing the vocabulary of a phone keyboard

A keyboard app for Indian English must fit its word list on the phone. Zipf's law says a few thousand words cover most of what people type (on Brown the top 2,000 cover 77.5% and the top 3,000 cover 81.9%). The long tail of names and rare words is handled with subword pieces instead of storing every word ever seen.

Lesson 4 of 4

The Zipf mystery: monkey typing

The professor set an experiment after a documentary on the "Zipf mystery". Give a monkey a keyboard with 26 letters and a space bar, each pressed with probability 1/27. A word is whatever falls between two spaces. Type a million characters, count the words, rank them and plot. Will this meaningless text follow Zipf's law?

Students who ran it found that it broadly does. George Miller pointed this out in 1957 and Wentian Li made it precise in 1992. The reason is counting, not meaning.

Proof: random typing gives a power law
  1. Each letter has probability (1 − s) ÷ M and the space has probability s. A word of length L is L letters then a space, so P(word) = ((1 − s)/M)L · s.
  2. Every word of length L has the same probability, and each extra letter divides it by M/(1 − s).
  3. There are ML words of length L. They sit at ranks of about c · ML, so L ≈ ln(r/c) ÷ ln M.
  4. Put this L into P(word): f ∝ ((1 − s)/M)ln r / ln M = rln((1 − s)/M) / ln M.
  5. f ∝ r−α,   α = 1 − ln(1 − s) ÷ ln M
  6. For 27 equally likely keys: α = ln 27 ÷ ln 26 = 1.0116, practically Zipf's exponent of 1.
Random typing exponentExam favourite
α = 1 − ln(1 − s)ln M
M letters, space probability s. As M grows α tends to 1 for any fixed s.

What the experiment shows, and what it does not

  • A Zipf like curve can come from pure chance plus the convention that spaces mark word boundaries. Zipf's law alone does not prove language is special.
  • The monkey's curve is a staircase, because all words of the same length tie. Real text gives a smooth curve.
  • Most importantly, the monkey's next letter cannot be predicted from the previous ones. Human language is highly predictable from context, which is what entropy measures in the next chapter.

The bigram homework

A student asked whether pairs of words also follow Zipf. On Brown the top bigrams are "of the" (9,771), "in the" (6,155), "to the" (3,525), "on the" (2,491) and "and the" (2,297), all function word pairs. Their rank frequency curve is also close to a straight line on log axes, but flatter, with slope about −0.76, because counts are spread over many more possible pairs.

One idea, four depths

Count every word in a book and sort them. The top word appears about twice as often as the second and three times as often as the third. A few words fill most of the pages.

Zipf's law f = k/r is a power law, a straight line of slope −1 on log log axes. The top n words cover Hn/HV of the text. Least effort explains why frequent words are short.

Zipf Mandelbrot f = k/(r + b)a fits the head better. Random typing gives α = 1 − ln(1 − s)/ln M, so the exponent alone cannot separate language from noise. Heaps' law V ≈ KNβ drives the need for smoothing and subword vocabularies.

Research asks which mechanism produces Zipf in real language: least effort optimisation (Ferrer i Cancho and Solé, 2003), preferential attachment, or sampling artefacts. Piantadosi (2014) reviews the evidence across languages, where exponents vary between about 0.8 and 1.2, and argues that the law constrains but does not explain the lexicon.
