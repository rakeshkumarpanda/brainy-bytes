Lesson 1 of 5

Language models and n-grams

A language model predicts what word comes next. Given "the students opened their", it gives a probability to every word of the vocabulary: books, laptops, exams, minds. Equivalently, it assigns a probability to a whole piece of text. You use one every day in phone keyboards and search suggestions, and ChatGPT is a language model too.

Next word distributionExam favourite
P(x(t+1) | x(t), ..., x(1))
x(t+1) can be any word of the vocabulary V.
Probability of a text (chain rule)
P(x(1), ..., x(T)) = ∏t=1T P(x(t) | x(t−1), ..., x(1))
Exact. Every model below approximates the conditional terms.
n-gram (Markov) assumptionExam favourite
P(x(t+1) | x(t), ..., x(1)) ≈ P(x(t+1) | x(t), ..., x(t−n+2)) ≈ count(x(t−n+2) ... x(t+1))count(x(t−n+2) ... x(t))
Only the last n − 1 words matter. Probabilities come from counting.
The lecture 4-gram example
  1. Text: "as the proctor started the clock, the students opened their ___". A 4-gram model keeps only the last 3 words, "students opened their", and discards the rest.
  2. "students opened their" occurred 1000 times. "students opened their books" occurred 400 times.
  3. P(books | students opened their) = 400 ÷ 1000 = 0.4
  4. "students opened their exams" occurred 100 times, so P(exams | ...) = 0.1. The discarded word "proctor" was a strong hint for exams.
ProblemWhat goes wrongPartial fix
Sparsity 1the full n-gram never occurred, so a valid word gets probability 0smoothing: add a small δ to every count
Sparsity 2the context never occurred, so nothing can be computedbackoff: condition on fewer words, "opened their"
Storageevery seen n-gram must be storednone: bigger n or corpus means a bigger model
Short contextn above 5 makes sparsity much worsemove to neural models

Sampling from a trigram model trained on news gives text such as "today the price of gold per ton , while production of shoe lasts and shoe industry , the bank intervened ...". It is surprisingly grammatical but incoherent, because three words of context are not enough.

Add-one (Laplace) smoothing
P(w | c) = count(c, w) + 1count(c) + |V|
No probability is ever 0. With δ instead of 1 it is add-δ smoothing.
In the exam
Questions often ask for an n-gram probability from counts and then what happens when a count is 0. Say "sparsity", name smoothing and backoff, and give the Markov assumption.
Lesson 2 of 5

A fixed window neural language model

Bengio and others (2003) replaced counting with a neural network. Take the last few words, look up their embeddings, concatenate them, pass them through a hidden layer and finish with a softmax over the vocabulary. The prefix is turned into one vector by a composition function. Choices include a sum of embeddings, concatenation, a feed-forward network, a CNN, an RNN or a transformer.

Fixed window modelExam favourite
e = [e(1), e(2), e(3), e(4)],   h = f(We + b₁),   ŷ = softmax(Uh + b₂)
e(i) is the embedding of the i-th window word. ŷ is a distribution over V.
Output matrix shape
U ∈ ℝ|V| × dh
One row per vocabulary word. Row w scores how well the prefix fits word w.
Improvement over n-gramsRemaining problem
no sparsity problem: every word gets a probabilitythe fixed window is too small
no need to store all observed n-gramsenlarging the window enlarges W
similar words share similar embeddingsx(1) and x(2) are multiplied by different parts of W, so there is no symmetry
Why "no symmetry" matters
  1. "the students opened their" and "yesterday students opened their" mean nearly the same.
  2. In the first, "students" is in slot 2 and meets the weights W₂. In the second it is in slot 3 and meets W₃.
  3. The model must learn "students in slot 2", "students in slot 3" and "students in slot 4" as separate patterns.
  4. No parameter sharing, so learning does not transfer across positions and more data is needed
Count the parameters of a fixed window model
  1. V = 10,000, embeddings d = 100, window 4, hidden size 500, biases ignored.
  2. Embeddings E: 10,000 × 100 = 1,000,000. W: 500 × (4 × 100) = 200,000.
  3. U: 10,000 × 500 = 5,000,000.
  4. Total = 6,200,000, most of it in the output layer
Real world scenario: predictive text on a phone

Early phone keyboards used n-gram counts and suggested only words they had seen after the last two words. A neural model can suggest "laptops" after "opened their" even if that exact phrase was rare, because "laptops" has an embedding close to "books".

Lesson 3 of 5

Recurrent neural networks

A recurrent neural network reads one word at a time and keeps a hidden state, a running summary of everything so far. The core idea is to apply the same weights at every step. That handles any input length, and the model size does not grow with the context.

RNN hidden stateExam favourite
h(t) = σ(Wh h(t−1) + We e(t) + b₁)
h(0) is the initial state. σ is usually tanh. Wh and We are shared across time.
RNN outputExam favourite
ŷ(t) = softmax(U h(t) + b₂) ∈ ℝ|V|
The distribution over the next word after step t.
Parameters (no biases, one-hot input)
|V| dh + dh² + dh |V|
Input to hidden, hidden to hidden, hidden to output.
RNN advantagesRNN disadvantages
any input lengthrecurrent computation is slow, steps cannot run in parallel
step t can in theory use information from many steps backin practice it is hard to use information from many steps back
model size does not grow with the context
the same weights at every step, so inputs are treated symmetrically
The lecture exercise: 96 parameters
  1. Vocabulary {I, he, go, walk, eat}, one-hot input of size 5, hidden size 6, no biases.
  2. Input to hidden Wxh: 5 × 6 = 30. Hidden to hidden Whh: 6 × 6 = 36.
  3. Hidden to output Why: 6 × 5 = 30.
  4. 30 + 36 + 30 = 96
Common mistake
The RNN parameter count never depends on the sentence length T. A common wrong answer multiplies by T.
Lesson 4 of 5

Training: cross-entropy and teacher forcing

Train an RNN language model on a big corpus. At every step t the model outputs a distribution ŷ(t), and the loss is the cross-entropy with the true next word x(t+1). Because the target is one-hot, the cross-entropy is just minus the log of the probability given to the correct word.

Loss at step tExam favourite
J(t)(θ) = −∑w ∈ V yw(t) log ŷw(t) = −log ŷ(t)x(t+1)
Only the true word counts.
Overall lossExam favourite
J(θ) = 1T ∑t=1T J(t)(θ)
An average over all steps, in practice over a batch of sentences.
Backpropagation through time
∂J(t)∂Wh = ∑i=1t ∂J(t)∂Wh|(i)
Wh is used at every step, so its gradient is the sum of the gradients at each use.
The two slide examples
  1. Gold [0, 1, 0, 0], predicted [0.1, 0.6, 0.2, 0.1]: the true word got 0.6.
  2. J = −ln 0.6 = 0.51
  3. Predicted [0.4, 0.3, 0.05, 0.25] and the true word got 0.05 (it must be the third word for the slide value).
  4. J = −ln 0.05 = 2.99, a strong penalty for being confidently wrong
  5. If the gold were still the second word, the loss would be −ln 0.3 = 1.20. Always read which word is gold.

Teacher forcing: during training the input at step t + 1 is the true word from the corpus, not the word the model predicted. The losses for "students", "opened", "their" and "exams" are added and averaged. Computing over the whole corpus at once is too expensive, so we use stochastic gradient descent on a sentence or a batch of sentences.

Remember
Why cross-entropy: logs turn products of probabilities into sums, confident wrong answers are penalised hard, and the gradient with softmax is simply ŷ − y, which is stable.
Going deeper: exposure bias

Teacher forcing means the model never sees its own mistakes during training, but at generation time it must continue from its own outputs. This mismatch is called exposure bias. Scheduled sampling (Bengio et al. 2015) gradually replaces gold inputs with model samples during training.

Lesson 5 of 5

Perplexity and generating text

The standard metric for language models is perplexity: the inverse probability of the corpus, normalised by the number of words. It equals the exponential of the cross-entropy loss. Lower is better.

PerplexityExam favourite
PP = ∏t=1T (1PLM(x(t+1) | x(t), ..., x(1)))1/T = exp(J(θ))
J is the average cross-entropy in natural logs.
Meaning
PP = k ⇔ the model is as unsure as a uniform choice among k words
A perfect model has PP = 1. Guessing uniformly over V gives PP = |V|.
PerplexityInterpretation from the slide
1.44on average the model chooses between about 1.44 words: confident and mostly right
4.07the model is confused, almost guessing among 4 words

Generation is autoregressive: feed a start token, sample a word from ŷ(1), feed that word back as the next input, and repeat. RNNs serve three uses from the slides: text generation, sequence labelling (one output per word) and text classification (one output per sentence).

One idea, four depths

A language model guesses the next word. Old models counted short word groups. An RNN reads words one by one and keeps a memory, so it can use more of the sentence.

An RNN applies the same weights at every step: h(t) = tanh(Whh(t−1) + Wee(t)). It is trained with the average cross-entropy using teacher forcing, and measured with perplexity = exp(J).

The gradient through time is a product of Jacobians WhTdiag(tanh′). When their norms are below 1 it vanishes, above 1 it explodes. That limits how far back an RNN can learn, which motivates LSTM and GRU in chapter 3.3.

Perplexity on benchmarks such as Penn Treebank fell from about 140 for n-gram models to about 80 with LSTMs and below 20 for large transformers on bigger corpora. Perplexity is only comparable between models with the same vocabulary and tokenisation.
