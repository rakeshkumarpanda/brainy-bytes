Lesson 1 of 4

What part of speech tagging is

Part of speech (POS) tagging gives every word in a sentence its grammatical category: The/DT student/NN reads/VBZ books/NNS. It is usually the first step after tokenisation, and later steps such as chunking, parsing, information extraction and translation lean on it.

How does a person decide? The class found two cues. The first is the word itself: "book" on its own is a noun. The second is the context: in "Please book a ticket", booking is happening, so book is a verb. The professor joked that the class was being unfair to the word by changing its tag, but that is exactly what language does.

A headline with two tag sequences

InputBritishleftwafflesonFalklandIslands
Reading 1: the political Left is indecisiveNNVPNN
Reading 2: British people left some wafflesNVNPNN

Both sequences are grammatical. Headlines drop the small words ("The British left is waffling") that would normally settle it. The professor: "Such a simple task. POS tagging is indeed very difficult. I pity the poor machines."

The problem statementExam favourite
given words W = w₁ … wn,   find tags T̂ = argmaxT P(T | W)
Supervised: train on sentences whose correct tags were added by linguists (a gold standard), then tag new sentences whose tags are hidden. It is classification into one of q tags, done for a whole sequence so the choices interact.
Remember
Not every ambiguity is a tagging problem. In "The fisherman sat near the bank", bank is a noun in both readings, river bank and money bank. That is word sense ambiguity, a different task.

How many tags are there?

Asked in class, students said eight or nine. The professor's answer: it depends on the tagset. Finer distinctions give more tags.

TagsetTagsExamples
Traditional grammarabout 8noun, pronoun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, conjunction, interjection
Universal (Petrov and colleagues, 2012)12NOUN, VERB, ADJ, ADV, PRON, DET, ADP, NUM, CONJ, PRT, ".", X
Universal Dependencies UPOS17adds PROPN, AUX, SCONJ, SYM, INTJ and others
Penn Treebank45 (46 in NLTK's list)NN, NNS, NNP, NNPS, VB, VBD, VBG, VBN, VBP, VBZ, JJ, JJR, JJS, …
Brown corpusabout 87even finer, with separate tags for forms of be and have

A bigger tagset is harder to predict, since there are more classes, but more useful to later steps such as parsing. NLTK maps Brown and Penn tags to the universal set with tagset="universal", which is how activity 3 compared them.

Why tagging is hard but not hopeless
  1. In the Brown corpus only about 11% of word types are ambiguous (DeRose 1988).
  2. But those types are the frequent ones, such as that, back, down, put and set, so about 40% of word tokens are ambiguous.
  3. Tagging each word with its most frequent tag is already right about 92% of the time.
  4. Models that use context reach 97% and above on the Penn Treebank. The last few percent are the hard cases.

The look up tagger

A student asked what "look up" means. It is the same idea as the n-gram tables of step 4: count, then store the answer in a dictionary. A unigram tagger stores each word's most frequent tag. A bigram tagger keys on the previous tag and the word. Chained with backoff, the most specific tagger that has seen the case decides.

Discriminative taggers

The perceptron tagger (Collins 2002) learns from its errors. It reads features of the word, its prefix and suffix, the words before and after, and the previous tags, so it can use clues that a bigram of tags cannot see. NLTK's default pos_tag is an averaged perceptron. A maximum entropy tagger is a log linear classifier, P(t | context) = exp(Σ θk fk(t, context)) ÷ Z, with binary features such as "word ends in -ing and tag is VBG". Ratnaparkhi (1996) reached about 96.6% on the Penn Treebank with one.

Remember
Whatever tagger you use, do error analysis. Find where it fails, group the errors, then improve. The professor asked students to do activity 3 with their own hands, not with a chatbot: "You will only learn when you do it yourself."
Lesson 4 of 4

Generative or discriminative

GenerativeExam favourite
T̂ = argmaxT P(W, T) = argmaxT P(T) · P(W | T)
Learns how tag sequences go and which words each tag produces: a noun tends to produce student, dog, book. "A joint win for both parties."
DiscriminativeExam favourite
T̂ = argmaxT P(T | W)
Learns directly which tags fit these words in this context: reads in "The student reads books" is likely a verb.
Why both give the same answer
  1. Bayes: P(T | W) = P(W, T) ÷ P(W).
  2. While we search over tag sequences, the sentence W is fixed, so P(W) is one positive number for every candidate T.
  3. Dividing every score by the same positive number cannot change which is largest.
  4. argmaxT P(T | W) = argmaxT P(W, T)
  5. For "they can fish" (chapter 5.3) the best sequence N M V has P(W, T) = 0.05292 and P(T | W) = 0.7233. The ranking of all six non zero sequences is the same under both.
Going deeper: Is this the same as supervised against unsupervised?

A student asked exactly this. No: it is a different axis. Both kinds of model can be trained from tagged data, as here. The difference is what is modelled: the joint P(W, T), which can also generate sentences, or only the conditional P(T | W). One practical bonus of the generative HMM is that it can also be trained from untagged text with the Baum-Welch (EM) algorithm, because it models P(W).

One idea, four depths

Give every word a label such as noun or verb. The same word can need different labels in different sentences.

Tagsets range from 12 to about 87 tags. Look up taggers with backoff are a strong baseline (about 92%). Generative HMMs model P(W, T), discriminative taggers model P(T | W), and both pick the same best sequence.

The decision is a structured prediction over qn sequences. Generative models need few features but strong independence assumptions. Discriminative models such as CRFs normalise over the whole sequence, avoiding the label bias of left to right classifiers.

Neural taggers reach about 97.5 to 98% on the Penn Treebank, close to the agreement between human annotators. Research looks at low resource languages, cross lingual transfer with Universal Dependencies, and what tagging information large language models learn without being taught.
