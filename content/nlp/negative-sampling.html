Lesson 1 of 4

The expensive denominator

The softmax of chapter 2.2 divides by a sum over every word of the vocabulary, maybe 100,000 words. Every single update pays for 100,000 dot products and exponentials. That is far too slow. Standard word2vec therefore trains skip-gram with negative sampling instead.

The costly part
P(o | c) = exp(uo·vc)∑w ∈ V exp(uw·vc)
The denominator sums over all words in the vocabulary.

The main idea is to train binary logistic regressions. One classifier step asks "is this a real pair seen in the text?" for the true pair (centre, context), and several noise pairs (the centre word with random words) are asked the same question with the answer no.

Real world scenario: learning to spot a friend in a crowd

To learn your best friend’s face you do not need to compare it with every person on Earth. A few random strangers at a time are enough: "this is my friend, those are not". Negative sampling teaches the model the same way, with a handful of random words instead of the whole vocabulary.

Lesson 2 of 4

The negative sampling objective

Mikolov and others (2013) replace the softmax with sigmoids. For a true pair we want σ(uo·vc) close to 1. For each of K noise words drawn from a distribution Pn we want σ(uk·vc) close to 0, which is the same as σ(−uk·vc) close to 1.

Loss for one pairExam favourite
Jneg(o, vc, U) = −log σ(uo·vc) − ∑k=1K log σ(−uk·vc)
First term: make the real pair likely. Second: make each noise pair unlikely.
Symmetry of the sigmoid
σ(−x) = 1 − σ(x)
So log σ(−uk·vc) = log(1 − σ(uk·vc)).
GradientsExam favourite
∂J∂vc = (σ(uo·vc) − 1)uo + ∑k σ(uk·vc)uk,   ∂J∂uk = σ(uk·vc)vc
Each term is (prediction − label) × the other vector. Only 1 + K output vectors change.
Lecture 5 question: how many sigmoids per centre word?
  1. Window 2 on each side, the full window used: 4 context words.
  2. For each context word: 1 positive pair and K = 10 negative pairs, so 11 sigmoids.
  3. A student answered 11 at first. That counts one context word only.
  4. 11 × 4 = 44 sigmoid computations for one centre word occurrence

Lesson 3 of 4

Which words to use as noise: U(w) to the 3/4

U(w) is the unigram distribution, how often each word appears in the corpus. Sampling negatives straight from U(w) would mostly pick "the" and "of". Those words appear in almost every context, so they teach the model little. Raising U(w) to the power 3/4 reshapes the distribution: common words drop a little and rare words rise.

Noise distributionExam favourite
Pn(w) = U(w)3/4∑w′ U(w′)3/4
α = 3/4 was found to work best. α = 1 is the raw unigram, α = 0 is uniform.

Going deeper: what negative sampling really optimises

Negative sampling is a simplified form of noise contrastive estimation. NCE would recover the true softmax probabilities, while negative sampling drops the correction term and only learns good vectors. Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed that at its optimum uo·vc = PMI(o, c) − log K, so the dot product estimates how much more often the two words co-occur than chance, shifted by log K. The 3/4 power smooths the PMI, which reduces its well known bias toward rare words.

The negative sampling notebook

The second notebook keeps the five sentences, window 1, d = 3, η = 0.05 and 500 epochs, and adds K = 3 negatives per positive pair drawn from counts0.75. It never uses the true context word as noise. With this objective cats and dogs become the closest pair, cosine 0.98, because they share like, chase and milk contexts.

Negative_samples.ipynb, the sampling and training cells (plotting trimmed)
counts = np.zeros(V)for sentence in sentences:    for word in sentence:        counts[word_to_id[word]] += 1neg_probs = counts ** 0.75                     # standard word2vec sampling distributionneg_probs = neg_probs / neg_probs.sum()def sample_negatives(context, K):    negatives = []    while len(negatives) < K:        w = np.random.choice(V, p=neg_probs)        if w != context:                       # never use the true context word as noise            negatives.append(w)    return negativesfor epoch in range(epochs):    for center, context in pairs:        h = E[center]        targets = [context] + sample_negatives(context, K)        labels = [1] + [0] * K        for t, label in zip(targets, labels):            score = sigmoid(h @ W[t])            g = score - label                  # sigmoid output minus label            grad_h += g * W[t]            W[t] -= learning_rate * g * h        E[center] -= learning_rate * grad_h

One idea, four depths

Instead of comparing a word with every other word, we compare it with its real neighbour and a few random words. That is much faster and still teaches which words belong together.

Each positive pair becomes 1 + K logistic regression examples. The cost per pair drops from V dot products to K + 1, so training scales to billions of words.

The class question "should the clusters be the same with and without negative sampling?" has the answer no in general. The two objectives have different optima: softmax models P(o | c), negative sampling models shifted PMI, and a tiny corpus with random initialisation amplifies the difference.

Contrastive learning with sampled negatives is now everywhere: sentence embeddings, CLIP for images and text, retrieval models. The choice of negatives (random, in batch, hard negatives) is a research topic of its own.

Remember
Stop words such as "the" are still used as positive pairs when they appear in the window. They are only down weighted as negatives. This was asked in class.
