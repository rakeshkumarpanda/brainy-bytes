Lesson 1 of 5

Count the neighbours directly

Word2vec walks through the corpus again and again, one window at a time. A simpler question from the lecture: why not walk through the corpus once and write down how often every pair of words appears near each other? The result is a co-occurrence matrix X. Row i belongs to word i, column j to word j, and Xij counts how often j appears within the window of i.

Window co-occurrenceExam favourite
Xij = number of times word j appears within m words of word i
A symmetric window counts left and right neighbours. Window 1 on the slide, 5 to 10 is more common.
Word vector by counting
vector(i) = row i of X
Its length is the vocabulary size V, so the vector grows with the vocabulary.

There are two kinds of matrix. A window matrix counts nearby words, like word2vec, and captures both syntax and meaning (word space). A word by document matrix counts which documents a word appears in. That gives general topics, for example all sports words get similar rows, and leads to Latent Semantic Analysis (document space).

The slide corpus is "I like deep learning .", "I like NLP ." and "I enjoy flying ." with window 1 and a symmetric count.

  2. Left neighbours: I twice. Right neighbours: deep once, NLP once.

  4. Rows "like" and "enjoy" share the column I, so the two verbs already look a little alike.

Raw count vectors have three problems. They are as long as the vocabulary, so they need a lot of storage. They are sparse, most cells are 0, so classifiers built on them are less robust. And frequent function words such as "the" and "has" dominate the counts. The cure is to keep the important information in a small dense vector of 25 to 1000 numbers.

Real world scenario: a supermarket learning what is bought together

A supermarket can keep a table of every pair of products that appear on the same receipt. Bread and butter get a high count, bread and engine oil a low one. Two products with similar rows, such as two brands of butter, play the same role for shoppers. That table is a co-occurrence matrix, and the rows are product vectors.

Lesson 2 of 5

Shrink the matrix with SVD

The classic way to get short dense vectors from X is the Singular Value Decomposition. Every matrix can be written as a rotation, a stretch along a few axes and another rotation. Keeping only the k biggest stretches gives the best possible rank k copy of X, and the first k columns of U become k-dimensional word vectors.

SVDExam favourite
X = U Σ VT
U and V are orthonormal: unit columns at right angles. Σ is diagonal with singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ ... ≥ 0.
Truncated SVDExam favourite
Xk = Uk Σk VkT
Keep the top k singular values. Word i gets the vector row i of UkΣk.
Singular values
σi = √λi(XTX)
The square roots of the eigenvalues of XTX.
Kept energy
σ₁² + ... + σk²∑ σi²
The share of the squared size of X that the rank k copy keeps.
A 2 × 2 example by hand
  1. X = 3045. Then XTX = 25202025.
  2. Eigenvalues of XTX: 25 + 20 = 45 and 25 − 20 = 5, with directions (1, 1) and (1, −1).
  3. σ₁ = √45 = 6.708, σ₂ = √5 = 2.236
  4. Rank 1 keeps 45 ÷ (45 + 5) = 90 percent of the energy, so one number per row already describes most of X.

The lecture warns that SVD on raw counts does not work well. Function words are too frequent, so syntax has too much influence. Rohde and others (2005, COALS) used several fixes.

FixWhat it does
log of the countssqueezes 1000 and 10 into 6.9 and 2.3
min(X, t) with t ≈ 100caps very frequent pairs
ignore function wordsdrops the, he, has from the counts
ramped windowscloser words count more than far ones
Pearson correlation, negatives set to 0measures association instead of raw frequency
Common mistake
SVD costs about O(V³) time for a V × V matrix and must be redone when new words arrive. That is one reason prediction based methods such as word2vec took over, and GloVe tries to combine the two.
Going deeper: why truncated SVD is the best low rank copy

The Eckart and Young theorem says that Xk minimises the Frobenius error ‖X − A‖ over every matrix A of rank k, and the error left over is √(σk+1² + ... ). Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed that skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises a shifted PMI matrix, so SVD of a PMI matrix and word2vec are close relatives.

Lesson 3 of 5

GloVe: meaning lives in probability ratios

GloVe (Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014) is a matrix factorisation method motivated by ratios of probabilities. First turn counts into probabilities: P(k | i) = Xik ÷ Xi, where Xi = ∑k Xik is the total count of row i.

Probe word kP(k | ice)P(k | steam)Ratio
solid1.9 × 10−42.2 × 10−58.9
gas6.6 × 10−57.8 × 10−48.5 × 10−2
water3.0 × 10−32.2 × 10−31.36
fashion1.7 × 10−51.8 × 10−50.96

A single probability says little, because water is common near both words. The ratio is what separates them. Much bigger than 1 means k belongs with ice, much smaller than 1 means k belongs with steam, and close to 1 means k does not help tell them apart.

From the ratio to the GloVe model
  1. Goal: a function of word vectors that gives the ratio, F(wi, wj, w̃k) = P(k | i) ÷ P(k | j).
  2. Vectors are linear spaces, so use the difference and a dot product: F((wi − wj)·w̃k).
  3. A ratio should turn into a difference. Choose F = exp, then wi·w̃k = log P(k | i) = log Xik − log Xi.
  4. log Xi does not depend on k, so it becomes a bias bi. Add b̃k to keep i and k symmetric.
  5. wi·w̃k + bi + b̃k = log Xik
GloVe model equationExam favourite
wi·w̃j + bi + b̃j ≈ log Xij
Each word has a word vector w, a context vector w̃ and two biases.
Ratio in vector form
(wi − wj)·w̃k = log P(k | i)P(k | j)
Differences of word vectors encode ratios, which is why analogies such as king − man + woman work.
In the exam
Exams like the table: given two probabilities, compute the ratio and say whether the probe word is an ice word, a steam word or neutral. 8.9 is an ice word, 0.085 a steam word, 1.36 and 0.96 neutral.
Lesson 4 of 5

The GloVe loss and its weighting function

GloVe fits the model equation with weighted least squares over every pair that was seen at least once. Rare pairs are noisy and very frequent pairs should not dominate, so each pair gets a weight f(Xij).

GloVe objectiveExam favourite
J = ∑i,j f(Xij) (wi·w̃j + bi + b̃j − log Xij)²
Only pairs with Xij > 0 enter, because f(0) = 0 and log 0 is undefined.
Weighting functionExam favourite
f(x) = (x ÷ xmax)α if x < xmax, else 1
The paper uses xmax = 100 and α = 3/4. The class notebook uses xmax = 10.
Gradient for one pair
∂J∂wi = 2 f(Xij) eij w̃j,   ∂J∂bi = 2 f(Xij) eij
eij = prediction − log Xij, the error of that pair.
Final vector
Wi = wi + w̃i
The two vectors play symmetric roles, so adding them (or averaging) is a little better than either alone.

f has three jobs from the slide: it is 0 when X is 0 so non-occurrences are ignored, it grows for small counts, and it saturates at 1 for very large counts.

One pair by hand
  1. wi = (0.5, 0.2), w̃j = (0.4, −0.1), bi = 0.1, b̃j = 0.2, Xij = 2, xmax = 10, α = 0.75.
  2. Prediction: 0.5 × 0.4 + 0.2 × (−0.1) + 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.48. Target: log 2 = 0.6931.
  3. Error e = 0.48 − 0.6931 = −0.2131. Weight f(2) = 0.20.75 = 0.2991.
  4. loss = 0.2991 × (−0.2131)² = 0.0136, and ∂J/∂bi = 2 × 0.2991 × (−0.2131) = −0.1275
  5. The gradient is negative, so gradient descent raises bi and the prediction moves up toward log 2.
Step on the slideWhat happens
1 to 3collect a corpus, choose a window (for example ±5), count X
4take log X because raw counts are noisy and biased by frequency
5 and 6predict wi·w̃j + bi + b̃j and match it to log Xij
7weight each pair with f(Xij)
8minimise the weighted squared error with gradient descent
9use wi or (wi + w̃i) ÷ 2 as the final vector
Common mistake
The loss is a weighted squared error on log counts, not a cross entropy. Students often write −log P like word2vec.

The GloVe notebook and what it teaches

The class notebook trains GloVe on ten tiny sentences: ice, water, steam and gas sentences plus the cats and dogs sentences. It counts with window 2 and adds 1 ÷ distance, as the algorithm box of the slides does, so a word two places away adds 0.5. Then it trains 20-dimensional vectors for 300 epochs with Adagrad.

Why the loss reaches 0 and ice is far from water
  1. Parameters: 21 words × 20 numbers × 2 vectors + 21 × 2 biases = 882.
  2. Equations to satisfy: only 80 observed pairs.
  3. 882 free numbers for 80 targets can fit every target exactly, so the loss is 0.000000 after 50 epochs.
  4. The model memorises, it does not generalise. Pairs never seen together get no constraint at all, so cosine values such as ice vs water = −0.75 are almost random.
  5. Lesson: GloVe needs many more pairs than parameters, which is why real GloVe uses billions of tokens

One idea, four depths

Count which words appear near each other. Words with similar neighbour counts mean similar things. GloVe squeezes those counts into short lists of numbers.

GloVe learns vectors whose dot product plus two biases equals the log co-occurrence count, with a weight that ignores missing pairs and caps very frequent ones.

The objective follows from asking that vector differences encode probability ratios. It is a weighted factorisation of the log count matrix and trains only on non-zero cells, so its cost grows with the number of seen pairs, not with V².

Levy, Goldberg and Dagan (2015) showed that once hyperparameters such as window weighting and context distribution smoothing are matched, SVD of PMI, SGNS and GloVe perform about the same. The gains came from the tricks as much as from the models.
