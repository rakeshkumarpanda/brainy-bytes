Sequence labelling with an RNN

RNNs have three main uses: generating text, labelling every word of a sequence, and classifying a whole sentence. In sequence labelling the RNN outputs one label per word. Part of speech tagging is the classic example: "Janet will back the bill" becomes NOUN AUX VERB DET NOUN.

Tag distribution at step tExam favourite
ŷ(t) = softmax(V h(t)) ∈ ℝK
K is the number of tags. h(t) is the RNN state after reading word t.
Training loss
J = −1T ∑t log ŷ(t)yt
Cross-entropy with the gold tag at every position.
Prediction
tagt = argmaxk ŷk(t)
One independent choice per word.
Use of an RNNOutputExample
Generationnext word at every steplanguage model
Sequence labellingone label per wordPOS tagging, NER
Classificationone label per sentencesentiment, spam
Tagging "Janet will back the bill"
  1. Each word goes in as an embedding. The RNN state h(t) carries what came before.
  2. "back" can be a noun, a verb or an adverb. After "Janet will" the state favours a verb.
  3. The softmax over 17 universal POS tags gives VERB the highest probability.
  4. Output: NOUN AUX VERB DET NOUN, one tag per word, no segmentation needed
Remember
A left to right RNN tagger only sees the past when it tags word t. "back" in "the back of the bill" needs the words after it, which is what bidirectional RNNs add.
Lesson 2 of 5

Named entity recognition

A named entity is anything that can be referred to with a proper name. NER finds the spans of text that are proper names and tags their type. The term also covers things that are not entities, such as dates, times and prices.

TagTypeExample
PERpersonMarie Curie
LOClocationNew York City
ORGorganisationStanford University
GPEgeo-political entityBoulder, Colorado

NER is useful for sentiment analysis (how do customers feel about a company or a person), question answering (answer questions about an entity) and information extraction (pull facts about entities out of text).

Why NER is hardExample
Segmentation: POS gives one tag per word, NER must find where each entity starts and ends"United Airlines Holding" is one entity of three words
Type ambiguity: the same string can be different typesWashington: a person, a city (GPE), a state, or the government (ORG)
Real world scenario: screening news for a trading desk

A bank reads thousands of news stories an hour. NER finds every company (ORG) and country (GPE) mentioned, so a story about "Apple" is routed to the analyst for Apple Inc. and not to the fruit market desk. Getting the span right matters: "Bank of America" must not be split into "Bank" and "America".

Lesson 3 of 5

BIO tagging: spans become one tag per word

How can a span problem become a sequence problem with one label per word, like POS tagging? BIO tagging marks the first word of a span with B, the other words inside it with I, and every word outside any span with O. The type is attached to B and I.

The slide sentence: [PER Jane Villanueva] of [ORG United] , a unit of [ORG United Airlines Holding] , said the fare applies to the [LOC Chicago] route.

WordJaneVillanuevaofUnited,UnitedAirlinesHoldingChicago
BIOB-PERI-PEROB-ORGOB-ORGI-ORGI-ORGB-LOC
Number of BIO tagsExam favourite
2n + 1
n B tags, n I tags and 1 O tag, where n is the number of entity types.
Other schemes
IO: n + 1,   BIOES: 4n + 1
BIOES adds E for the last word and S for a single word span.

NER is scored per entity span, not per word. A predicted span counts only if both its boundaries and its type match the gold span exactly.

Span precision and recallExam favourite
P = correct spanspredicted spans,   R = correct spansgold spans
F1 = 2PR ÷ (P + R).
Span F1 by hand
  1. Gold has 4 spans. The model predicts 5 spans.
  2. "United Airlines" is predicted instead of "United Airlines Holding": a boundary error, so it is wrong. 3 predicted spans match exactly.
  3. P = 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6, R = 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75.
  4. F1 = 2 × 0.6 × 0.75 ÷ 1.35 = 0.667
Common mistake
Why B is needed: with IO tags "[ORG Google] [ORG Microsoft]" becomes I-ORG I-ORG and looks like one entity. B marks the start of the second one.
Lesson 4 of 5

The two parameter questions

The lecture asks for parameter counts of an RNN tagger. The input is a 300-dimensional embedding, the hidden vector has 50 numbers, and biases are excluded. Three matrices matter: embedding to hidden, hidden to hidden, hidden to output.

RNN tagger parameters (no biases)Exam favourite
E·H + H·H + H·K
E embedding size, H hidden size, K number of output labels. The embedding table itself is not counted here.
BiRNN tagger
2(E·H + H·H) + 2H·K
Two directions, and the output layer reads the joined 2H vector.
POS tagging with 50 tags
  1. Embedding to hidden: 300 × 50 = 15,000.
  2. Hidden to hidden: 50 × 50 = 2,500.
  3. Hidden to output: 50 × 50 = 2,500.
  4. Total = 20,000
NER with 5 entity types and BIO
  1. BIO creates a B tag and an I tag for each type plus O: 2 × 5 + 1 = 11 labels.
  2. Embedding to hidden 15,000 and hidden to hidden 2,500 are unchanged.
  3. Hidden to output: 50 × 11 = 550.
  4. Total = 15,000 + 2,500 + 550 = 18,050
In the exam
State what you count. If the question says "excluding bias terms" and does not mention the embedding table, count only the RNN and output weights, as the lecture answer does.

Sentence classification and bidirectional RNNs

To classify a whole sentence, run the RNN over it and turn the hidden states into one sentence vector. The simplest choice is the final state h(T). Often better is the element-wise mean or max of all the hidden states. A softmax on that vector gives the class.

Sentiment shows why one direction is not enough. In "the movie was terribly exciting", the word "terribly" looks negative from the left, but "exciting" on its right makes it positive. A bidirectional RNN runs one RNN left to right and another right to left and joins their states.

Forward RNN
h→(t) = RNNFW(h→(t−1), x(t))
Reads from the start.
Backward RNN
h←(t) = RNNBW(h←(t+1), x(t))
Reads from the end, with its own weights.
Joined stateExam favourite
h(t) = [h→(t), h←(t)] ∈ ℝ2H
Each word now sees the whole sentence.
Sentence vector
s = 1T ∑t h(t)   or   s = h(T)
Then ŷ = softmax(W s).
UnidirectionalBidirectional
Sees at word twords 1 to tthe whole sentence
Good forlanguage modelling and generationtagging, NER, classification, encoders

BiRNN tagger parameters
  1. E = 300, H = 50 in each direction, 11 BIO labels, no biases.
  2. Each direction: 300 × 50 + 50 × 50 = 17,500, so two directions: 35,000.
  3. Output layer reads 2H = 100 numbers: 100 × 11 = 1,100.
  4. Total = 36,100
One idea, four depths

An RNN can give a label to every word, for example "this word is the name of a person". Reading the sentence from both ends helps, because the meaning of a word often depends on what comes after it.

NER becomes per word classification with BIO tags (2n + 1 labels). A BiRNN joins forward and backward states for each word, and a sentence vector from the final or pooled states feeds a classifier.

Choosing each tag independently can produce invalid sequences such as O I-PER. A CRF layer on top scores whole tag sequences with transition weights and decodes the best one with the Viterbi algorithm, as in the BiLSTM-CRF of Lample et al. (2016).

Modern NER fine-tunes a pre-trained transformer such as BERT, with subword tokens aligned to word level BIO tags. Nested entities ("[ORG Bank of [GPE America]]") do not fit flat BIO and need span based or hypergraph models.
