Lesson 1 of 3

Lexical ambiguity: one word, several meanings

A student in lecture 1 said "I sat near a bank" and the class split. People who think of the State Bank of India heard money. People who like nature heard a river. Taken alone, the word cannot decide. What decides in real life is the earlier conversation, the knowledge speaker and listener share, and the physical setting.

TermWhat it meansExample
HomonymySame spelling and sound, unrelated meanings with different historiesbank (river, from Old Norse banki) and bank (money, from Italian banca, a bench)
PolysemyOne word, several related senses from one originmouth of a person and mouth of a river, head of a company
HomophoneSame sound, different spelling and meaningpear and peer, write and right
HomographSame spelling, different sound and meaninglead the metal and lead the verb

Choosing the right sense by computer is called word sense disambiguation (WSD). The oldest practical method, Lesk (1986), compares the words around the target with the dictionary definition (the gloss) of each sense and picks the sense with the largest overlap. It is Firth's idea that you know a word by the company it keeps, turned into a counting rule.

Simplified LeskExam favourite
sense* = argmaxs |context(w) ∩ gloss(s)|
Count the shared words between the sentence and each gloss, after removing very common words. Ties and zero overlaps fall back to the most frequent sense.

The cat jumped over the wall

The class drew four different paths for one sentence: landing on top of the wall, a straight hop beside it, dropping onto it from above, and a full arc to the other side. Most chose the full arc, but not everyone. The word over carries the ambiguity. It can mean above and across (a plane over a hill), covering (a blanket over a bed) or finished (the meeting is over). George Lakoff (1987) showed these senses form a family around one spatial picture, a textbook case of polysemy. Language under specifies, and the listener fills the gap with expectations about how cats jump.

Lesson 2 of 3

Structural ambiguity: one string, several trees

"John saw Mary with a telescope" has no ambiguous word. Yet it has two meanings, because the same words can be grouped into two different trees.

ReadingWhere the phrase attachesMeaning
InstrumentTo the verb phrase (high attachment)John used a telescope to see Mary
PossessionTo the noun phrase Mary (low attachment)John saw Mary, who had a telescope

This is prepositional phrase attachment ambiguity. Grammar allows both trees, so only world knowledge or context can choose. "Ate pizza with a fork" prefers the verb (a fork is a tool), "ate pizza with olives" prefers the noun (olives are a topping), yet the two strings have exactly the same shape.

Ambiguity grows very fast

Add more phrases and the number of trees explodes. Church and Patil (1982) showed that a verb, its object and k prepositional phrases can be bracketed in a number of ways given by the Catalan numbers.

Catalan numbersExam favourite
Cn = 1n + 1 × (2n choose n) = (2n)!(n + 1)! n!
With k phrases the count is Ck+1: 2, 5, 14, 42, 132 for k = 1 to 5.
Why "I saw the man on the hill with a telescope" has 5 readings
  1. There are k = 2 phrases: "on the hill" and "with a telescope".
  2. The count is C3 = (1 ÷ 4) × (6 choose 3) = 20 ÷ 4 = 5.
  3. Two of the five: I was on the hill using a telescope, or the man was on a hill that had a telescope on it.
  4. A third phrase ("in the morning") would give C4 = 14 trees from a short, ordinary sentence.

The 1985 advert

Lecture 2 showed a real McDonnell Douglas slogan: "At last, a computer that understands you like your mother." The class found three readings.

  1. The computer understands you as well as your mother understands you (the intended one).
  2. The computer understands that you like your mother.
  3. The computer understands you as well as it understands your mother.

Spoken aloud it adds a speech recognition problem, since "you like your mother" sounds almost like "your lie cured mother". And "mother" even has an old second sense, a slimy starter used to make vinegar. One slogan shows acoustic, lexical and structural ambiguity at once. "Copy the local patient files to disk" adds an adjective attachment puzzle: are the patients local or the files?

In the exam
If an exam asks you to classify an example, ask one question: does any single word have two meanings? If yes, lexical. If every word is clear but the grouping can change, structural.

How machines resolve ambiguity

The professor promised later lectures on the technology. Here is the map of how the field has attacked the problem, from rules to neural models.

EraApproachHow it decides
1960s to 1980sRules and heuristicsHand written grammar plus defaults such as right association (attach low, to the nearest phrase)
1990s to 2000sStatistics from treebanksCount how often a verb or noun takes this preposition in a hand parsed corpus, choose the more likely tree
2010s onwardContextual neural modelsEach word vector depends on the whole sentence, so telescope near astronomy and telescope near ship get different representations
Lexical association for attachment
P(verb attachment) ≈ c(verb, prep)c(verb, prep) + c(noun, prep)
In the spirit of Hindle and Rooth (1993): compare how strongly the preposition goes with the verb and with the noun in a large corpus.
One idea, four depths

Many sentences can mean two things. People pick the meaning that makes most sense in the situation. A computer has to learn the same habit from lots of examples.

Lexical ambiguity is solved by word sense disambiguation, for example Lesk overlap. Structural ambiguity is solved by a parser that scores competing trees, for example with counts of which words go with which preposition.

Probabilistic parsers (chapter 5.3) assign every tree a probability and return the most likely one. Attachment decisions are a classic benchmark. Lexicalised models that condition on the head words beat plain grammars because "eat with fork" and "eat with olives" differ only in the head noun of the phrase.

Large models still fail on carefully built ambiguity tests. The class tried ChatGPT and Gemini live and found errors on these classic sentences. Research asks whether models represent several readings at once, how they use discourse to decide, and how to evaluate when even people disagree, as the class did on the cat and the wall.

Real world scenario: a voice assistant in a hospital

A nurse says "copy the local patient files to disk". If the assistant reads "local patient" it might search for patients from the local area. The safe design is to detect that two readings are possible and ask, exactly what SHRDLU did with "grasp the pyramid" (chapter 1.3). In medicine, a wrong guess costs more than one clarifying question.
