Lesson 1 of 4

Counting with FreqDist

Almost every method in this course starts by counting. How often does a word occur, how often does a tag occur, how often does one word follow another. NLTK gives you one class for all of it, FreqDist, which is a dictionary from each item to its count with a few helpful extras.

Python with NLTK, from the notebook (outputs combined)
from nltk.probability import FreqDisttokens = ['a', 'b', 'a', 'a', 'c', 'b', 'a']fdist = FreqDist(tokens)print(fdist)print(fdist['a'], fdist['d'], fdist.max())print(fdist.freq('a'), fdist.N(), fdist.B())
Output recorded in the notebook
<FreqDist with 3 samples and 7 outcomes>
4 0 a
0.5714285714285714 7 3
CallMeaningValue here
fdist[x]Count of x. An unseen item gives 0, not an errorfdist['a'] = 4, fdist['d'] = 0
N()Number of outcomes, which is every token counted7
B()Number of samples (bins), which is the distinct types3
freq(x)Relative frequency, count of x divided by N4 ÷ 7 = 0.5714
max()The most common samplea
most_common(k)The k most common samples with counts[('a', 4), ('b', 2)] for k = 2

The words sample and outcome come from probability. Each token is one outcome of an experiment, and each type is a possible sample value. So N() is tokens and B() is types, the two numbers from chapter 2.1.

Relative frequencyExam favourite
freq(x) = c(x)N
This is also the maximum likelihood estimate of P(x). Chapter 4 builds language models from exactly this ratio.
Check
∑x freq(x) = 1
The relative frequencies of all samples always add to one, so they form a probability distribution.

The notebook then counted the sentence "This is an example sentence to tell you how this example works". word_tokenize gave 12 tokens with 11 types, because This and this are different strings. After lower casing there were 10 types, since this and example each occur twice.

Remember
An item seen once is called a hapax legomenon (Greek for "said once"). In real corpora about half of all types are hapaxes. They are the reason smoothing exists, which is the subject of chapter 4.

Corpora: Brown and the Penn Treebank

A corpus is a large, carefully chosen collection of text. A tagged corpus also stores a part of speech tag for every token, added by trained annotators. These tags are the gold standard that taggers learn from and are scored against.

What tagged text looks like

On disk the class file brown.txt stores the same thing as text, one word/tag token after another: The/at Fulton/np-tl County/nn-tl. The suffix -tl marks a word in a title, so nn-tl is a common noun that is part of a name such as "Grand Jury".

Why Brown and PTB look different

  • Size. Brown in NLTK is more than eleven times larger, so it has more types (56,057 against 12,408).
  • Genre. PTB is financial news, full of numbers and company names. Brown mixes fifteen genres.
  • Empty elements. PTB inserts placeholder tokens such as *-1 and 0 that mark moved or missing words in its syntax trees. They are tagged -NONE-. There are 6,592 of them, so only 94,084 tokens are real words, and two of the top ten "words" are placeholders.
  • Commas. In PTB the comma (4,885) beats the (4,045). Business writing uses many lists and clauses.
In the exam
Exam questions often ask you to "verbally describe how the results differ". Mention size, genre, tagset and the -NONE- tokens, and back each point with one number.

Coarse and fine

A fine tagset can be mapped to a coarse one, never the other way. The notebook checked this on PTB adverbs:

Merging tags adds their countsExam favourite
c(ADV) = c(RB) + c(RBR) + c(RBS) + c(WRB)
Every universal tag count is the sum of the PTB tags mapped to it.
Notebook numbers
3,171 = 2,822 + 136 + 35 + 178
RB does not exist in the universal tagset, so fdist["RB"] gave 0 there.

Which tagset should you use

Fine tagset (PTB, Brown)Coarse tagset (universal)
More information: singular or plural, past or presentLess information
Harder to tag, because there are more classes to confuseEasier and more accurate
Useful for parsing and grammar checkingUseful across languages, since the same 12 tags fit most languages
Real world scenario: a grammar checker for job applications

A tool flags "She have worked here for two years". It needs to know that She is third person singular and that have is a base form verb (VBP) where VBZ (has) is required. The universal tag VERB hides that difference, so this tool must use a fine tagset like PTB.

Going deeper: Tags and ambiguity in numbers

Jurafsky and Martin report that in Brown only about 11 percent of word types can take more than one tag, but they are the common words, so about 40 percent of tokens are ambiguous. Always choosing the most frequent tag for each word is right about 92 percent of the time. Context based taggers in chapter 5 push this past 97 percent.

Counting pairs: towards language models

So far we counted single items. Counting pairs of neighbouring words, called bigrams, is the small step that turns counting into prediction. If "I am" occurs 2 times and "I" occurs 3 times, then after "I" the next word was "am" two times out of three.

Bigram probability by countingExam favourite
P(wi | wi−1) = c(wi−1 wi)c(wi−1)
The maximum likelihood estimate. Chapter 4 explains why it fails for pairs never seen.
Most frequent tag baseline
tag(w) = argmaxt c(w, t)
Counting word and tag pairs gives the simplest tagger. Chapter 5 adds context.
Using counts to answer a question from the notebook
  1. Question: what share of PTB tokens are nouns of any kind?
  2. Under the universal tagset, NOUN = 28,867.
  3. 28,867 ÷ 100,676 ≈ 0.2867 = 28.67%
  4. Under the PTB tagset you would add NN, NNS, NNP and NNPS, which gives the same total because they all map to NOUN.
One idea, four depths

Counting tells you which words and tags are common. Dividing a count by the total gives a share, which is a probability.

FreqDist stores counts, N() is tokens and B() is types. Relative frequency is the maximum likelihood estimate. Tagged corpora like Brown and PTB give counts of words, tags and word tag pairs, and tagsets range from 12 to about 87 base tags.

Counts from a sample estimate a population distribution. The estimate is unbiased for frequent items but unreliable for rare ones, and gives zero to anything unseen. Good Turing and Kneser Ney smoothing (chapter 4) correct this. Corpus design (balance across genres, as in Brown) decides which population the counts represent.

Modern corpora reach trillions of tokens, and counting itself becomes a systems problem: count min sketches, suffix arrays and infini-gram style indices return n-gram counts over web scale text. Research also studies dataset documentation, contamination of test sets inside training data, and how tagset design affects cross lingual transfer.
