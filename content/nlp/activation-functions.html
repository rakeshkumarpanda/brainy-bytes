Lesson 1 of 3

Why a network needs a bend

At the end of the day a neural network is a function approximator. It maps inputs to outputs for regression and classification. If every layer were only a matrix multiplication, stacking layers would change nothing, because the product of matrices is one matrix.

Linear layers collapseExam favourite
W₂(W₁x) = (W₂W₁)x = Wx
Ten linear layers are still one linear layer, with ten times the compute.
A layer with an activation
h = f(Wx + b)
f is applied to each number separately. That one bend is what gives depth its power.
Worked example: the collapse in numbers
  1. W₁ = 1201, W₂ = 2011, x = (3, 1).
  2. W₁x = (5, 1), then W₂(5, 1) = (10, 6).
  3. W₂W₁ = 2413 and (W₂W₁)x = (10, 6), the same answer.
  4. With ReLU between them, x = (−3, 1) gives (0, 1) after the first layer, so the output is (0, 1), not the linear (−2, −2)
Real world scenario: folding paper

A linear layer can stretch, rotate or slide a sheet of paper but never fold it. Two red and blue patterns that are mixed up on the sheet stay mixed up. An activation folds the sheet along a line. After a few folds, points that were tangled can be separated with one straight cut. This is the slide line: linear layers stretch and rotate space, non-linearities bend it.

One idea, four depths

Adding up and multiplying can only draw straight lines. To draw curves, each neuron needs a little bend. The bend is the activation function.

Without activations an L layer network is the linear map W = WL … W₁. With them, a network with one hidden layer of enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region (the universal approximation theorem).

The theorem says nothing about how many units are needed or whether gradient descent finds them. Depth helps because composing bends multiplies the number of linear pieces: a ReLU network of depth L can produce a number of regions that grows exponentially with L.

Activation choice interacts with initialisation (He for ReLU, Glorot for tanh), with normalisation and with the loss landscape. Smooth gates such as GELU and SwiGLU are standard in transformers, and research still compares them by trainability more than by expressive power.

Common mistake
More non-linear layers give more expressive power but also harder optimisation and a higher risk of overfitting. The slide closes with exactly this warning.
Lesson 2 of 3

Threshold, sigmoid, tanh and hard tanh

The oldest units fired 1 or 0, a threshold. They have no useful slope, so gradient descent gets no signal and cannot learn. The logistic sigmoid replaced them with a smooth curve and a probabilistic reading. tanh is the same curve rescaled to (−1, 1).

tanh from the sigmoidExam favourite
tanh(z) = 2σ(2z) − 1
Twice as steep at 0 (slope 1 against 0.25) and centred on zero.
Slopes
σ′ = σ(1 − σ)    tanh′ = 1 − tanh²
Both shrink toward 0 for large |z|: the saturation problem.
Hard tanh
hardtanh(z) = −1 for z < −1, z in between, 1 for z > 1
Straight pieces instead of curves, so no exponentials are needed.
UnitRangeGoodProblem
Threshold0 or 1SimpleSlope 0, no learning
Sigmoid(0, 1)Probability readingSaturates, outputs always positive
tanh(−1, 1)Zero centred, faster convergenceSaturates, needs exponentials
Hard tanh[−1, 1]Cheap to computeFlat outside [−1, 1]
Check the identity at z = 0.5
  1. σ(2 × 0.5) = σ(1) = 0.73106.
  2. 2 × 0.73106 − 1 = 0.46212.
  3. tanh 0.5 = 0.46212, the same number
Remember
Sigmoid outputs are always positive, so the gradients of all weights into the next neuron share one sign. Updates zig-zag. tanh avoids this, which is why it was preferred in hidden layers.
Lesson 3 of 3

ReLU and its smooth successors

For deep networks the first thing to try is ReLU: max(z, 0). It is cheap and its slope is exactly 1 for every positive input, so gradients flow back through many layers. Its weakness is the flat side: the slope for z < 0 is 0, the answer to the slide question, and a neuron stuck there is dead.

ReLU and leaky ReLUExam favourite
ReLU(z) = max(z, 0)    leaky(z) = z for z > 0, a·z otherwise
Leaky ReLU keeps a small slope a (for example 0.01). Parametric ReLU learns a.
Swish
swish(z) = z · σ(z)
A soft gate between 0 and 1 multiplies the input. Ramachandran and others, arXiv 1710.05941.
GELUExam favourite
GELU(z) = z · P(X ≤ z), X ~ N(0, 1)   ≈ z · σ(1.702 z)
Scales z by how likely a standard normal is to be below it. Used in BERT and RoBERTa. arXiv 1606.08415.

Going deeper: why transformers moved to smooth gates

ReLU variants solved gradient flow, but their kink at 0 makes the loss surface less smooth. Swish and GELU behave like ReLU for large |z| and bend smoothly near 0, even passing small negative values. Gated linear units such as SwiGLU multiply one projection by a swish of another and are now standard in large language models. The gain is a small but consistent improvement in training stability and final loss.

In the exam
Expect "what is the gradient of ReLU for x < 0?" Answer 0. Expect also "why tanh over sigmoid in hidden layers?" Answer: zero centred and stronger slope.
