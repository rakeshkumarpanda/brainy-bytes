Lesson 1 of 5

Greedy decoding and its problem

At inference time the decoder generates one token at a time and feeds it back in. Which word should it pick at each step? The simplest answer is greedy decoding: take the single most probable word, argmaxw P(w | y<t, x), every time.

Greedy stepExam favourite
ŷt = argmaxw ∈ V P(w | ŷ<t, x)
One choice per step, never revisited.
What we really wantExam favourite
ŷ = argmaxy P(y | x) = argmaxy ∏t P(yt | y<t, x)
The best whole sequence, not the best word at each step.
The slide question: what will greedy decoding choose?
  1. First step: a 0.5, the 0.4, one 0.1. Greedy takes "a".
  2. After "a": cat 0.4, dog 0.3, mat 0.3. Greedy takes "cat". Total 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.20.
  3. What is globally optimal? "the cat" scores 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.36, and "one cat" 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.05.
  4. Greedy returns "a cat" (0.20), the best sequence is "the cat" (0.36)

The issue with greedy decoding is that it has no way to undo decisions. A word that looks best now can lead to a poor continuation, and a slightly weaker first word can lead to a much better sentence. In translation, greedy decoding of "il a m’ entarté" can commit to "he hit a" and then has nothing sensible to say.

In the exam
Greedy is fast and often fine, but it is not optimal. Exams ask for an example where greedy fails: show a first choice with a high probability followed by low probabilities, against a lower first choice followed by a high one.
Lesson 2 of 5

Beam search

Exhaustive search would score every possible sequence: |V|T of them, which is far too many. Beam search is the practical middle way. On each step it keeps track of the k most probable partial translations, called hypotheses. k is the beam size, in practice around 5 to 10.

Hypothesis scoreExam favourite
score(y1, ..., yt) = log P(y1:t | x) = ∑i=1t log P(yi | y<i, x)
All scores are negative. Higher is better. Logs turn products into sums.
One beam stepExam favourite
expand each of the k hypotheses by every word, keep the top k of the k·|V| candidates
k = 1 is greedy decoding.
Beam size 2 on the slide tree (slide 116)
  1. Step 1: keep the top 2 of {a 0.5, the 0.4, one 0.1}: "a" (log 0.5 = −0.693) and "the" (log 0.4 = −0.916).
  2. Step 2: expand both. a cat −1.609, a dog −1.897, a mat −1.897, the cat −1.022, the dog −3.219.
  3. Keep the top 2: "the cat" (−1.022) and "a cat" (−1.609).
  4. Beam search returns "the cat" with P = 0.36, the same as the optimal solution here
Common mistake
Beam search is not guaranteed to find the optimal solution. It is much more efficient than exhaustive search, and a larger k gets closer to the optimum at a higher cost.

Stopping, length and cost

In greedy decoding we stop when the model produces <END>. In beam search different hypotheses can end at different times. When a hypothesis produces <END> it is complete: place it aside and continue exploring the others.

Stopping criterion (slide 117)Meaning
reach a time step Ta preset maximum length
have n completed hypothesesa preset number of finished candidates

Finishing up: pick the completed hypothesis with the best score. One problem: longer hypotheses have lower scores, because every extra word adds another negative log probability. Fix it by normalising by length.

Length normalised scoreExam favourite
1t ∑i=1t log P(yi | y<i, x)
Average log probability per word, used to pick the final answer.
Cost per step
exhaustive: |V|T sequences in total,   beam: k · |V| candidates per step
Beam search is linear in T.
Why length normalisation matters
  1. Hypothesis A: 2 words with log probabilities −0.5 and −0.6, total −1.1.
  2. Hypothesis B: 4 words, each −0.4, total −1.6.
  3. Raw totals prefer A (−1.1 > −1.6). Per word: A −0.55, B −0.40.
  4. Normalised scores prefer B, the longer and more fluent hypothesis
How many sequences would exhaustive search score?
  1. |V| = 10,000 and T = 10.
  2. 10,00010 = 1040 sequences
  3. Beam search with k = 5 scores 5 × 10,000 = 50,000 candidates per step, 500,000 in total.
  4. That is 2 × 1034 times fewer than exhaustive search.
Lesson 4 of 5

Sampling: temperature, top-k and top-p

Greedy and beam search are deterministic: the same input always gives the same output. For open ended generation such as stories or chat, that leads to bland and repetitive text. Holtzman et al. (2020) showed that beam search on open ended text often loops, repeating the same phrase. Sampling decoders bring in some randomness instead.

Pure sampling
yt ~ P(· | y<t, x)
Draw the next word at random from the full distribution.
TemperatureExam favourite
PT(w) = exp(sw ÷ T)∑w′ exp(sw′ ÷ T)
T < 1 sharpens (more greedy), T > 1 flattens (more diverse). T → 0 is greedy.
Top-kExam favourite
sample only from the k most probable words, renormalised
A fixed number of candidates.
Top-p (nucleus)Exam favourite
sample from the smallest set Vp with ∑w ∈ Vp P(w) ≥ p
The number of candidates adapts to how sure the model is.
Top-p with p = 0.9 on "the students opened their ___"
  1. Probabilities at T = 1: books 0.4684, laptops 0.2841, exams 0.1723, minds 0.0634, zoo 0.0086, bananas 0.0032.
  2. Running sums: 0.4684, 0.7525, 0.9248. The third word passes 0.9.
  3. Nucleus = {books, laptops, exams}
  4. Renormalised: books 0.5065, laptops 0.3072, exams 0.1863. "zoo" and "bananas" can no longer be drawn.

Remember
This is the quality against diversity trade off. Lower T, smaller k or smaller p give safer and more repetitive text. Higher values give more varied and riskier text.
Lesson 5 of 5

Choosing a decoder

The decoder is chosen at inference time and does not change the trained model. The same NMT network can be decoded greedily for speed, with beam search for quality, or with sampling for variety.

Real world scenario: one model, three products

A company trains one model that writes product descriptions. For the legal summary on the checkout page they use beam search with k = 4: the most probable, stable wording every time. For marketing emails they use top-p = 0.9 at T = 0.8, so every customer gets a slightly different but sensible text. For a brainstorming tool they raise T to 1.2 and accept some odd ideas in exchange for more surprising ones.

Temperature by hand
  1. Two words with scores 2 and 1.
  2. T = 1: e² ÷ (e² + e¹) = 7.389 ÷ 10.107 = 0.731.
  3. T = 0.5: scores become 4 and 2, so e⁴ ÷ (e⁴ + e²) = 0.881.
  4. T = 2: scores 1 and 0.5, weight 0.622. Lower T sharpens, higher T flattens
One idea, four depths

To write a sentence, the model picks one word at a time. It can always pick the top word, keep a few good options open (beam search), or pick at random from the likely words to be more creative.

Greedy takes argmax each step. Beam search keeps the top k hypotheses by summed log probability and normalises by length at the end. Sampling uses temperature, top-k or top-p to trade quality for diversity.

Beam search approximates the MAP sequence. Its search error can be small while the MAP itself is poor: Stahlberg and Byrne (2019) found the exact MAP translation is often the empty sentence, which is why length normalisation and moderate beams work best.

Modern LLMs combine temperature with top-p or min-p sampling, use repetition penalties, and add speculative decoding (a small draft model proposes tokens that the large model verifies) to speed up generation without changing the output distribution.
