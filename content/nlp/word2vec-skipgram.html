Lesson 1 of 4

The word2vec idea: predict the neighbours

Word2vec (Mikolov and others, 2013) learns word vectors by a simple game. Walk through a long text. At each position t there is a centre word c and outside words o within a window. Use the similarity of the vectors of c and o to compute the probability of o given c, and keep adjusting the vectors to make the true neighbours more probable.

Likelihood of the whole text
L(θ) = ∏t=1T ∏−m ≤ j ≤ m, j ≠ 0 P(wt+j | wt, θ)
θ is every word vector. The product runs over every position and every neighbour in the window.
Objective to minimiseExam favourite
J(θ) = −1T ∑t ∑j log P(wt+j | wt, θ)
The average negative log likelihood.
Three transformations of the likelihood, from the slide
  1. Take the log: products become sums and tiny numbers no longer underflow.
  2. Put a minus sign in front: optimisers minimise, so we minimise the negative log likelihood.
  3. Divide by T: the average does not grow with the length of the corpus.
  4. Minimising J is the same as maximising the likelihood L
Real world scenario: guessing a missing word

On a phone keyboard, after "I will call you" the words "later" and "tomorrow" are offered. A model that has seen millions of sentences learns that both often sit in the same places, so their vectors end up close. Word2vec is that guessing game played on a whole corpus, keeping the vectors and throwing away the guesses.

Lesson 2 of 4

The prediction function: dot, exponentiate, normalise

Each word has two vectors: vw when it is the centre word and uw when it is an outside word. The lecture stresses that these are not two meanings of the word but two roles it can play. The probability of an outside word is a softmax over dot products.

Skip-gram prediction functionExam favourite
P(o | c) = exp(uo · vc)∑w ∈ V exp(uw · vc)
Step 1 dot product (similarity), step 2 exp (always positive), step 3 divide by the sum (a distribution).
Softmax
softmax(x)i = exp(xi)∑j exp(xj)
"Max" because large scores dominate, "soft" because small ones still get some probability.
StepWhat it doesWhy
1. Dot product uo·vcScores how compatible the two words arebanking and crises score high, banking and skillet low
2. ExponentiateMakes every score positiveProbabilities cannot be negative, and big scores grow much bigger
3. NormaliseDivides by the sum over the vocabularyThe probabilities add to 1

In network terms the input is the one-hot vector of the centre word, which simply selects its row of the embedding matrix E. That vector h is compared by dot product with every output vector, giving one score per vocabulary word. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Training raises the probability of the true context words, and over time words that appear in similar contexts get similar vectors.

In the exam
Sigmoid or softmax? The lecture answer: sigmoid for a yes or no (binary) decision, softmax for picking one of many classes. Full word2vec picks one word out of V, so it uses softmax. Negative sampling turns it into many yes or no decisions, so it uses sigmoids.
Lesson 3 of 4

Training: the gradient and the notebook loop

The slide pseudo-code WORD2VEC-SKIPGRAM builds the vocabulary, initialises E and W randomly, generates the training pairs, and then for each epoch shuffles the pairs and runs one forward pass, one loss and one update per pair. The gradient has a beautiful form: what the model expected minus what it saw.

Gradient for the centre vectorExam favourite
∂J∂vc = −uo + ∑w ∈ V P(w | c) uw
Observed context vector against the expected context vector under the model.
In the notebook’s matrix form
error = p − y,   ∂J/∂h = W · error,   ∂J/∂W = h ⊗ error
y is the one-hot vector of the true context word. ⊗ is the outer product.
Stochastic gradient descent
θ ← θ − η ∇θ Jwindow(θ)
Update after each sampled window, not after the whole corpus.
Derive the centre gradient in three lines
  1. log P(o | c) = uo·vc − log ∑w exp(uw·vc).
  2. Differentiate the first term: uo. Differentiate the log sum: ∑w exp(uw·vc) uw ÷ ∑x exp(ux·vc) = ∑w P(w | c) uw.
  3. ∂(−log P(o | c)) ÷ ∂vc = −uo + ∑w P(w | c) uw
word2vec 1.ipynb, the training cell (full softmax)
np.random.seed(42)E = np.random.randn(V, d) * 0.1          # input (centre) vectors, one row per wordW = np.random.randn(d, V) * 0.1          # output weights, one column per worddef softmax(x):    x = x - np.max(x)    exp_x = np.exp(x)    return exp_x / np.sum(exp_x)for epoch in range(epochs):              # epochs = 500, learning_rate = 0.05, d = 3    np.random.shuffle(pairs)    for center, context in pairs:        h = E[center].copy()             # forward pass        scores = h @ W        probabilities = softmax(scores)        loss = -np.log(probabilities[context] + 1e-12)        error = probabilities.copy()     # backpropagation        error[context] -= 1        gradient_W = np.outer(h, error)        gradient_h = W @ error        W -= learning_rate * gradient_W        E[center] -= learning_rate * gradient_h

Remember
J depends on every window in the corpus, possibly billions. Computing the full gradient before one update would take forever, so all neural NLP models use stochastic or mini batch gradient descent.
Common mistake
With full softmax the error vector p − y has V entries, so every single update touches all V output vectors. That is the cost negative sampling removes.

Skip-gram, CBOW and why two vectors

Word2vec comes in two model variants and several loss functions. Each word keeps two vectors because that makes the optimisation easier (a word is rarely its own neighbour). At the end the two are usually averaged.

ModelPredictsLearns
Skip-gramthe context words from the centre wordvectors that capture co-occurrence patterns, good for rare words
CBOWthe centre word from the bag of context wordssimilar vectors, faster to train
GloVe (chapter 2.4)log co-occurrence countsvectors that encode global count statistics
CBOW input
h = 12m ∑−m ≤ j ≤ m, j ≠ 0 vwt+j
Average the context vectors, then predict the centre word with the same softmax.
Parameters of skip-gram with full softmaxExam favourite
|E| + |W| = V·d + d·V = 2Vd
V = 10,000 and d = 300 give 6,000,000 numbers.
LossCost per pairNote
Naive softmaxV dot productsSimple but expensive with many output classes
Hierarchical softmaxabout log₂ VA binary tree over the vocabulary
Negative sampling1 + K sigmoidsThe standard choice, chapter 2.3
One idea, four depths

Skip-gram looks at one word and guesses its neighbours. CBOW looks at the neighbours and guesses the missing word, like a fill in the blank exercise.

Both are shallow networks: an embedding lookup, a dot product layer and a softmax. Skip-gram makes 2m training examples per position, CBOW one, so skip-gram sees rare words more often.

The softmax normaliser costs O(V) per example. Hierarchical softmax replaces it with a path of log₂ V binary decisions in a Huffman tree, and negative sampling with a noise contrastive objective of K + 1 terms.

Static embeddings give one vector per word type. Their ideas survive inside modern models: the input embedding table of a transformer is E, the output projection is W, and many models tie the two, which is exactly the "average the two vectors" observation made trainable.
