Lesson 1 of 5

Finite state automata

Before probabilities come in, here is the machine underneath. A finite state automaton (FSA) reads a string one symbol at a time. It sits in one state, follows the arrow labelled with the symbol it reads, and at the end it says yes or no. Lift buttons, vending machines and traffic lights all work this way: a small set of situations and fixed rules for moving between them.

The FSA five tupleExam favourite
(Q, Σ, q₀, δ, F)
Q is the set of states, Σ the alphabet of symbols, q₀ the start state, δ the transition function (state, symbol) → state, and F the final states, drawn as double circles.

The slide machine has Q = {q0, q1, q2, q3}, Σ = {c, a, t, r}, F = {q3} and four arrows: q0 c q1, q1 a q2, q2 t q3 and q2 r q3. A string is accepted only if two things hold: every symbol has an arrow to follow, and the machine ends in a final state.

Sheep talk

The second slide machine accepts baa!, baaa!, baaaa! and so on: b, then at least two a's, then !. A self loop on q3 eats any number of extra a's. The regular expression is /baa+!/. Kleene's theorem says every regular expression has an equivalent FSA and the other way round.

Going deeper: Why is the loop on q3 and not on q2?

A student asked this. Many machines accept the same language. A loop on any state reached after the first two a's gives /baa+!/. A loop on q1 would be wrong, since it would let "ba!" through with only one a. For every regular language there is one smallest deterministic machine, the minimal DFA.

Remember
How does this help with language? Finite state machines run tokenisers, regular expression taggers, spelling checkers and morphology (leaves maps to leaf + N + PL). Put probabilities on the arrows and you get a Markov chain, the n-gram model of step 4. Let each state also emit a word and you get the HMM.
Lesson 2 of 5

The hidden Markov model

In tagging we see the words but not the tags. A hidden Markov model (HMM) explains the words with a hidden walk over tags. Picture a person in another room who flips between moods you cannot see, and each mood makes them shout certain words. You hear only the words and must guess the moods.

The HMM five tupleExam favourite
(Q, A, O, B, π)
Q the tag states, A the transition table P(tag | previous tag), O the vocabulary of words, B the emission table P(word | tag), π the start probabilities.
Joint probability of words and tagsExam favourite
P(W, T) = π(t₁) bt₁(w₁) ∏i=2n ati−1ti bti(wi)
One start, n emissions and n − 1 transitions: 2n factors in all. "We multiply the transition probability with the emission probability" at every position.
The two assumptions that make the formula short
  1. Markov assumption: a tag depends only on the tag before it. P(ti | t1 … ti−1) ≈ P(ti | ti−1).
  2. Output independence: a word depends only on its own tag. P(wi | all tags and other words) ≈ P(wi | ti).
  3. Start from the exact chain rule P(W, T) = P(T) P(W | T).
  4. The first assumption turns P(T) into π(t₁) × a product of tag bigrams.
  5. The second turns P(W | T) into a product of emissions bti(wi). Multiply the two and regroup position by position.

British Left Waffles written out

For the reading N N V P N N the twelve factors are:

P(N) P(British | N) · P(N | N) P(Left | N) · P(V | N) P(Waffles | V) · P(P | V) P(on | P) · P(N | P) P(Falkland | N) · P(N | N) P(Islands | N)

A tag bigram alone would miss the words. A word bigram alone would miss the tags. The HMM multiplies both because it scores words and tags together.

Two kinds of machine

MachineOutput is attached toIn the HMM
Mealyeach arrow (transition)not used
Mooreeach stateyes: a tag state emits a word

The two are equally powerful. The professor's summary: an HMM is essentially the finite state automaton with probabilities, a probabilistic Moore machine. It starts in state i with probability πi, moves from i to j with P(j | i) and emits word o from state j with P(o | j).

Common mistake
Do not confuse an HMM with a bigram word model. In the bigram model the thing you condition on, the previous word, is visible. In the HMM it is a hidden tag, so to find tags you must search (Viterbi) or sum (forward) over them.

Is this HMM valid?

Two rules, checked row by row. Every state's outgoing transitions sum to 1. Every state's emissions sum to 1. The start probabilities sum to 1 as well.

The slide HMM has two tags, A (adjective) and N (noun), and four words: crazy, killer, clown and problem.

The class exercise, one line at a time
  1. From A: 1/3 + 2/3 = 1.
  2. From N: 1/10 + 9/10 = 1.
  3. A emits only crazy, with probability 1. So bA(killer) = 0, "not because killer is a noun, but because A already spends all its probability on crazy".
  4. N emits killer, clown and problem at 1/3 each, which is 1. So bN(crazy) = 0.
  5. Start: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Every row sums to 1, so the HMM is valid.
In the exam
Exam questions often hide one cell. Fill it with 1 minus the rest of its row (problem G2). The rows are what sum to 1, never the columns: P(N | A) + P(N | N) = 2/3 + 9/10 is not 1 and does not need to be.

Problem G5 by hand
  1. Corpus: they/N can/M fish/V · they/N fish/V · fish/N can/M swim/V · they/N can/V fish/N · swim/V.
  2. Start tags: N four times, V once. π = (N 4/5, V 1/5).
  3. Tag bigrams: N M 2, N V 2, M V 2, V N 1. Out of N there are 4, so a(N → M) = a(N → V) = 1/2. a(M → V) = 1 and a(V → N) = 1.
  4. Emissions: N is they 3 and fish 2 (5 in all), M is can 2, V is fish 2, swim 2 and can 1 (5 in all).
  5. bN: they 3/5, fish 2/5. bM(can) = 1. bV: fish 2/5, swim 2/5, can 1/5.
  6. Read the zeros: N never followed N, so "fish fish" as N N gets probability 0. Real taggers smooth A and B, exactly as in chapter 4.3.
Going deeper: Training without tags

If you have only raw text, the Baum-Welch algorithm (an instance of expectation maximisation) guesses tag counts from the forward and backward probabilities, re-estimates A and B from those soft counts, and repeats. Each round cannot lower the likelihood of the text, but it can stop at a poor local optimum, and the hidden states it finds need not match linguists' tags.

Common mistake
Two different killer clown HMMs appear in the deck. Slides 23 and 24 give P(killer clown, N N) = 1/2 × 1/3 × 9/10 × 1/3 = 0.05. Slide 27 gives 0.045. Same winner, different number. Always use the tables the question gives you (problem G4).
Real world scenario: reading a shipping label

A warehouse scanner reads "ORANGE BOX 12". Is ORANGE a colour (adjective) or a fruit (noun)? A small HMM trained on past labels scores both tag paths. Adjective then noun then number is common on labels, noun noun number is rarer, so the scanner files it under colour. With two or three words, scoring every path is instant. With a 25 word sentence and 45 tags it would never finish, which is the subject of the next chapter.

One idea, four depths

Hidden tags walk from one to the next, and each tag says a word. Multiply the chance of each step and each word, and pick the walk with the biggest result.

An HMM is (Q, A, O, B, π). P(W, T) is a product of 2n factors. Train by counting. Check that each row sums to 1. For tiny inputs, enumerate all qn sequences.

The HMM is a generative model of P(W, T) with a first order Markov chain over hidden states and conditionally independent emissions. Supervised MLE is closed form. Unsupervised training uses Baum-Welch. Inference is dynamic programming over the trellis.

HMMs are the simplest dynamic Bayesian networks. Extensions include higher order HMMs (TnT), factorial and hierarchical HMMs, and neural HMMs where emissions come from a network. Linear chain CRFs are their discriminative counterpart.
