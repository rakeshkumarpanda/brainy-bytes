Lesson 1 of 5

A model is a guess with knobs

A dog gets patted and wags its tail. The lecture records three dogs: pats 1, 2 and 4 gave wags 2, 4 and 5. We want a rule that predicts wags from pats. The simplest rule is a straight line with two knobs.

Linear model
wags(pats) = w · pats + b
w is the weight (how steep), b is the bias (where the line starts at pats = 0).

A first random guess on the slide was w = 0.25 and b = 0.68. It predicts 0.93, 1.18 and 1.68, far below the real 2, 4 and 5. A second guess, w = 1.00 and b = 1.37, predicts 2.37, 3.37 and 5.37, much closer. Learning is the job of turning a bad guess into a good one automatically.

Real world scenario: a taxi fare app

A taxi app predicts the fare from distance. Fare = rate × km + base fare. The rate is w and the base fare is b. The app learns both from thousands of past trips, exactly the way this lesson learns w and b from three dogs.

One idea, four depths

A model is a rule with dials. You turn the dials until the rule gives answers close to the real ones. Here the dials are w and b.

Linear regression fits ŷ = wx + b by minimising squared error. With one input it has a closed form, but we learn it by gradient descent because that method also works for neural networks with millions of weights.

The squared error cost is a convex quadratic in (w, b), so it has one minimum. Its Hessian, 2 times the matrix of ∑x², ∑x and n, sets the curvature and with it the largest safe learning rate.

Neural language models are the same story at scale: a parametric function, a differentiable loss (cross entropy instead of squared error) and stochastic gradient methods. Research studies why non-convex losses of huge networks are still easy to optimise in practice.

Lesson 2 of 5

A cost function scores a guess

To improve a guess we need one number that says how bad it is. The lecture uses the squared error: subtract the target from the prediction, square it, and add up over all examples.

Squared error costExam favourite
C(w, b) = ∑i (wxi + b − yi)²
Squaring makes every error positive and punishes big errors much more than small ones.
Mean squared error
MSE = 1n ∑ (ŷi − yi)²
The same cost divided by the number of examples, so it does not grow with the data size.
Worked example: the cost of w = 1.00, b = 1.37
  1. Predictions: 1 × 1 + 1.37 = 2.37, then 3.37, then 5.37.
  2. Errors: 2.37 − 2 = 0.37, 3.37 − 4 = −0.63, 5.37 − 5 = 0.37.
  3. Squares: 0.1369, 0.3969, 0.1369.
  4. C = 0.1369 + 0.3969 + 0.1369 = 0.6707, the 0.67 on the slide

The cost is a function of the knobs w and b, not of the data. The data is fixed. That is why we can draw it as a curve or a surface over the knobs.
Lesson 3 of 5

Rolling downhill: gradient descent

The lecture first strips the network to its smallest form: NN() = b, no input at all, with target 4. The cost is (b − 4)², a U shaped parabola with its bottom at b = 4. We pretend we cannot see the answer and only feel the slope under our feet.

Slope of the teaching cost
d C ÷ db = 2(b − 4)
Positive slope: move left. Negative slope: move right. Zero: you are at the bottom.
Gradient descent updateExam favourite
b ← b − η · dCdb
η is the learning rate, the fraction of the slope we step by.
Worked example: two steps from b = 0 with η = 0.1
  1. Slope at 0 is 2(0 − 4) = −8. New b = 0 − 0.1 × (−8) = 0.8.
  2. Slope at 0.8 is 2(0.8 − 4) = −6.4. New b = 0.8 + 0.64 = 1.44.
  3. The distance to 4 went 4, 3.2, 2.56: each step multiplies it by 1 − 2η = 0.8.
  4. Distance after n steps = 4 × 0.8n, so it shrinks but never jumps past the target

Common mistake
A negative slope does not by itself promise a lower cost after the step. If η is too large the step overshoots and the cost can go up. The lecture asked exactly this.
In the exam
Expect "if ∂C/∂w is negative, does w increase or decrease after the update?" It increases, because w − η × (negative) adds a positive amount.
Lesson 4 of 5

Two knobs: partial derivatives and the cost bowl

With both w and b free, the cost is a surface over a flat plane of (w, b) pairs. The lecture plotted it in 3D: it is a bowl with one lowest point. To walk downhill we need the slope in each direction separately, which is what a partial derivative is.

Partial derivatives of the squared errorExam favourite
∂C∂w = ∑ 2(wx + b − y) · x    ∂C∂b = ∑ 2(wx + b − y)
Hold the other knob fixed and take the ordinary derivative.
Update both at once
w ← w − η ∂C/∂w,   b ← b − η ∂C/∂b
The vector (∂C/∂w, ∂C/∂b) is the gradient. It points uphill, so we subtract it.
Worked example: the gradient at w = 1, b = 1.37
  1. Errors are 0.37, −0.63, 0.37 at x = 1, 2, 4.
  2. ∂C/∂w = 2(0.37 × 1 − 0.63 × 2 + 0.37 × 4) = 2 × 0.59 = 1.18.
  3. ∂C/∂b = 2(0.37 − 0.63 + 0.37) = 0.22.
  4. With η = 0.01: w = 1 − 0.0118 = 0.9882 and b = 1.37 − 0.0022 = 1.3678
Going deeper: why this bowl has a speed limit

The cost is quadratic, so its curvature is a constant matrix, the Hessian H = 2 {HGEN} = {HESS} for this data. Its eigenvalues are 46.80 and 1.196. Gradient descent is stable only when η < 2 ÷ λmax = 0.0427. The ratio 46.80 ÷ 1.196 ≈ 39 is the condition number: the bowl is a long narrow valley, which is why plain gradient descent zig-zags and why methods with momentum or per parameter rates (Adam) were invented.

Remember
Derivatives matter because they are the exact slope. No guessing is needed, and the same rule works for models with billions of knobs.
Lesson 5 of 5

The exam question: a tanh model by the chain rule

In the revision class the model was changed to ŷ = tanh(wx + b) with one training example x = 1, y = 0.35 and loss L = (ŷ − y)². The task was to write ∂L/∂w. The chain rule breaks it into three small derivatives you already know.

Chain rule for one neuronExam favourite
∂L∂w = ∂L∂ŷ · ∂ŷ∂z · ∂z∂w = 2(ŷ − y) · (1 − ŷ²) · x
z = wx + b. The derivative of tanh z is 1 − tanh² z.
Worked example with w = 0.5, b = 0.1
  1. z = 0.5 × 1 + 0.1 = 0.6, so ŷ = tanh 0.6 = 0.53705.
  2. ∂L/∂ŷ = 2(0.53705 − 0.35) = 0.37410.
  3. ∂ŷ/∂z = 1 − 0.53705² = 0.71158, and ∂z/∂w = x = 1.
  4. ∂L/∂w = 0.37410 × 0.71158 × 1 = 0.2662. Positive, so the update lowers w
In the exam
Formulas for derivatives are given in the exam if you need them. If none is given, the question can be answered without one. This was said in class.
