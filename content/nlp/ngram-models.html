Lesson 1 of 4

Why predict the next word?

Say "He put the coffee" to a room of people and pause. Almost everyone finishes with "on the table" or "on the desk". Nobody says "on the elephant". People carry a sense of which words are likely next, and that sense is shared. A language model is a program with the same skill: it gives every possible next word a probability.

The lecture gave several uses. In speech recognition, "I will be back soonish" and "I will be bassoon dish" sound almost the same, and the language model picks the likely word string. Spelling correction, machine translation, phone keyboard suggestions and every large language model work the same way. The professor's advice was to master the simple n-gram first: RNNs, LSTMs and Transformers all predict the next token from a history, so they are easy to follow once n-grams are clear.

True probability and estimated probability

How likely is "jelly" after "peanut butter and"? We cannot open a speaker's mind and read the true probability P. Instead we take a corpus of millions of words, assume it is a fair sample of the language, and estimate. The estimate is written m, and the slide's definition is short: a language model is a probability distribution over sentences.

Conditional estimateExam favourite
m(jelly | peanut butter and) = C(peanut butter and jelly)C(peanut butter and)
Count the whole four word sequence, then divide by how often the three word history occurred.
Chain rule (exact)Exam favourite
P(w₁ … wn) = P(w₁) · P(w₂ | w₁) · P(w₃ | w₁ w₂) ⋯ P(wn | w₁ … wn−1)
No approximation yet. Every word is conditioned on everything before it.
Common mistake
A student first answered "count jelly and divide by the total number of words". That is P(jelly), the unigram probability, which ignores the history. The question asks for the conditional probability, which needs the joint count of the whole sequence.

Why count "put the coffee" and not just "coffee" when estimating P(coffee | put the)? Because P(x | y) = P(x, y) ÷ P(y). C(coffee) counts coffee anywhere, after "a", after "black", at the start of a sentence. Dividing it by C(put the) mixes two unrelated events and can even give a number above 1.

Counting a phrase in a big file
  1. Numerator: how many times does "put the coffee" occur? Denominator: how many times does "put the" occur?
  2. grep -c counts matching lines, not matches. A line with the phrase twice counts once, so use grep -o "put the coffee" file | wc -l.
  3. Add word boundaries, or "output theory" will match "put the".
  4. The scalable way is one pass that counts every n-gram into a table. That is what SRILM's ngram-count does, and any probability is then a look up.
Real world scenario: a phone keyboard

You type "see you" and the keyboard offers "tomorrow", "soon" and "later". It has counted, across millions of messages, which words follow "see you" and offers the three with the largest counts. It does not understand meetings or time. It only knows what usually comes next.

Lesson 2 of 4

The Markov assumption

The chain rule is exact but useless as it stands. To estimate P(table | He put the coffee on the) we would need that whole seven word history many times in the corpus, and long histories almost never repeat. The fix is to forget most of the past.

Markov assumptionExam favourite
P(wi | w₁ … wi−1) ≈ P(wi | wi−n+1 … wi−1)
The next word depends only on the last n − 1 words. An n-gram has n words: n − 1 of context and one predicted.
Bigram model
P(w₁ … wn) ≈ ∏i P(wi | wi−1)
A bigram model is a first order Markov chain over words. A trigram model is second order.

In the exam
In class the answer to "how many context words does a 5-gram have?" came out garbled. The rule is simple: n words in total, the last is predicted, so a 5-gram has 4 context words.

The model as a machine

A bigram model can be drawn as a graph. Each word is a state, each arrow carries P(next | current), and a start distribution π says where sentences begin. The slide used three states: He, cool and is.

Probability of "is He cool" from the chain
  1. Start in "is": π(is) = 0.2.
  2. Follow is → He: P(He | is) = 0.5.
  3. Follow He → cool: P(cool | He) = 0.3.
  4. P(is He cool) = 0.2 × 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.03
  5. Sanity check: the arrows leaving each state add to 1. He: 0.1 + 0.3 + 0.6, cool: 0.2 + 0 + 0.8, is: 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.
Going deeper: Is a self loop allowed?

A student objected that the loop from He back to He is a mistake, as it would be in a compiler diagram. The professor was right to keep it. Self loops are legal in every kind of finite state machine, deterministic or not, weighted or not. Language repeats ("very very good"), so a word can follow itself. The chain has one real gap: it has no end state, so it only scores strings of a fixed length. Adding a state for </s> turns it into a model of whole sentences.

Lesson 3 of 4

Counting: the maximum likelihood estimate

Every probability in an n-gram model is a ratio of two counts. The slide described the bigram case as "count of the bigram, normalised by the sum of all bigrams that share the same first word", and that sum is just the count of the first word.

Bigram MLEExam favourite
P(wn | wn−1) = C(wn−1 wn)C(wn−1)
Because every occurrence of a word is followed by exactly one token, possibly </s>, the bigram counts in a row add up to the count of the history.
General n-gram MLE
P(wn | wn−N+1 … wn−1) = C(wn−N+1 … wn−1 wn)C(wn−N+1 … wn−1)
Pad each sentence with n − 1 start tags so the first word also has a full history.
Unigram MLE
P(w) = C(w)N
N counts every token the model must predict: the words and one </s> per sentence, never <s>.

Why 22, and not 17 or 27?

This was the most asked question of the week. The rule used in class, and by SRILM: <s> is only context. It conditions the first word, as in P(Sam | <s>), but it is never predicted and never counted in N. </s> is a real token that the model predicts, as in P(</s> | like). So N = 17 words + 5 end tags = 22.

  • With 17 the model can never end a sentence, so "Sam I" and "Sam I am" do not compete for the same probability and the total over all sentences exceeds 1.
  • With 27 the token <s> takes 5/27 of the mass but is never predicted, so the real tokens add to only 22/27.
  • With 22 the unigram values 5/22, 5/22, 5/22, 3/22, 2/22, 2/22 add to exactly 1.

The professor's short reason: if you know the end tag, you know a start follows, so counting both adds a redundant event.

Proof that the counting estimate is the best one (MLE)
  1. Fix a history h seen C(h) times, followed by word w C(h w) times. Let θw = P(w | h), with Σ θw = 1.
  2. The part of the training log likelihood that depends on h is L(θ) = Σw C(h w) log θw.
  3. Add a Lagrange multiplier μ for the constraint and set the derivative to 0: C(h w) ÷ θw − μ = 0, so θw = C(h w) ÷ μ.
  4. The constraint gives Σ C(h w) ÷ μ = C(h) ÷ μ = 1, so μ = C(h).
  5. θw = C(h w) ÷ C(h), the relative frequency
  6. Check on the row of I: am 2, like 2, do 1, so 2/5 + 2/5 + 1/5 = 1.
Lesson 4 of 4

The activity: which sentence is better?

The questions sheet asked: under a bigram model, which sentence gets the higher probability, S1 = Sam I do I like or S2 = Sam I am? Then the same with a unigram model. "Better" here means "higher probability under the model". That is a sensible stand in for human preference: "John went to London in the late afternoon" and "John went in the late afternoon to London" are both grammatical, yet almost everyone votes for the first. Grammar cannot decide, probability can.

Question 1(a): bigram probability of S1
  1. Pad: <s> Sam I do I like </s>. That is 5 words and one </s>, so 6 factors.
  2. P(Sam | <s>) = 3/5, P(I | Sam) = 3/5, P(do | I) = 1/5, P(I | do) = 1/2, P(like | I) = 2/5, P(</s> | like) = 2/3.
  3. P(S1) = 3/5 × 3/5 × 1/5 × 1/2 × 2/5 × 2/3 = 6/625 = 0.0096
  4. log₁₀ P = −0.22185 − 0.22185 − 0.69897 − 0.30103 − 0.39794 − 0.17609 = −2.01773.
  5. PP = 102.01773/6 = 2.16914. PP1 divides by 5 words only: 2.53248.
Question 1(b): bigram probability of S2
  1. Pad: <s> Sam I am </s>, 4 factors.
  2. P(Sam | <s>) = 3/5, P(I | Sam) = 3/5, P(am | I) = 2/5, P(</s> | am) = 1/2.
  3. P(S2) = 9/125 = 0.072, log₁₀ P = −1.14267, PP = 1.93049
  4. S2 is about 7.5 times more probable than S1 and also wins per word, so the bigram model prefers Sam I am.
Question 2: the unigram model
  1. Unigram factors are plain C(w) ÷ 22, no condition: P(Sam) = P(I) = P(</s>) = 5/22, P(like) = 3/22, P(do) = 2/22.
  2. S1: 5/22 × 5/22 × 2/22 × 5/22 × 3/22 × 5/22 = 3.3075 × 10⁻⁵, log₁₀ −4.48050, PP 5.5815, PP1 7.8723.
  3. S2: 5/22 × 5/22 × 2/22 × 5/22 = 1.06721 × 10⁻³, log₁₀ −2.97175, PP 5.5327, PP1 9.7855.
  4. S2 is about 32 times more probable. Per word the two almost tie, because a unigram model ignores order and sees only frequencies.
Common mistake
Three errors in the answer sheet are worth knowing. It labels unigram factors as P(Sam | <s>), but a unigram has no condition. Its product for S1 lists three numbers and gives 3.25 × 10⁻⁵, which comes from rounding 5/22 to 0.227. The exact value is 3.3075 × 10⁻⁵. And many students used N = 17 or 27, or conditioned the unigram on the previous word.
In the exam
PP divides the total surprise by words plus </s>, PP1 by words only, so PP1 is always the larger. The N in PP = P−1/N is the length of the sentence being scored, 6 for S1, not the 22 tokens of the training corpus. The professor saw that mistake in many submissions.
One idea, four depths

Count how often each word follows each other word in some text, and use those counts to guess the next word.

Under the Markov assumption P(sentence) is a product of n-gram factors, each C(history word) ÷ C(history). The sentence tags make it a proper distribution over sentences of every length.

The MLE maximises training likelihood (proof by Lagrange multipliers). An n-gram model is an order n − 1 Markov chain with Vn−1(V − 1) free parameters, which is why data sparsity grows so fast with n.

Neural language models keep the chain rule but drop the Markov cut off. Research compares how much of their gain comes from longer context against better generalisation across similar words, and studies n-gram statistics inside trained Transformers.
