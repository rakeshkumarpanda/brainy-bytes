What a chunk is

"Fast" is a word and "food" is a word, but "fast food" is one unit of meaning. So are "heavy rain", "the yellow dog" and "cats and dogs". Chunking finds these groups in a sentence. It is called shallow parsing because it finds flat, non overlapping pieces instead of a full tree.

ChunkConstituent
Shapeflat, never nestednested inside a full tree
Example[the yellow dog][[the yellow dog] [in [the park]]]
Defined byany useful group, often base noun and verb phrasesgrammar: noun phrase, verb phrase, adjective phrase
Costfast and robust, a tagging problemslower, needs a parser (chapter 5.5)

In class: "A constituent is more grammatical, a syntactic category like a noun phrase. A chunk is any meaningful group of units that we may not always annotate grammatically."

A rule based chunker

The quickest chunker is a regular expression over POS tags. "An optional determiner, any number of adjectives, then one or more nouns" catches most base noun phrases. NLTK writes it as NP: {<DT>?<JJ>*<NN.*>+}.

Remember
This is why POS tagging comes first. The chunker never looks at the words, only at the tags, so tagging errors flow straight into chunking errors.
Lesson 2 of 3

IOB: spans as tags

Finding spans sounds like a new problem, but a small trick turns it into tagging, so every tagger from the last three chapters can do it. Give each word one of three kinds of label.

IOB tagsExam favourite
B-X begins a chunk of type X   I-X is inside it   O is outside every chunk
With k chunk types there are 2k + 1 labels. The input can be the words and their POS tags.

"We saw the yellow dog ." becomes We/B-NP saw/B-VP the/B-NP yellow/I-NP dog/I-NP ./O, which is [NP We] [VP saw] [NP the yellow dog] in brackets. As the professor put it, "everything remains the same" as POS tagging: same models, new tag set.

Problem H4, both directions
  1. Encode [NP The quick brown fox] [VP jumped] over [NP the lazy dog].
  2. The/B-NP quick/I-NP brown/I-NP fox/I-NP jumped/B-VP over/O the/B-NP lazy/I-NP dog/I-NP.
  3. over is outside every chunk, so it gets O. Each chunk starts with B and continues with I of the same type.
  4. Decode Sidharth/B-PER Ranjan/I-PER teaches/O at/O IIT/B-ORG Jodhpur/I-ORG.
  5. A span is one B-X plus the I-X tags that follow it: [Sidharth Ranjan] PER and [IIT Jodhpur] ORG.
Remember
Why B at all? Two chunks of the same type can sit side by side: "gave [the girl] [a book]". With only I and O, the girl a book would merge into one span. B marks where the second one starts.
Going deeper: IOB1, IOB2 and BIOES

IOB2, used above, puts B on the first word of every chunk. The older IOB1 (Ramshaw and Marcus 1995) uses B only when a chunk directly follows another of the same type. BIOES, also called BILOU, adds E for the last word and S for a single word chunk, giving 4k + 1 labels. The extra labels give the model more precise targets and often help slightly for named entities. Ill formed sequences such as O then I-X are usually repaired by reading the I-X as B-X.

Lesson 3 of 3

Named entity tagging

Named entity recognition (NER) finds names of people, organisations, places, dates and amounts. It is chunking with entity types instead of phrase types.

Reading the slide example
  1. United/B-ORG cancelled/O the/O flight/O from/O San/B-LOC Francisco/I-LOC.
  2. A span starts at each B- and continues through the following I- tags of the same type.
  3. Spans: [United] ORG and [San Francisco] LOC. Everything tagged O is outside any entity.
TypeExamples
PER (person)Sidharth Ranjan, Marie Curie
ORG (organisation)United, IIT Jodhpur, the World Health Organization
LOC or GPE (place)San Francisco, the Falkland Islands, India
DATE, MONEY, PERCENTnext Tuesday, 500 rupees, 12%

Models

The same families as POS tagging. Features matter even more: a capital letter, a title such as Dr., a word shape such as Xxxx, a gazetteer list of known cities, and the neighbouring words. The perceptron (Collins 2002) learns from its errors with these features. A conditional random field (CRF) normalises over the whole tag sequence, so it can learn that I-LOC never follows B-PER. Neural taggers, BiLSTM with a CRF layer or a fine tuned BERT, lead today.

Entity level F1Exam favourite
P = correct spanspredicted spans   R = correct spansgold spans   F1 = 2PRP + R
A predicted span counts only if its start, end and type all match. Token accuracy is misleading because most tokens are O.
Real world scenario: a bank reading cheque requests

A bank's email system receives "Please transfer 25,000 rupees to Asha Verma at Kotak Mahindra Bank by Friday". An NER tagger marks MONEY, PER, ORG and DATE spans, and a form is filled in for a human to approve. A missed boundary, such as tagging only "Kotak" as ORG, would count as wrong under entity level scoring, which is exactly the strictness the bank needs.

One idea, four depths

Mark groups of words that belong together, and names of people and places, by giving every word a small label: begin, inside or outside.

IOB turns span finding into tagging with 2k + 1 labels. Rule chunkers use regular expressions over POS tags. NER uses the same taggers with richer features and is scored with entity level precision, recall and F1.

Linear chain CRFs model P(Y | X) ∝ exp(Σ θ · f(yi−1, yi, X, i)) and decode with Viterbi. They avoid the label bias of left to right classifiers (MEMMs). Encoding choice (IOB2 or BIOES) changes what the transition weights can express.

Nested NER (an organisation inside a location name) breaks flat IOB and uses span based or hypergraph models. Low resource NER uses cross lingual transfer, and large language models can extract entities from instructions with few or no examples.
