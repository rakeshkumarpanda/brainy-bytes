Constituents and phrase structure

A constituent, or phrase, is a group of words that acts as one unit. All of these are noun phrases, and each can sit before a verb: "Harry the Horse", "the Broadway coppers", "they", "a high class spot such as Mindy's", "three parties from Brooklyn". Phrase structure nests these units into a hierarchy: N + N gives the NP "interest rates", V + NP gives the VP "raises interest rates", and NP + VP gives a sentence S.

Bracket notationExam favourite
(S (NP (N Fed)) (VP (V raises) (NP (N interest) (N rates))))
Each opening bracket starts a node and names its label. Treebanks store trees this way, on one line, called a linearised tree.

Every phrase type contains a word of its own kind, its head: a VP contains a verb (VB*), an NP a noun (NN*), an ADJP an adjective (JJ*), a PP a preposition (IN).

How do we know something is a constituent?

TestWhat you do"about drugs" in "John talked to the children about drugs"
MovementMove the group as a blockJohn talked [about drugs] [to the children]: fine
Fragment answerUse it alone as an answerWhat did John talk about? About drugs.
SubstitutionReplace it with a pro form (it, there, did so)John talked to the children about it.
CoordinationJoin it with a similar groupabout drugs and about school
Clefting"It was X that …"It was about drugs that John talked to the children.

"drugs to the children" fails every test: it cannot move as a block, cannot answer a question and has no pro form. It straddles two prepositional phrases, so it is not a constituent (problem H6). A star marks an ungrammatical sentence, such as *John talked drugs to the children about.

Problem H5: read a tree
  1. Tree: (S (NP (Det the) (N dog)) (VP (V chased) (NP (Det a) (N cat)))).
  2. Root S has two children, NP and VP. VP has V and a second NP.
  3. Constituents: [the dog] NP, [a cat] NP, [chased a cat] VP, [the dog chased a cat] S.
  4. Phrase nodes S, NP, VP, NP: 4. Part of speech nodes Det, N, V, Det, N: 5.
  5. 9 non terminals and 5 leaves

Context free grammars

CFG four tupleExam favourite
(N, Σ, R, S)
N non terminals (S, NP, VP …), Σ terminals (the words), R rules of the form A → β, and the start symbol S. A sentence is grammatical if S can be rewritten into it, and the rewriting history is its parse tree.
RuleReads as
S → NP VPa sentence is a noun phrase followed by a verb phrase
NP → Det Na noun phrase can be a determiner and a noun
VP → V NP | Va verb phrase is a verb with or without an object
Det → the, N → dog | cat, V → chased | sleptthe lexicon: which words fill each slot

This small grammar generates 2 × (2 × 2 + 2) = 12 sentences, from "the dog chased the cat" to "the cat slept". Some read well. Others, such as "the cat chased the cat", are grammatical but odd. Add the determiner a and the count jumps to 40. Grammar decides structure, not sense.

The Penn Treebank has about a million words of Wall Street Journal text with hand built trees. Reading the rules off those trees and counting them gives a probabilistic grammar, used in the last lesson.

Going deeper: Why not a finite state machine?

Sentences can nest without limit: "the rat the cat the dog chased ate died". Matching each subject with its verb needs a stack, which an FSA does not have. Formally, centre embedding produces patterns like anbn, which no FSA can recognise but a CFG can. In practice deep nesting is rare, which is why finite state tools still cover most local patterns. Parsing a CFG takes O(n³) with the CKY chart algorithm, the parsing cousin of Viterbi.

Lesson 3 of 4

Dependency structure

Instead of phrase nodes, dependency grammar links words directly. "You can get a lot done with binary relations among the words." Each link goes from a head (governor) to a dependent (modifier) and may carry a label.

In "The boy put the tortoise on the rug", put is the root and has three immediate children: boy, tortoise and on. The, the and rug are grandchildren, not children. Whether on or rug hangs from put is a design choice: older schemes and the slide let the preposition head the phrase, while Universal Dependencies makes content words heads, so put → rug (obl) and rug → on (case).

KindLabelsMeaning
Argumentnsubj, obj, iobjthe participants the verb needs: who paints, what is painted, to whom

Problem H7: The student reads books
  1. The root is the main verb: reads ← ROOT.
  2. student → head reads, nsubj. books → head reads, obj. The → head student, det.
  3. Each word has exactly one head. 4 words give 4 arcs including ROOT.
  4. No cycles, and no arcs cross when drawn above the sentence, so the tree is projective.
In the exam
A student asked whether this is a DAG, as in compilers. It is stricter: a tree. Every word has exactly one head, there are no cycles, and a sentence of n words has exactly n arcs counting the ROOT arc.

Lesson 4 of 4

Attachment ambiguity and PCFGs

Groucho Marx: "One morning I shot an elephant in my pajamas. How he got into my pajamas I'll never know." Two ambiguities sit here. Lexical: shot with a gun or with a camera. Attachment: the phrase "in my pajamas" attaches inside the verb phrase (I was wearing them) or inside the noun phrase (the elephant was).

Probabilistic CFGExam favourite
P(tree) = ∏rules A → β used P(β | A),   with Σβ P(β | A) = 1 for each A
Rule probabilities come from treebank counts: P(VP → V NP) = C(VP → V NP) ÷ C(VP). Choose the tree with the higher product.
Problem H9: who wore the pajamas?
  1. Both parses use S → NP VP, NP → Pro, VP → V NP, PP → P NP and NP → Det N twice.
  2. VP attachment adds VP → VP PP (0.3). NP attachment adds NP → NP PP (0.2).
  3. Shared factor: 1.0 × 0.3 × 0.7 × 1.0 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.0525.
  4. VP attachment = 0.0525 × 0.3 = 0.01575,   NP attachment = 0.0525 × 0.2 = 0.0105
  5. Ratio 1.5, so the PCFG says "I was in my pajamas". It never looked at the words elephant or pajamas.

How fast ambiguity grows

"I saw the man on the hill with a telescope." Each prepositional phrase can attach to the verb or to any noun before it, as long as no arcs cross. If "on the hill" attaches to man, "with a telescope" can go to saw, man or hill: 3 parses. If it attaches to saw, "with" can go to saw or hill but not to man, since that arc would cross: 2 parses. That is 5 in all (problem H10).

Catalan growth
Cn = (2n)!(n + 1)! n!   parses with k PPs = Ck+1: 2, 5, 14, 42, 132 …
Exponential growth, just like tag sequences. Chart parsers (CKY) share sub trees in O(n³) time.

Real world scenario: a legal contract search

A law firm searches contracts for "payment to the supplier within 30 days". A dependency parser finds that "within 30 days" attaches to payment, not to supplier, so the system extracts a deadline on the payment. Read with the wrong attachment, the clause would describe a supplier located within 30 days, which is nonsense. Getting attachment right is what turns text into the correct database row.

One idea, four depths

Sentences have structure. Words group into phrases, or each word hangs from another word. The same words can sometimes be grouped two ways, and that changes the meaning.

Constituents pass movement, substitution and fragment tests. CFGs generate trees. Dependency trees have one head per word and n arcs. A PCFG multiplies rule probabilities to pick between parses.

CKY parses a CFG in Chomsky normal form in O(n³ |G|). The inside algorithm sums over trees like the forward algorithm. Lexicalised PCFGs (Collins 1999) and Hindle and Rooth's attachment statistics fix the blindness of plain PCFGs to words. Transition based and graph based dependency parsers work in linear or cubic time.

Neural parsers with biaffine attention reach about 95% unlabelled attachment on English. Research covers non projective parsing, cross lingual parsing with Universal Dependencies, and probing whether large language models encode syntax trees in their hidden states.
