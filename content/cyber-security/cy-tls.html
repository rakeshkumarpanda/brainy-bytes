Lesson 1 of 10

Why we need PKI

Public key cryptography has a gap that the maths alone cannot fill. When your browser receives a public key from shop dot example, how does it know the key really belongs to that shop and not to someone in the middle of the network? The answer the internet settled on is the public key infrastructure, PKI for short. A small number of certificate authorities, CAs, check who owns a domain and then sign a certificate, a document that binds a name to a public key. Browsers and operating systems ship a list of about 150 trusted root authorities. The model goes back to the X.509 standard of 1988 and to Netscape’s SSL in the mid 1990s. It has failed in public several times. In 2011 the Dutch authority DigiNotar was hacked, and a fake certificate for Google was used to spy on users in Iran. DigiNotar was removed from every browser and went bankrupt within weeks. Those failures led to the defences in this chapter: Certificate Transparency logs, shorter certificate lifetimes, and TLS 1.3, which hides certificates and removes old weak options.

Source: RFC 5280 (X.509 profile), RFC 6962 and RFC 9162 (Certificate Transparency), RFC 8446 (TLS 1.3), RFC 8448 (example handshakes), RFC 8555 (ACME), RFC 6797 (HSTS), RFC 8996 (deprecating TLS 1.0 and 1.1), CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements and ballot SC-081 (2025).

What it is
PKI (public key infrastructure) is the system of certificate authorities, certificates, root stores and revocation that ties public keys to names such as domain names.
Why we need it
Without it, a man in the middle could hand you his own public key and read everything. Encryption without authentication protects only against passive listeners.
How it works
A CA checks that you control a domain, then signs a certificate containing your name and public key. Browsers trust a set of root CAs and verify a chain of signatures from your certificate up to one of them.

Lesson 2 of 10

X.509 certificates

A certificate is a structured document, encoded in a format called ASN.1 DER, and usually shown as a block of base64 text between BEGIN CERTIFICATE lines. Its core fields are short. The subject is who the certificate is for. The issuer is the authority that signed it. Not Before and Not After give the validity period. The subject public key is the key being vouched for. Then come extensions, and they matter more than the old fields. Subject Alternative Name lists the domain names the certificate is valid for, and browsers ignore the old common name field. Basic Constraints says whether the certificate belongs to a CA that may sign other certificates. Key Usage and Extended Key Usage say what the key may do, such as server authentication. Finally the issuer’s signature covers all of it. Certificates are also getting shorter lived. In 2025 the CA and Browser Forum voted to cut the maximum lifetime of public TLS certificates step by step from 398 days to 47 days by 2029, which pushes everyone towards automatic renewal with the ACME protocol, as Let’s Encrypt has done since 2015.
What it is
An X.509 certificate binds a subject (such as a domain name) to a public key, for a validity period, signed by an issuer. Extensions limit what it can be used for.
Why we need it
It lets a browser trust a public key it has never seen, by trusting the CA that signed it. Its fields decide which names and uses are allowed.
How it works
The CA fills in the fields, hashes the to be signed part with SHA-256, and signs the hash with its private key (RSA or ECDSA). Anyone can check the signature with the CA’s public key.

Lesson 3 of 10

Chains of trust and how browsers verify them

Root CA keys are too valuable to use every day. They sit in offline hardware security modules, often in guarded rooms, and are switched on only to sign a few intermediate certificates. The intermediates sign the certificates of websites. So a website sends its own certificate and the intermediate, and the browser builds a chain up to a root that is already in its store. Then it checks, for every link, that the signature is valid, that the certificate is within its dates, and that every certificate above the leaf is really allowed to act as a CA. For the leaf, it checks that the name typed in the address bar appears in the Subject Alternative Name list. It also checks that the certificate has not been revoked. Each of these checks has failed in real software. In 2014 Apple’s goto fail bug skipped signature checks in iOS and macOS. In 2020, the CurveBall bug in Windows let attackers forge chains with a custom elliptic curve. And countless apps have disabled checking entirely to silence errors during development, then shipped that way.

What the browser checks, in order
  1. Build a path from the leaf through intermediates to a root in the local trust store.
  2. Verify each signature with the public key of the certificate above it.
  3. Check every certificate is within its validity dates.
  4. Check that every issuer has Basic Constraints CA = true and respects path length and name constraints.
  5. Check the host name against the leaf’s Subject Alternative Name list, and the Extended Key Usage allows serverAuth.
  6. Check revocation (stapled OCSP or CRL data) and Certificate Transparency proofs.

Lesson 4 of 10

Revocation and Certificate Transparency

What happens when a private key is stolen while its certificate still has months to run? The CA can revoke the certificate, but browsers must find out. The first method was a certificate revocation list, a signed list of revoked serial numbers that grew to many megabytes. Then came OCSP, where the browser asks the CA about one certificate, which is slow and tells the CA which sites you visit. OCSP stapling lets the server fetch a fresh signed answer and send it in the handshake. In practice browsers often ignored revocation failures, so the industry is moving to short lifetimes and compressed lists that browsers download in the background, such as Firefox’s CRLite. In 2024 Let’s Encrypt announced it would end OCSP and rely on revocation lists. The second problem is that any trusted CA can issue a certificate for any name. Certificate Transparency, designed at Google by Ben Laurie and Adam Langley after the DigiNotar breach, requires every public certificate to be recorded in public append only logs built as Merkle trees. Browsers refuse certificates without proof of logging, and domain owners watch the logs for certificates they never asked for.
What it is
Revocation marks a certificate as untrusted before it expires (CRL, OCSP, stapling). Certificate Transparency (CT) puts every public certificate in public Merkle tree logs.
Why we need it
Revocation limits the damage of stolen keys. CT makes it impossible for a CA, hacked or dishonest, to issue a certificate in secret.
How it works
The CA submits a certificate to logs and gets back signed promises (SCTs) to include it. The log publishes signed tree heads, and anyone can request inclusion and consistency proofs of a few dozen hashes.

Lesson 5 of 10

The TLS 1.3 handshake

TLS 1.3, published as RFC 8446 in August 2018 after four years and 28 drafts, is a deep clean of the protocol. Everything known to be fragile was removed: RSA key transport, static Diffie-Hellman, CBC mode ciphers, RC4, SHA-1 signatures, compression and renegotiation. What remains is a short list of five cipher suites, all authenticated encryption, and key exchange that is always ephemeral Diffie-Hellman, so forward secrecy is guaranteed. The handshake is also faster. The client guesses which group the server will accept and sends its key share in the very first message, so the server can answer with its own share and start encrypting at once. Data flows after one round trip instead of two. Everything after the ServerHello is encrypted, including the certificate, which used to travel in plain view. The server proves its identity by signing the whole handshake transcript with its certificate key, in a message called CertificateVerify. A returning client can even send data in its first message, called 0-RTT, but that early data can be replayed by an attacker, so servers must only accept it for requests that are safe to repeat.
What it is
TLS 1.3 is the current Transport Layer Security protocol. It runs an authenticated ephemeral (EC)DHE handshake and then protects records with AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
Why we need it
It secures HTTPS, email transport, VPNs and APIs. Version 1.3 cut a round trip, encrypted the handshake, and removed the options behind a decade of attacks.
How it works
ClientHello with key shares, ServerHello with the server share, then encrypted EncryptedExtensions, Certificate, CertificateVerify and Finished messages. Keys come from HKDF over the ECDHE secret and the transcript hash.

The TLS 1.3 key schedule

In TLS 1.3, every key comes from one carefully designed chain of HKDF steps, called the key schedule. It starts with an early secret, which is just zeros unless a pre shared key is used. A derived value from it becomes the salt for mixing in the fresh Diffie-Hellman result, which gives the handshake secret. From that come the keys that encrypt the rest of the handshake, separately for each direction. Another derive and extract step gives the master secret, and from it the application traffic keys. Every derivation also mixes in a hash of all handshake messages so far, called the transcript hash, so keys are bound to exactly what both sides saw. The labels, such as c hs traffic for client handshake traffic, keep each key separate. The design was checked by cryptographers with formal tools such as Tamarin and ProVerif before the standard was finished, a first for TLS. Lesson 7 of 10

A history of TLS attacks

Almost every weakness removed in TLS 1.3 was found by a real attack. In 2011 BEAST used predictable CBC initial vectors in TLS 1.0 to recover cookies. In 2012 CRIME used compression: the size of compressed data leaked whether a guess matched a secret. Lucky Thirteen in 2013 used tiny timing differences in CBC padding checks. In April 2014, Heartbleed, a missing length check in OpenSSL’s heartbeat feature, let anyone read up to 64 kilobytes of server memory per request, including private keys, and affected a large part of the web. Later in 2014, POODLE broke SSL 3.0. In 2015 FREAK and Logjam tricked servers into using deliberately weak export grade keys from 1990s US export rules. DROWN in 2016 used servers that still supported SSL 2.0 to break modern connections. ROBOT in 2017 revived Bleichenbacher’s padding oracle. Several of these were protocol design flaws, and others were implementation bugs in one library. The lessons: remove options you do not need, keep libraries updated, and test your servers with tools such as testssl.sh or the SSL Labs scanner.

Lesson 8 of 10

Deploying TLS well

Getting TLS right on a real site is mostly configuration. Use certificates from an ACME authority such as Let’s Encrypt, renewed automatically, because outages from expired certificates are still common, even at large companies. Enable TLS 1.3 and TLS 1.2 only, with ECDHE key exchange and AEAD ciphers. Send the HTTP Strict Transport Security header, so browsers always use HTTPS for your domain and an attacker cannot strip the connection back to plain HTTP. For the strongest protection, add your domain to the browser HSTS preload list. Staple OCSP if your CA supports it. For service to service traffic inside a company, use mutual TLS, where the client also presents a certificate, often issued automatically by a service mesh. Certificate pinning, where an app accepts only specific keys, can stop rogue CAs, but browsers abandoned HTTP public key pinning around 2018, because mistakes locked sites out of their own users. Finally, plan for the quantum transition: modern browsers and servers already negotiate the hybrid group X25519MLKEM768, which combines X25519 with the post quantum ML-KEM.
A good HTTPS setup
  1. Automate certificates with ACME (certbot, Caddy, cert-manager) and monitor expiry.
  2. Allow TLS 1.2 and 1.3 only, ECDHE plus AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
  3. Redirect HTTP to HTTPS and send Strict-Transport-Security: max-age=63072000; includeSubDomains; preload.
  4. Enable OCSP stapling where supported, and watch CT logs for your domains (for example with crt.sh).
  5. Use mutual TLS for internal services, and prefer the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 group where available.
  6. Scan regularly with testssl.sh or SSL Labs and fix anything below an A grade.

From beginner to expert

TLS is something every developer uses and few understand fully. A beginner learns that the padlock means the connection is encrypted and that the site’s certificate was checked, and that a certificate warning should never be clicked through. A college student can read a certificate, explain the chain of trust, describe the TLS 1.3 handshake and its key schedule, and configure a server with HSTS and modern ciphers. A masters student studies authenticated key exchange proofs, the formal analysis of TLS 1.3 that found and fixed problems in drafts, attacks on implementations through state machine bugs, and the economics and policy of the CA system, including root programs and distrust decisions such as Symantec’s in 2018. At the research level, people verify TLS implementations formally, as in Project Everest’s miTLS and EverCrypt, design Encrypted Client Hello to hide even the site name, measure the deployment of post quantum hybrids, and propose replacements for parts of the CA system, such as Merkle tree certificates.
One idea, four depths

The padlock means the connection is encrypted and the site proved its identity with a certificate from a trusted authority. Never click through a certificate warning.

Read certificate fields, verify a chain step by step, explain revocation and CT, trace the TLS 1.3 handshake and key schedule, and configure HTTPS with ACME, HSTS and modern suites.

Authenticated key exchange models and the TLS 1.3 proofs, state machine attacks, downgrade resistance, 0-RTT replay, PKI governance (root programs, distrust of Symantec), and mutual TLS at scale.

Verified implementations (Project Everest), Encrypted Client Hello, post quantum and hybrid key exchange in TLS, Merkle tree certificates and other proposals to change the CA model.

Lesson 10 of 10

Chapter summary

  1. PKI binds names to keys: CAs sign X.509 certificates, browsers trust about 150 roots. DigiNotar (2011) showed what a hacked CA can do.
  2. Certificates: subject, issuer, validity, public key, SAN, Basic Constraints, Key Usage, all signed by the issuer. Lifetimes fall to 47 days by 2029.
  3. Chain checks: path to a trusted root, every signature, dates, CA flags, host name in SAN, revocation. Never disable verification.
  4. Revocation: CRL, OCSP, stapling, CRLite, short lifetimes. CT: public Merkle logs, proofs of about log2 n hashes.
  5. TLS 1.3: 1-RTT, always ephemeral (EC)DHE, five AEAD suites, encrypted certificate, CertificateVerify signs the transcript. 0-RTT data can be replayed.
  6. Key schedule: HKDF from early to handshake to master secret, labels and transcript hashes bind every key.
  7. Attacks: BEAST, CRIME, Lucky Thirteen, Heartbleed, POODLE, FREAK, Logjam, DROWN, ROBOT, each removed or fixed.
  8. Deploy: ACME automation, TLS 1.2 and 1.3 only, HSTS with preload, mTLS inside, hybrid X25519MLKEM768 for quantum safety.
