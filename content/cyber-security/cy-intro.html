Lesson 1 of 12

What cybersecurity is

Welcome to cybersecurity. Before we read the page, here is the story behind it. In the early days, computers sat in locked rooms and only a few trusted people could touch them. Security meant a good door lock. Then computers were joined together into networks, and later into the internet. Suddenly a person on the other side of the world could reach your computer without ever touching it. In 1988 a student named Robert Morris released a small program to measure the size of the internet. A bug made it copy itself again and again, and it slowed down thousands of computers in one day. People realised that a computer can be harmed from far away, by code alone. Today almost everything runs on computers: your bank, your hospital, the power in your home and the trains you ride. So cybersecurity is no longer a topic only for experts. It is about keeping daily life working. In this lesson we will see what cybersecurity protects, who it protects it from, and the simple words every security person uses.

Source: NIST SP 800-12 Rev. 1, the NIST glossary, Saltzer and Schroeder (1975) and the UK National Audit Office report on WannaCry (2017).

What it is
Cybersecurity is the work of protecting computers, networks, programs and data from people who want to steal them, change them or stop them working. It includes tools (like passwords and firewalls), habits (like not clicking strange links) and plans (like backups and what to do after an attack).
Why we need it
Our money, health records, photos, messages, jobs and even electricity and water now depend on computers. If an attacker steals or breaks them, real people get hurt: hospitals cancel operations, shops close, and families lose savings. Cybersecurity keeps these systems trustworthy.
How it works
We first ask what we are protecting (the assets), from whom (the threats) and where we are weak (the vulnerabilities). Then we add controls, which are the things that block, spot or recover from an attack. We keep checking, because attackers keep changing.
Real world scenario: the WannaCry attack on hospitals, May 2017

On 12 May 2017 a program called WannaCry spread across the world and locked the files on more than 200,000 computers in about 150 countries. It demanded payment in Bitcoin to unlock them. In England many hospitals and doctors had to cancel appointments and send ambulances to other hospitals. The program used a weakness in old Windows file sharing. Microsoft had released a fix two months before, in March 2017. The computers that were hit had simply not been updated. One missed update turned into a crisis for patients.

Cybersecurity is a mix of three things, and you need all three. People must know how to spot tricks. Process means clear rules, such as who can approve a payment and how often backups are tested. Technology means the tools, such as encryption, firewalls and updates. A great tool fails if people switch it off, and good people fail if the process lets one person do everything alone.

Remember
Cybersecurity is not only about hackers in hoodies. Most damage comes from ordinary gaps: a missed update, a reused password, a public storage folder, or a person tricked by a fake email.
Lesson 2 of 12

The three goals: confidentiality, integrity and availability

Security people love short names, and the most famous one is the CIA triad. It has nothing to do with the spy agency. C stands for confidentiality, I for integrity and A for availability. These three ideas were written down in the 1970s and 1980s by people building military and banking computers, and they are still the first thing every security exam asks. Here is an easy way to remember them. Think of your diary. Confidentiality means nobody else reads it. Integrity means nobody secretly tears out a page or adds a fake line. Availability means you can open it whenever you want. If any one of these breaks, the diary is no longer safe. Every attack you will ever study breaks at least one of these three goals. So when you hear about a new attack in the news, ask yourself: which letter did it break?
What it is
The CIA triad is the three goals of security. Confidentiality: only the right people can see the data. Integrity: the data is correct and nobody changed it without permission. Availability: the system and data work when the right people need them.
Why we need it
A clear goal tells you what "safe" means. Without it, people argue about tools. With it, you can look at any system and ask three simple questions, and you can measure if a control really helps.
How it works
For each asset we decide how much each goal matters. Medical records need high confidentiality and integrity. A news website needs high availability and integrity, but its articles are public. Then we pick controls: encryption for confidentiality, hashes and signatures for integrity, backups and spare servers for availability.

Three more words that go with CIA
Authenticity: it really comes from who it says. Non repudiation: the sender cannot later deny sending it. Accountability: every action can be traced to a person.
Some books add these to make five goals. The Parkerian hexad (Donn Parker, 1998) uses six: confidentiality, possession, integrity, authenticity, availability and utility.
AAAExam favourite
Authentication (who are you), Authorisation (what may you do), Accounting (what did you do)
The three steps every login system follows. Exam questions often mix them up.
In the exam
Goals can pull against each other. Locking a file with a strong password raises confidentiality but can hurt availability if the password is lost. Good security balances all three for each asset.
Lesson 3 of 12

The words every security person uses

Security has its own language, and the words can feel heavy at first. The good news is that they map to things you already know from daily life. Think of your house. The things inside, like your phone and jewellery, are assets. A burglar is a threat. A window that does not lock is a vulnerability. The burglar climbing through that window is an exploit. The chance that you get robbed, and how much it would cost, is the risk. The locks, the dog and the alarm are controls. Once you see the house picture, every security report becomes easier to read. These words are also defined officially by NIST, the American standards body, and those definitions are used all over the world in exams and in contracts.

Common mistake
A vulnerability is the weakness, an exploit is the method that uses it, and a threat is who might use it. Students often swap these three in exams.
Lesson 4 of 12

Who attacks and why: real incidents

Why would anyone attack a computer? Mostly for the same reasons people commit any crime: money, anger, beliefs, or power. In the 1980s and 1990s many attacks were done by curious teenagers who wanted to show off. Today the biggest share is organised crime, run like a business, with customer support for victims who want to pay a ransom. Governments also attack each other quietly, to spy or to prepare for conflict. And some of the most damaging attacks come from inside: an angry employee, or simply a tired one who makes a mistake. In this lesson we meet these groups and look at real incidents. Each one teaches a lesson that is still true today, so pay attention to the last column of the table, the lesson learned.

Real world scenario: a fake call from the bank

Riya gets a call: "Madam, this is your bank. Your card is blocked. Please tell me the OTP we just sent so we can unblock it." The caller knows her name and the last four digits of her card, so it feels real. The OTP was really for a payment the caller started. Nothing was hacked in the computer sense. The attacker hacked a person. This is social engineering, and it is behind a huge share of real fraud. Banks never ask for your OTP, PIN or password on a call.

Remember
Attackers usually pick the easiest way in, not the cleverest. That is why boring basics (updates, MFA, backups, training) stop most real attacks.
Lesson 5 of 12

Defence in depth: many layers, not one wall

Old castles were not protected by one wall. They had a moat, then an outer wall, then an inner wall, then guards at the keep. An enemy had to beat every layer. Security people borrowed this idea and called it defence in depth. The reason is simple. Every single control fails sometimes. A spam filter misses some emails. A person clicks a link on a busy day. An antivirus does not know a brand new virus. But if several different layers stand in the way, an attack must slip past all of them, and that is much less likely. The maths behind this is just multiplication of chances, and the animation will show you how quickly the numbers drop. Keep one warning in mind. The maths assumes the layers fail for different reasons. If two layers share the same weakness, they can fall together.
What it is
Defence in depth means putting several different controls one behind another, so that an attacker must beat all of them.
Why we need it
No control is perfect. If you rely on one, its single failure is a full breach. Layers turn one big failure into several small chances that must all go wrong together.
How it works
An attack gets through only if every layer misses it. If layer i misses with chance mi, and the layers fail independently, the chance of a breach is m1 × m2 × ... × mk.

Chance that an attack passes every layerExam favourite
P(breach) = m1 × m2 × ... × mk
mi is the chance that layer i misses. Only true when the layers fail independently.
Chance that at least one of many attempts succeedsExam favourite
P(at least one) = 1 − (1 − p)n
p is the chance one attempt works, n is the number of attempts. Attackers only need to win once.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 at_least_one.py.
Worked example: why one click in a big company is almost certain
  1. A company has 100 staff. A phishing email fools each person with chance p = 0.03.
  2. Chance one person does not click: 1 − 0.03 = 0.97.
  3. Chance nobody clicks, if people act independently: 0.97100 ≈ 0.048.
  4. P(at least one click) = 1 − 0.048 ≈ 0.952
  5. So there is about a 95% chance someone clicks. The defender cannot rely on "nobody will click". There must be layers after the click: MFA, antivirus, network limits and monitoring.
What the attacker triesWhat the defender does

The email gets through and someone clicksTraining helps people report it, the browser warns about fake sites
The person types their password into a fake pageMFA means the stolen password alone does not log in

Malware tries to spreadThe network is split into zones, so it cannot reach the servers
Data is copied outMonitoring spots the large upload, and the data is encrypted at rest
In the exam
The attacker needs to win once. The defender needs to win every time. Layers change this: now the attacker must win at every layer, every time.
Lesson 6 of 12

Design principles that last

In 1975, two researchers at MIT, Jerome Saltzer and Michael Schroeder, wrote a paper about protecting information in computers. Computers then were huge machines shared by many users. Yet the eight design principles in their paper are still taught in every university today, because they describe how to build systems that fail safely. Think of them as habits of a careful builder. Give each person only the keys they need. Make the safe choice the default. Check every request, not just the first one. Keep things simple, because simple things are easier to check. Later, around 2010, the idea of zero trust was added: never assume someone is friendly just because they are inside your network. We will look at each idea with an everyday picture.
PrinciplePlain meaningEveryday picture
Least privilegeGive each user and program only the access it needs, and only for as long as neededA hotel key card opens only your room, and only until you check out
Fail safe defaultsIf unsure, say no. Access is denied unless it is allowedA new employee gets no access until a manager approves it
Complete mediationCheck permission on every request, not only the firstA cinema checks your ticket at the door of each screen
Economy of mechanismKeep the design small and simple so it can be checkedA simple lock has fewer ways to break
Open designSecurity must not depend on hiding how it works. Only the key is secretEveryone knows how a padlock works, yet it is safe without the key
Separation of privilegeNeed two conditions or two people for important actionsA bank locker needs both your key and the bank’s key
Least common mechanismDo not share the same thing between users unless neededSeparate towels for each guest
Psychological acceptabilitySecurity must be easy enough that people really use itIf the door is too hard to lock, people prop it open
Zero trustNever trust because of location. Always verify the user, the device and the requestOffice staff still show ID at every floor, not only at the front gate
Going deeper: Zero trust in NIST SP 800-207

NIST SP 800-207 (2020) describes zero trust as moving defences from a fixed network edge to users, devices and resources. Every request is checked by a policy decision point using who the user is, how healthy the device is and how sensitive the resource is, and it is enforced by a policy enforcement point in front of the resource. Being on the office network gives no extra trust. The old model is often called the castle and moat model: hard outside, soft inside. Zero trust exists because attackers who get inside the moat (through phishing or a stolen VPN password) used to move freely.

In the exam
Open design is also called Kerckhoffs’s principle (1883): a cipher should stay safe even if the enemy knows everything about it except the key. Hiding the method is called security through obscurity, and it is not enough on its own.
Lesson 7 of 12

Cybersecurity compared with similar ideas

People often use words like cybersecurity, information security, privacy and safety as if they mean the same thing. They overlap, but each one has its own focus, and knowing the difference helps you read job ads, laws and exam questions. Information security is the oldest and widest. It protects information in any form, even paper in a filing cabinet. Cybersecurity focuses on information and systems that are connected, such as the internet, phones and industrial machines. Privacy is about people: what is collected about you and how it is used, even when nothing is stolen. Safety is about protecting people from accidents, while security is about protecting against someone who means harm. Let us put them side by side.
Security and its neighbours
Information securityCybersecurityPrivacySafety and reliability

Main enemyAnyone who misuses informationAttackers who come through networks and codeOver collection and misuse, even by the company itselfChance failures, wear, human error
Typical toolsPolicies, locked cabinets, encryption, access controlFirewalls, patching, MFA, monitoring, incident responseConsent, data minimisation, anonymisation, laws such as GDPRRedundancy, testing, safety checks
Question it asksIs the information protected?Can an attacker get in through the network?Should we collect and use this data at all?What if this part fails by itself?

Bottom line: Information security is the widest circle. Cybersecurity is the part about connected systems. Privacy asks whether data should be used at all, so a perfectly secure system can still break privacy. Safety plans for accidents, security plans for enemies, and modern systems such as cars and power grids need both.

Lesson 8 of 12

The maths of security decisions

You might think security is all about clever tricks, but behind every good security decision there is some simple maths. Banks, insurance companies and governments decide how much to spend on security by estimating chances and costs. In 2002 two economists, Lawrence Gordon and Martin Loeb, asked a famous question: how much should a company spend to protect a piece of information? Their surprising answer was that, under fairly general conditions, it is never worth spending more than about 37 percent of the expected loss. The number 37 percent comes from one divided by e, the special number from maths that is about 2.718. In this lesson you will use three tools: multiplying chances, the chance of at least one success, and expected loss. With these you can already reason like a risk analyst.

These four formulas come back in every section of this subject. Read them slowly and try the worked problems tab after this lesson.

Expected lossExam favourite
expected loss = probability × cost
The average loss per year if the same risk is faced many years. Used to compare risks of very different kinds.
Independent events, all happen
P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Used for layers that must all fail.
Complement
P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
Often it is easier to work out the chance that nothing goes wrong, then subtract from 1.
Gordon and Loeb boundExam favourite
best spend ≤ (1 / e) × expected loss ≈ 0.368 × expected loss
For the classes of breach probability functions studied by Gordon and Loeb (2002). A rule of thumb, not a law.

Worked example: is this security tool worth buying?
  1. Ransomware has a 5% chance per year of hitting a school. One attack would cost 20 lakh rupees (lost days, recovery, new machines).
  2. expected loss = 0.05 × 20,00,000 = 1,00,000 rupees per year
  3. A backup system costs 30,000 rupees a year and would cut the cost of an attack to 2 lakh rupees.
  4. new expected loss = 0.05 × 2,00,000 = 10,000 rupees per year
  5. Saving per year = 1,00,000 − 10,000 = 90,000 rupees, for a cost of 30,000. The tool is worth it. Its cost is also well below the Gordon and Loeb limit of about 36,800 rupees.
Going deeper: Where the 1 / e comes from

Gordon and Loeb model a firm with a potential loss L, a vulnerability v (the chance of a breach with no spending) and a breach probability S(z, v) after spending z. For the two families of S they studied, with S falling smoothly and with diminishing returns as z grows, they proved that the best spend z* satisfies z* < (1/e) v L. In words: spend less than about 37% of the expected loss v L. They also showed that the best spend does not always rise with v. For very vulnerable assets it can be better to spend less and accept the loss, because each rupee buys too little protection.

Roles and the certification path

Cybersecurity is a big field with many kinds of jobs, and you do not need to be a genius programmer to join it. Some people defend networks in a security operations centre, watching alerts like air traffic controllers. Some test systems by attacking them with permission, which is called ethical hacking or penetration testing. Some investigate crimes after they happen, which is digital forensics. Some write policies and check that companies follow the law. Some design secure systems, and some do research on new cryptography. Certifications help employers trust that you know the basics. They usually start broad, like CompTIA Security Plus, and then become specialised, like the hands on OSCP exam for penetration testers, or CISSP for experienced managers. Lesson 10 of 12

From beginner to expert

The same idea can be explained at many depths. A school student, a college student, a masters student and a researcher can all talk about security, but they ask different questions. At school level, security is about keeping your secrets safe and your devices working. At college level, you name the goals, the threats and the controls, and you start to measure risk. At masters level, you build a threat model: you write down exactly what the attacker can and cannot do, and you check every part of a system against it. At research level, people prove that a system is secure under a precise mathematical model, or they find a new kind of attack that breaks those models. Pick the tab that matches you, then try the next one when you feel ready.
One idea, four depths

Cybersecurity keeps your computer, phone and accounts safe. It keeps secrets secret, stops people changing your data, and keeps things working. Use strong passwords, turn on updates and never share an OTP.

Security protects confidentiality, integrity and availability of assets against threats that use vulnerabilities. Risk is likelihood times impact. Controls are preventive, detective or corrective, and defence in depth stacks them so one failure is not a breach.

Security engineering starts with a threat model: the assets, the attacker’s goals, what the attacker can do (read the network, run code, bribe an insider) and the trust boundaries. Tools such as STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege) and attack trees check each part of a design against that model. Risk is managed with expected loss and the economics of controls.

Research formalises security as a game between a challenger and an adversary with bounded resources. A scheme is secure if every efficient adversary wins with only negligible advantage, proved by reduction to a hard problem (factoring, discrete log, lattice problems). The Dolev and Yao model (1983) lets the attacker control the whole network but not break cryptography. Security economics (Anderson, 2001) explains why systems stay insecure: those who could fix a problem often are not the ones who suffer from it.

Beginner
Name the CIA goals, spot threat, vulnerability and risk in a story, and list daily habits that keep you safe.
Intermediate
Use risk = likelihood × impact, compute layered breach chances and at least one probabilities, and explain the Saltzer and Schroeder principles.
Expert
Write a threat model with STRIDE, reason about security investment with Gordon and Loeb, and explain the Dolev and Yao attacker and reduction proofs.

Chapter summary

  1. Cybersecurity protects computers, networks, programs and data from people who want to steal, change or stop them. It needs people, process and technology together.
  2. The CIA triad: confidentiality (only the right people read it), integrity (nobody changes it unnoticed), availability (it works when needed). Every attack breaks at least one.
  3. AAA: authentication (who you are), authorisation (what you may do), accounting (what you did).
  4. An asset is what we protect, a threat is who may harm it, a vulnerability is the weakness, an exploit uses the weakness, and risk = likelihood × impact.
  5. Attackers range from script kiddies to criminal gangs, hacktivists, nation states and insiders. Most real breaches use easy gaps: missed patches, no MFA, phishing.
  6. Defence in depth: with independent layers, P(breach) is the product of the miss chances. With many attempts, P(at least one success) = 1 − (1 − p)n.
  7. Saltzer and Schroeder principles (least privilege, fail safe defaults, complete mediation, open design and others) and zero trust guide good design.
  8. Expected loss = probability × cost. Gordon and Loeb: spend less than about 37% (1/e) of the expected loss.
