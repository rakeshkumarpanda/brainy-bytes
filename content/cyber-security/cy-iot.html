Lesson 1 of 10

Computers in everything: IoT and industrial systems

Look around your home. A Wi-Fi router, a smart TV, maybe a doorbell camera, a smart speaker, a fitness band, perhaps a smart electricity meter. Each one is a small computer on the internet, and together they are called the Internet of Things, IoT. There are well over fifteen billion of them worldwide. Behind the scenes there is a second, older family: industrial control systems, the computers that run power stations, water treatment plants, oil pipelines, factories and railway signals. Both families share a problem. They were often built by companies focused on features and price, not security, they stay in service for many years, often more than ten for industrial equipment, and many are hard or impossible to update. When they are attacked, the damage can be physical, not only lost data: a power cut, a flooded tank, a car that brakes by itself. In 2015 the researchers Charlie Miller and Chris Valasek showed they could control a Jeep Cherokee’s brakes and steering over the mobile network from miles away, and the maker recalled 1.4 million vehicles. In 2021 hackers got into the cloud account of the camera company Verkada using a password found exposed online, and could watch about 150,000 cameras in hospitals, schools and prisons. This chapter starts with consumer IoT and the Mirai botnet, then moves into factories and power grids, where the rules of security change.

What it is
IoT security protects connected everyday devices. Industrial control system (ICS) or operational technology (OT) security protects the computers that control physical processes, such as PLCs, SCADA systems and safety systems.
Why we need it
These devices are many, cheap, long lived and hard to patch, and attacks on them can cause physical harm, from botnets that take down websites to power cuts and explosions.
How it works
Unique passwords and automatic updates for devices, network segmentation, allow lists for industrial commands, independent safety systems, monitoring of both the network and the physical process, and laws that set minimum standards.

Real world scenario: Around you

Every traffic signal at a big junction is run by a controller in a roadside box. If someone could change its timing from the internet, they could turn every light green at once. Engineers design these so that a hardware safety circuit refuses conflicting greens, whatever the software says. That idea, a safety layer that does not trust the computer, runs through this whole chapter.

Lesson 2 of 10

Default passwords and the Mirai botnet

In September 2016 the security journalist Brian Krebs’s website was hit by one of the largest floods of traffic ever seen, about 620 gigabits per second. Days later the French hosting company OVH saw nearly a terabit per second. Then on 21 October 2016 the same kind of attack hit Dyn, a company that runs DNS for many big websites, and Twitter, Netflix, Reddit and GitHub became unreachable for many people across the United States and Europe. The weapon was a botnet called Mirai, made of hundreds of thousands of cameras, video recorders and routers. Mirai did not use any clever exploit. It scanned the internet for devices with the telnet port open and tried a list of about sixty factory usernames and passwords, such as admin and admin, or root and xc3511, a password shared by many cheap video recorders. Researchers later counted about 600,000 infected devices at its peak. Its authors, three young men from the United States, released the source code online, and copies are still active. The animation shows why the share of devices on default passwords matters so much: with 60 percent the worm spreads like a fire, with 2 percent it barely moves. That is why the UK banned universal default passwords for new consumer devices from 29 April 2024, why California required unique passwords from 2020, and why the EU’s Cyber Resilience Act will require security updates for connected products.

Lesson 3 of 10

Building safer devices: the OWASP IoT Top 10

What should a well made connected device do? The OWASP IoT Top 10, published in 2018, lists the commonest failures, and the first one is the Mirai problem: weak, guessable or hard coded passwords. Next come insecure network services left running, like telnet, insecure web and cloud interfaces, and the lack of a secure update mechanism. A device that cannot be updated safely will carry every bug it shipped with for its whole life. Good devices check that every firmware update is signed by the maker before installing it, use secure boot so that only signed software can start, store keys in a secure element chip, turn off debugging ports such as UART and JTAG in production, encrypt their traffic, and collect only the data they need. Makers should publish how long the device will get security updates and give researchers a way to report bugs. As a buyer you can help too: choose brands that promise updates, change any default password straight away, keep devices updated, put them on a separate guest network away from your laptops, and switch off features such as remote access or UPnP that you do not use.

Making a smart home safer, today
  1. Change every default password to a unique one, kept in a password manager.
  2. Turn on automatic updates, and replace devices that no longer get them.
  3. Put IoT devices on a separate guest network.
  4. Switch off UPnP and remote access features you do not use.
  5. Check the router’s list of connected devices now and then.
Lesson 4 of 10

Industrial control systems: when safety comes first

Industrial control systems look very different from office IT. At the bottom are the physical things: pumps, valves, motors, breakers and sensors. Above them sit programmable logic controllers, PLCs, small rugged computers that read sensors and switch outputs many times a second, following simple logic written by engineers. Above those are SCADA systems, supervisory control and data acquisition, and HMIs, human machine interfaces, the screens where operators watch the plant and give commands. A historian database records every reading. And separate from all of these is the safety instrumented system, an independent controller with its own sensors whose only job is to shut the process down safely if something dangerous happens. In office IT the priority is usually confidentiality, then integrity, then availability. In a plant the order flips. Safety comes first, then availability, because stopping a furnace or a grid can itself be dangerous and expensive, then integrity, and confidentiality last. That changes everything. You cannot just reboot a PLC to install a patch, because the process it controls might run for months without stopping, and a plant may run the same controller for twenty years. Scanning a network aggressively can crash old controllers. So industrial security leans on separation, careful monitoring and safety systems that do not trust the computers.

Bottom line: Treat the plant network as a separate world with its own rules, and design so that even a fully hacked control system cannot cause a disaster, because an independent safety layer will stop it.

Industrial protocols with no passwords: Modbus and friends

Many industrial protocols were designed in the 1970s and 1980s for wires that ran only inside a factory, where everyone on the network was trusted. Modbus, created by the company Modicon in 1979, is the classic example and is still everywhere. A Modbus command is just a few bytes: who it is for, a function code such as read registers or write a coil, an address and a value. There is no username, no password and no signature. Any device that can reach a PLC on Modbus TCP port 502 can tell it to open a valve, and the PLC will obey. The animation walks through such a frame byte by byte on a toy water tank, and shows the two defences that matter most. An industrial firewall that understands Modbus can allow write commands only from the operator’s HMI and drop everything else. And an independent safety system with its own sensor and valve can stop the tank overflowing whatever the PLC does. Newer versions add security, such as Modbus TCP Security, which wraps the protocol in TLS with certificates and roles, and DNP3 Secure Authentication for power grids, but old equipment lives for decades, so plants must protect insecure protocols for a long time to come.

The Purdue model and network segmentation

Because old protocols cannot defend themselves, the main protection in a plant is keeping attackers away from them. The Purdue model, from a reference architecture developed at Purdue University in the 1990s, gives a shared way to draw this. It stacks the plant in levels: level 0 is the physical process, level 1 the controllers, level 2 the HMIs and SCADA, level 3 site operations, then an industrial DMZ at level 3.5, level 4 the business network with email and ERP, and level 5 the internet. The rule is that traffic should not jump levels, and that the business network should never talk straight to the control network. Data the business needs, such as production figures, is copied to servers in the DMZ, and engineers who must reach the plant from outside go through a jump host in the DMZ with multi factor authentication and recorded sessions. The most sensitive sites go further and use data diodes, hardware that physically lets signals travel in only one direction, out of the plant. The international standard IEC 62443 turns this into zones and conduits, each zone with a required security level. The animation shows why it matters. On a flat network a phishing email on one office PC can lead all the way to the breakers, which is close to what happened in Ukraine in 2015. With a DMZ, the same breach stays in the office.

Lesson 7 of 10

Real attacks on industry: Stuxnet to Colonial Pipeline

A handful of incidents shaped this whole field. Stuxnet, discovered in 2010, was a worm built to sabotage Iran’s uranium enrichment plant at Natanz. It spread on USB drives into a network with no internet connection, used four previously unknown Windows bugs, found Siemens controllers driving centrifuges, and made them spin too fast and then too slow while replaying normal readings to the operators. Analysts estimate it destroyed around a thousand centrifuges, and it showed the world that code could break machines. On 23 December 2015 attackers who had entered three Ukrainian power companies through phishing emails months earlier used the companies’ own control software to open breakers, cutting power to about 225,000 customers, and then wiped computers and disabled backup power to slow recovery. A year later a malware called Industroyer, which spoke grid protocols directly, caused a blackout in part of Kyiv. In 2017 the Triton malware, also called Trisis, targeted the safety instrumented system of a petrochemical plant in Saudi Arabia. It was found only because a bug in it tripped the plant safely. In May 2021 ransomware hit the office IT of Colonial Pipeline in the United States. The control systems were not infected, but the company shut the pipeline for several days because it could not bill or track fuel safely, causing fuel shortages on the east coast. And in 2019 the operator of India’s Kudankulam nuclear plant confirmed malware on an administrative network, while stating the control systems were isolated and unaffected.

Defending plants: monitor, separate and fail safe

Defending industrial systems uses a different rhythm from office security. First, know what you have. Many plants do not have a complete list of their controllers, firmware versions and network links, and you cannot protect what you cannot see. Passive monitoring tools listen to a copy of the network traffic without sending anything, so they cannot crash fragile devices, and they learn which device normally talks to which, using which commands. A new device, a write command from an unusual computer, or a firmware download outside a maintenance window raises an alert. Second, watch the process itself. If a tank level rises while every screen says the valve is shut, something is wrong, whatever the network says, which is the lesson of Stuxnet. Third, keep remote access tight, with a jump host, multi factor authentication and approval for each session, because remote access was the door in Ukraine and at Colonial. Fourth, patch in planned maintenance windows and use compensating controls, such as stricter firewall rules, for devices that cannot be patched. Fifth, keep the safety instrumented system truly independent, with physical key switches that must be turned before its program can be changed. Finally, practise: plants should be able to run manually for a while and should rehearse recovery from a cyber incident just as they rehearse fire drills.
An OT security programme in order
  1. Build an asset inventory: every controller, HMI, firmware version and network link.
  2. Draw zones and conduits (IEC 62443) and put a DMZ between IT and OT.
  3. Lock down remote access: jump host, MFA, approval and recording.
  4. Deploy passive network monitoring and alerts on new devices and write commands.
  5. Monitor the physical process for changes nobody ordered.
  6. Patch in maintenance windows and add compensating controls for the rest.
  7. Keep safety systems independent, with key switches in RUN.
  8. Exercise manual operation and incident recovery every year.
Practise safely and legally
Never scan or probe industrial or utility systems you do not own, even if they appear on the internet. Doing so can break real equipment and is a crime. Practise with simulators and virtual labs such as GRFICSv2, or with a cheap PLC trainer kit on your own desk.
