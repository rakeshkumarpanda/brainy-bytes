Lesson 1 of 9

Why anonymising data is not enough

For decades organisations protected people in released data by removing names. It kept failing. In 1997 Latanya Sweeney, then a graduate student at MIT, bought the voter list of Cambridge, Massachusetts for twenty dollars, joined it with supposedly anonymous hospital records using ZIP code, birth date and sex, and found the medical records of the state governor, William Weld. She later estimated that those three facts alone single out about 87 percent of Americans. In 2006 AOL released twenty million search queries with user names replaced by numbers, and New York Times reporters identified user 4417749 as a sixty two year old widow in Georgia from her searches. In 2008 Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov re identified Netflix Prize users by matching their anonymous movie ratings with public reviews on IMDb. Irit Dinur and Kobbi Nissim proved in 2003 that answering too many statistical queries too accurately lets anyone rebuild the database, and the US Census Bureau later showed it could rebuild many individual records from its own published 2010 tables. The answer, introduced by Cynthia Dwork, Frank McSherry, Kobbi Nissim and Adam Smith in 2006, is differential privacy. It is not a property of a dataset but of the process that produces an answer. It promises that the answer would be almost the same whether or not any one person’s data was included, so nobody can learn much about you from it, whatever else they know. The four authors received the Gödel Prize in 2017, the 2020 US Census became the first census to protect its published tables with differential privacy, and Apple and Google use it to collect statistics from phones.

Source: Warner (1965), Sweeney (1997, 2000), AOL search logs (2006), Narayanan and Shmatikov (2008), Dinur and Nissim (2003), Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith (2006), Dwork and Roth (2014), McSherry and Talwar (2007), RAPPOR (2014), Apple (2017), Abadi et al. (2016), Mironov (2017), McMahan et al. (2018), Yeom et al. (2018), US Census Bureau 2020 disclosure avoidance.

What it is
Differential privacy (DP) is a mathematical promise about a data release: adding or removing any one person changes the probability of every possible output by at most a factor eε.
Why we need it
Removing names fails because other facts link records back to people, and accurate answers to many queries can rebuild a database. DP holds whatever an attacker already knows.
How it works
Add carefully sized random noise to answers (Laplace or Gaussian), or to the data itself (randomised response), track the total privacy loss as a budget, and clip each person’s contribution so the noise can hide it.

Randomised response: the first private survey

The oldest differentially private method is older than the definition. In 1965 the statistician Stanley Warner wanted honest answers to embarrassing survey questions, such as whether someone had used drugs or cheated in an exam. His idea was to let each person randomise their own answer with a coin. In the version we use, the person flips a coin in secret. On heads they answer truthfully. On tails they flip again and say yes on heads and no on tails, whatever the truth. Any single yes could have come from the coin, so every person can deny it. But the surveyor knows exactly how the coins behave, so from the share of yes answers across many people they can work out the true rate. Someone who cheated says yes three quarters of the time, someone who did not says yes one quarter of the time, so a yes is at most three times as likely from a cheater. That ratio of 3 is e to the power epsilon, so this survey is differentially private with epsilon equal to the natural log of 3, about 1.1. Because each person adds the noise themselves, before the data leaves them, this is called local differential privacy. Google’s RAPPOR, published by Úlfar Erlingsson and colleagues in 2014, used a version of it to learn which home pages Chrome users had set without learning any one user’s setting, and Apple uses local differential privacy for things like popular emoji and new words typed on iPhones.

Lesson 3 of 9

The definition, sensitivity and the Laplace mechanism

Here is the definition carefully. Two databases are neighbours if they differ in one person’s data. A randomised algorithm M is epsilon differentially private if, for every pair of neighbouring databases and every set of possible outputs, the probability that M gives an output in that set is at most e to the epsilon times the probability for the neighbour. Small epsilon means strong privacy: with epsilon of 0.1 the two probabilities differ by at most about 10 percent. The relaxed version, epsilon delta privacy, allows the promise to fail with a tiny probability delta, which should be much smaller than one over the number of people. To achieve DP for a numeric query we first ask how much one person can change the answer. That is the sensitivity. For a count it is 1. For an average of n values clipped to a range from L to U, it is U minus L divided by n. The Laplace mechanism then adds noise drawn from a Laplace distribution with scale b equal to the sensitivity divided by epsilon. The Laplace curve falls off exponentially, and moving its centre by the sensitivity changes the height at any point by at most e to the epsilon, which is exactly the definition. The animation shows the curves for a count of 100 without you and 101 with you. The typical error is b, independent of how many people are in the database, so DP is very accurate for large populations and noisy for small groups.

Why it works
p(x − f(D)) / p(x − f(D′)) = e(|x − f(D′)| − |x − f(D)|) / b ≤ eΔf / b = eε
By the triangle inequality the exponent is at most Δf / b.
Going deeper: What does ε mean in practice

Think of an attacker who believes with probability 50% that you are in a database. After seeing an ε-DP answer, their belief can rise to at most eε / (1 + eε): 52.5% for ε = 0.1, 73% for ε = 1, 88% for ε = 2. Researchers often call ε ≤ 1 strong. Real deployments have used values from below 1 to above 10, so always ask what ε a system uses and over what time period.

Composition and the privacy budget

Every differentially private answer leaks a little. Ask enough questions and the leaks add up, which is exactly how reconstruction attacks work. Differential privacy measures this precisely, which is one of its great strengths. Sequential composition says that running mechanisms with epsilons epsilon 1, epsilon 2 and so on, on the same people, gives a total that is at most their sum. So an organisation sets a privacy budget, for example a total epsilon of 1, and every query spends some of it, as the animation and program show. Parallel composition says that queries on separate groups of people, such as one count per city when everyone lives in one city, cost only the largest of their epsilons, because each person is affected by just one of them. Post processing is free: anything you compute from a DP answer without looking at the data again, such as rounding or forcing a count to be at least zero, stays DP with the same epsilon. Group privacy says that protecting a group of k people, such as a family, costs k times epsilon. For many small queries, simple addition is too pessimistic. Advanced composition and newer accountants such as Rényi differential privacy, introduced by Ilya Mironov in 2017, give much tighter totals, and they are what makes training neural networks with DP practical.

The Gaussian and exponential mechanisms

Two more mechanisms complete the basic toolkit. The Gaussian mechanism adds normally distributed noise instead of Laplace noise. It gives epsilon delta privacy rather than pure epsilon privacy, and its noise is set by the L2 sensitivity, the length of the largest change one person can make to a whole vector of answers. That makes it the natural choice for high dimensional outputs such as gradients, and it composes very well with modern accountants, which is why DP-SGD uses it. For a single number, as in our program, the Laplace mechanism often adds less noise. Both need bounded sensitivity, so data is usually clipped first: salaries forced into a range, or each gradient scaled to a maximum length. Clipping is what stops one billionaire, or one very unusual patient, from dominating an answer. The exponential mechanism, from Frank McSherry and Kunal Talwar in 2007, handles answers that are not numbers, such as choosing the most common item, the best price, or the best split in a decision tree. It scores every possible answer with a utility function, then picks one at random with probability that grows exponentially with the score. Good answers are very likely, but no single person can change the probabilities by more than a factor e to the epsilon.

Differential privacy for machine learning: DP-SGD and DP-FedAvg

Advanced 1.2 showed that trained models can leak their training data through membership inference and extraction. Differential privacy is the strongest known defence, because it bounds what any output reveals about one example. In 2016 Martín Abadi and colleagues at Google introduced DP-SGD, differentially private stochastic gradient descent. Each training step does three things differently. It computes the gradient for each example separately, not just for the batch. It clips each per example gradient to a maximum length C, so no single example can push the model further than C. And it adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation sigma times C to the sum before taking the step. The batches are random samples, which adds privacy amplification by sampling. A privacy accountant, the moments accountant in the original paper and Rényi DP accountants today, adds up the privacy cost over all steps to report a final epsilon and delta. The cost is some accuracy, more computation for per example gradients, and careful tuning. For federated learning, Brendan McMahan and colleagues gave DP-FedAvg in 2018: clip each user’s whole update and add noise to the sum, which protects every user, not just every example. Samuel Yeom and colleagues showed that epsilon differential privacy caps any membership inference attacker’s advantage at e to the epsilon minus 1, which links this chapter back to the attacks.

One DP-SGD step
  1. Sample a batch: each example is included with probability q = batch size / dataset size.
  2. For each example compute its own gradient gi.
  3. Clip: gi ← gi · min(1, C / ‖gi‖).
  4. Add up the clipped gradients and add noise N(0, σ2C2) to each coordinate.
  5. Divide by the batch size and take the step w ← w − η · noisy average.
  6. The accountant records q, σ and the step, and updates the total (ε, δ).

Differential privacy compared with other privacy methods

Differential privacy is often compared with older anonymisation methods such as k anonymity, and with cryptographic tools. They answer different questions.

Holds against extra knowledge?NoNo, linkage and homogeneity attacksYes, whatever the attacker knowsYes, for the data in transit or in use

CostNoneGeneralised, less useful dataNoise, a privacy budgetComputation and engineering

Bottom line: Use cryptography to protect data while it is stored, sent or computed on, and differential privacy to protect what the published result reveals.

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that removing names is not enough and that adding the right amount of noise can protect people while keeping statistics useful. A student can apply randomised response, the Laplace and Gaussian mechanisms and composition, and run DP-SGD. A professional chooses epsilon, sets budgets and deploys DP in analytics and ML. Experts design new mechanisms and tighter accountants.
One idea, four depths

Data with names removed can often be matched back to people. Adding the right amount of random noise lets researchers learn about groups while protecting each person.

ε-DP definition, sensitivity, Laplace b = Δ/ε, randomised response ε = ln 3, Gaussian σ, composition (sum, max, kε), exponential mechanism, DP-SGD clip and noise, Adv ≤ eε − 1.

Use libraries such as OpenDP, Google’s DP library, Opacus or TensorFlow Privacy, choose ε and δ with stakeholders, track budgets, and combine DP with secure aggregation in FL.

Research tighter accounting, private synthetic data, DP for large language models, fairness under DP, and the policy question of what ε society should accept.
