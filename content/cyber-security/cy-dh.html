Lesson 1 of 11

The key exchange problem and Diffie-Hellman

Until the 1970s, every secret message required a key delivered in advance, by courier, in a sealed bag, or in a meeting. Militaries and banks spent fortunes moving keys around. In 1974 Ralph Merkle, then an undergraduate at Berkeley, proposed a way to agree on a key in public using puzzles, and his professor rejected the idea. Two years later, at Stanford, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published New Directions in Cryptography. It showed how two people who have never met can agree on a secret key while an eavesdropper hears every word. Hellman later asked that the method be called Diffie-Hellman-Merkle, to credit Merkle’s part. At GCHQ, Malcolm Williamson had found the same method in secret around 1974. Diffie and Hellman received the Turing Award in 2015. Their exchange is still inside almost every secure connection today, in TLS, SSH, IPsec VPNs and the Signal messaging protocol, usually in its elliptic curve form, which comes in the next chapter.

Source: Diffie and Hellman, New Directions in Cryptography (1976), ElGamal (1985), Pohlig and Hellman (1978), RFC 3526, RFC 7919, RFC 5869 (HKDF), RFC 8446 (TLS 1.3), Adrian and others, Imperfect Forward Secrecy: How Diffie-Hellman Fails in Practice (Logjam, 2015).

What it is
Diffie-Hellman key exchange lets two parties agree on a shared secret over a public channel. Each picks a secret exponent and sends g raised to it modulo a prime. Each raises what they receive to their own secret, and both get gab.
Why we need it
It solves key distribution: no courier and no earlier meeting. With fresh exponents for every session it also gives forward secrecy, so a key stolen later cannot unlock recorded traffic.
How it works
Security rests on the discrete logarithm problem: from ga mod p it seems infeasible to find a when p is large and well chosen.

Lesson 2 of 11

Groups, generators and safe primes

To understand why Diffie-Hellman is built the way it is, we need a little group theory. Take a prime p and look at the numbers from 1 to p minus 1 under multiplication modulo p. Multiplying two of them gives another one, every number has an inverse, and 1 does nothing. That makes them a group, written Z p star, with p minus 1 elements. The order of an element g is the smallest k with g to the k equal to 1. If the order is p minus 1, then g is a generator, and its powers visit every element. Joseph-Louis Lagrange proved in the 1770s a fact that matters a lot here: the order of any element divides the size of the group. So if p minus 1 has small factors, there are small subgroups, and as you will see in the Pohlig-Hellman lesson, small subgroups leak secrets. That is why careful designs use a safe prime, p equal to 2q plus 1 with q also prime. Then the only subgroups have sizes 1, 2, q and 2q, and we work in the subgroup of size q, which is huge. The standard 2048 bit group 14 from RFC 3526 is built this way, with g equal to 2.
What it is
A cyclic group is generated by one element g: every element is gk. The order of g is the smallest k > 0 with gk = 1. A safe prime is p = 2q + 1 with q prime.
Why we need it
The discrete log is only as hard as the subgroup it lives in. Small subgroups make it easy, so the group must have a large prime order subgroup.
How it works
Pick a safe prime p. Squares mod p form the subgroup of prime order q. Use a generator g of that subgroup, for example g = 4, or g = 2 when 2 is a square, as in RFC 3526.
Order of an elementExam favourite
ord(g) = smallest k > 0 with gk ≡ 1 (mod p)
Mod 23: ord(5) = 22, so 5 is a generator. ord(2) = 11, because 211 = 2048 = 89 × 23 + 1.

In the exam
Exam favourite: "Is g a generator modulo p?" Factor p − 1, then test g(p−1)/r for each prime factor r. Do not list all the powers.
Lesson 3 of 11

The exchange step by step

Here is the exchange itself. Alice and Bob first agree, in public, on a prime p and a generator g. Alice picks a secret number a and sends Bob A equal to g to the a, modulo p. Bob picks a secret b and sends Alice B equal to g to the b. Now Alice raises B to her secret a, and Bob raises A to his secret b. Both get g to the a times b. The eavesdropper, called Eve in cryptography papers, sees p, g, A and B, but not a or b. Getting the key from what she sees is called the computational Diffie-Hellman problem, and the only known way to solve it is to compute a discrete logarithm. The animation uses the classic example with p equal to 23 and g equal to 5. Then the program repeats the exchange with the real 2048 bit prime from RFC 3526, the one used by many VPN and SSH servers. One important detail: the shared number is not used as a key directly. It goes through a key derivation function, which turns it into an AES key, as the forward secrecy lesson shows.

Lesson 4 of 11

The discrete logarithm problem and how it is attacked

The discrete logarithm problem asks: given g, p and h, find x with g to the x equal to h modulo p. With real numbers, logarithms are easy, because powers grow smoothly. Modulo p, the powers jump around with no visible pattern, so there is no shortcut from size. Brute force tries x equal to 1, 2, 3 and so on, up to p steps. In 1971 Daniel Shanks found baby step giant step, which needs only about the square root of p steps, at the cost of storing a table of the same size. John Pollard’s rho method from 1978 gets the same square root time with almost no memory, and it is the best known attack on elliptic curves. For groups modulo a prime there is something much better, index calculus and the number field sieve, which are sub exponential, just like factoring. That is why finite field Diffie-Hellman needs keys as long as RSA, 2048 or 3072 bits. In 2015 the Logjam team showed that one huge precomputation for a single prime lets an attacker then solve each new log in that group quickly. For 512 bit export grade groups, the precomputation took about a week and each log about a minute. They estimated that a nation state could do the same for one widely shared 1024 bit prime.
What it is
The discrete logarithm of h to base g modulo p is the x with gx ≡ h (mod p). The DLP is finding it.
Why we need it
Diffie-Hellman, ElGamal, DSA and Schnorr signatures are only as strong as the DLP in their group. Key sizes follow from the best attacks.
How it works
Baby step giant step: write x = i m + j with m ≈ √n. Store gj, then step h · g−mi until it hits the table. Pollard rho: a random walk that finds a collision in about √n steps with tiny memory.

Lesson 5 of 11

Small subgroups: Pohlig-Hellman and bad public values

In 1978 Stephen Pohlig and Martin Hellman showed that the size of the prime is not the whole story. What matters is the largest prime factor of the group order. If p minus 1 splits into small primes, an attacker solves the discrete log separately inside each small subgroup, where it takes only a few steps, and then joins the pieces with the Chinese remainder theorem. The program in this lesson breaks a 63 bit discrete log in a few hundred steps because p minus 1 is a product of tiny primes. A related danger comes from the values the other side sends. If Bob blindly accepts A equal to 1, his key is 1. If he accepts p minus 1, his key is 1 or p minus 1. And if he reuses his secret with a group that has small subgroups, an attacker can send elements of small order and learn his secret a few bits at a time, an attack described by Chae Hoon Lim and Pil Joong Lee in 1997. In 2016 OpenSSL fixed a high severity bug of exactly this kind. The defences are simple: use a safe prime group, check every received value, and use fresh secrets each time.
What it is
Pohlig-Hellman solves a discrete log in a group of order n = ∏ riei by solving it in each subgroup of order riei and joining the results with CRT.
Why we need it
It shows that the group order must have a large prime factor, ideally be prime, and it powers small subgroup attacks on careless implementations.
How it works
For each prime power f dividing n: gf = gn/f, hf = hn/f, find x mod f with gfx = hf. Then CRT gives x mod n.

Lesson 6 of 11

Man in the middle and authenticated key exchange

Diffie-Hellman protects against someone who only listens. It does nothing against someone who can change messages. Mallory, sitting on the network, catches Alice’s value and sends Bob her own instead, and does the same in the other direction. Alice ends up sharing a key with Mallory, and so does Bob. Mallory decrypts every message, reads or changes it, and encrypts it again for the other side. Nobody sees an error. The cure is authentication: each side must prove who sent the public values. In TLS, the server signs the whole handshake with the private key of its certificate, and the browser checks the certificate chain up to a trusted authority. In the Station to Station protocol from 1992, by Diffie, Paul van Oorschot and Michael Wiener, both sides sign. Messaging apps such as Signal and WhatsApp let users compare a safety number, which is a fingerprint of their keys, in person or by a call. If Mallory is in the middle, the numbers differ.

What it is
A man in the middle (MITM) relays and changes messages between two parties. Against unauthenticated Diffie-Hellman she sets up two separate keys and reads everything.
Why we need it
Without authentication, key exchange only stops passive eavesdroppers. Active attackers on Wi-Fi, ISPs or compromised routers can intercept.
How it works
Bind the exchange to identities: signatures with certificates (TLS, SSH host keys), a pre shared key, or a human fingerprint check (safety numbers).

The very first SSH connection to a new server shows a fingerprint and asks you to accept it. That is the only moment a man in the middle can slip in, so check the fingerprint with the server owner.
Lesson 7 of 11

ElGamal encryption

In 1985 Taher Elgamal, who later led the team that created SSL at Netscape, turned Diffie-Hellman into public key encryption. The receiver’s long term key is h equal to g to the x. To send a message, the sender makes a one time Diffie-Hellman exchange with that key: pick a random k, send c1 equal to g to the k, and multiply the message by h to the k, which only the receiver can recompute as c1 to the x. Because k is random, the same message gives a different ciphertext each time, unlike textbook RSA. ElGamal is also homomorphic for multiplication: multiplying two ciphertexts gives an encryption of the product of the messages. That property is useful in electronic voting and mix networks, and dangerous anywhere an attacker could change ciphertexts. The randomness must never repeat. If k is used twice, one known message reveals the other. ElGamal’s signature scheme, also from 1985, led to DSA, whose own nonce rule, broken by Sony in the PlayStation 3, is covered in the elliptic curve chapter.
What it is
ElGamal encryption: public key h = gx. Encrypt m with random k as (c1, c2) = (gk, m · hk). Decrypt m = c2 · (c1x)−1.
Why we need it
It gives randomized public key encryption from the discrete log, and its multiplicative homomorphism is used in voting systems and threshold cryptography.
How it works
c1x = gkx = hk, so dividing c2 by it leaves m. In the prime order subgroup it is IND-CPA secure under DDH, but it is malleable, so it is not CCA secure without extra steps.

Lesson 8 of 11

Forward secrecy, key derivation and TLS 1.3

Suppose an agency records all your encrypted traffic for years, and one day steals your server’s private key. With RSA key transport, where the client encrypts the session key to the server’s RSA key, every recorded session can now be decrypted. With ephemeral Diffie-Hellman, each session used fresh secrets that were deleted afterwards, so the stolen long term key, which was only used to sign, unlocks nothing from the past. This property is called forward secrecy. After the Snowden revelations in 2013, major sites switched to ephemeral key exchange, and TLS 1.3, published in 2018, made it mandatory by removing RSA key transport altogether. Two more details matter. First, the raw shared value is not uniformly random, so it is passed through a key derivation function. HKDF, designed by Hugo Krawczyk in 2010, extracts the randomness and expands it into as many keys as needed, separate ones for each direction. Second, Signal goes further with its double ratchet, which mixes in a new Diffie-Hellman exchange as messages flow, so even a stolen phone state heals itself after a few messages.
What it is
Forward secrecy: compromise of long term keys does not reveal past session keys. It comes from ephemeral Diffie-Hellman, where session secrets are generated fresh and deleted.
Why we need it
Encrypted traffic is often recorded and kept. Forward secrecy limits the damage of a later break in or legal seizure of keys.
How it works
Each handshake: new secrets a and b, exchange, sign the transcript with the long term key, derive keys with HKDF, then erase a and b. HKDF-Extract(salt, shared) gives a pseudorandom key, HKDF-Expand(key, info, length) gives the working keys.

Lesson 9 of 11

Diffie-Hellman compared

Key exchange can be done in several ways, and each has a place. Finite field Diffie-Hellman, the version in this chapter, is well understood and has standard groups, but needs large keys because of the number field sieve. Elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman, especially X25519, does the same job with 32 byte keys and is now the default almost everywhere. RSA key transport is simpler but has no forward secrecy and is gone from TLS 1.3. Pre shared keys avoid public key maths entirely but need the keys delivered in advance, the very problem Diffie and Hellman solved. And ML-KEM, the new post quantum standard, is a key encapsulation mechanism rather than an exchange, but it plays the same role, and is already deployed in hybrid mode with X25519 in major browsers.

Lesson 10 of 11

From beginner to expert

Diffie-Hellman connects school arithmetic to some of the deepest questions in cryptography. A beginner learns that two people can agree on a secret in public, and that this is what the padlock in a browser relies on. A college student can run the exchange by hand, test generators, explain the discrete log problem, use baby step giant step and spot a man in the middle. A masters student studies the CDH and DDH assumptions and the reductions between them, the generic group model and Shoup’s square root lower bound, Pohlig-Hellman and index calculus, and authenticated key exchange models such as Canetti and Krawczyk’s. At the research level, people prove the security of real protocols such as TLS 1.3 and Signal with formal tools like Tamarin and ProVerif, study the number field sieve for discrete logs, and design key exchange that survives quantum computers, from lattices and from isogenies. One isogeny scheme, SIKE, was a strong post quantum candidate until Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru broke it in 2022 with a single laptop in about an hour.
One idea, four depths

Two people can agree on a secret key in public. Each keeps one secret number and sends one public number. The padlock in your browser uses this, and it only stops attackers if the other side is also proved genuine.

Run the exchange by hand, test a generator with g(p−1)/r, explain CDH and the DLP, solve small logs with baby step giant step, break a smooth group with Pohlig-Hellman, and show how a man in the middle and a fingerprint check work.

CDH, DDH and their relations, why DDH fails in Zp*, the generic group model and the √q bound, index calculus, ElGamal IND-CPA from DDH, authenticated key exchange (SIGMA, the Canetti and Krawczyk model), HKDF and the TLS 1.3 key schedule.

Formal verification of TLS 1.3 and Signal (Tamarin, ProVerif), discrete log records with the number field sieve (795 bits in 2019), precomputation attacks (Logjam), isogeny based key exchange and the 2022 break of SIKE, and post quantum KEMs as replacements.

Chapter summary

  1. Diffie-Hellman (1976): public p, g. A = ga, B = gb, key K = Ba = Ab = gab. With p = 23, g = 5, a = 6, b = 15: A = 8, B = 19, K = 2.
  2. Groups: ord(g) divides p − 1 (Lagrange). Test a generator with g(p−1)/r ≠ 1. Use a safe prime p = 2q + 1 and the subgroup of order q.
  3. DLP: find x from gx. Generic attacks (BSGS, Pollard rho) take about √q. Index calculus and the NFS are sub exponential mod p, so use 2048 to 3072 bit groups.
  4. Pohlig-Hellman: the largest prime factor of the order sets the cost. Validate received values: 1 < A < p − 1 and Aq = 1.
  5. Man in the middle: plain DH has no authentication. Sign the handshake (TLS, SSH) or compare fingerprints (safety numbers).
  6. ElGamal: (gk, m hk). Randomized and multiplicatively homomorphic. Never reuse k.
  7. Forward secrecy from ephemeral keys. Derive keys with HKDF. TLS 1.3 requires (EC)DHE and removed RSA key transport.
  8. Logjam (2015): 512 bit export groups broken, shared 1024 bit primes at risk. Prefer X25519 or hybrids with ML-KEM.
