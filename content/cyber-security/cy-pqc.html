Lesson 1 of 9

The quantum threat and why we must move now

In 1994 Peter Shor, then at Bell Labs, showed that a quantum computer could factor large numbers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time. That one result threatens RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves, which means almost all the public key cryptography on the internet. Two years later Lov Grover found a quantum search algorithm that speeds up brute force from n steps to about the square root of n, which halves the effective strength of symmetric keys. No quantum computer today comes close to breaking real keys. The largest machines have around a thousand noisy physical qubits, and an attack on RSA-2048 is estimated to need about a million. So why worry now? Because of harvest now, decrypt later: an adversary can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once a quantum computer exists. Michele Mosca put this as a simple inequality. If the years your data must stay secret plus the years your migration takes are more than the years until a quantum computer arrives, you are already late. In August 2024 NIST published the first three post quantum standards, and the move has begun across browsers, messaging apps and operating systems.

What it is
Post quantum cryptography (PQC) means classical algorithms, run on ordinary computers, whose security rests on problems that quantum computers are not known to solve quickly, such as lattice problems and hash functions.
Why we need it
Shor’s algorithm breaks RSA, DH and ECC. Data recorded today may be decrypted later, so long lived secrets need protection now.
How it works
Replace key exchange with ML-KEM (often as a hybrid with X25519) and signatures with ML-DSA, SLH-DSA or FN-DSA. Use AES-256 and SHA-384 or larger for long term symmetric security.

Lesson 2 of 9

How Shor’s algorithm breaks RSA

Shor’s algorithm turns factoring into a different question: finding the period of a sequence. Pick a random number a and look at a, a squared, a cubed and so on, all modulo N. Because there are only N possible values, the sequence must repeat, and the length of the cycle is called the period r. Once r is known, and if it is even, a to the r minus 1 is divisible by N, and it splits as a to the r over 2 minus 1, times a to the r over 2 plus 1. Each factor usually shares exactly one prime with N, and Euclid’s algorithm pulls it out. Finding r is the hard part. A classical computer has to walk through the sequence, which takes about as long as factoring. A quantum computer prepares all exponents at once in superposition, computes a to the x modulo N for all of them together, and then applies the quantum Fourier transform, which turns the hidden repetition into a measurable frequency. The same idea computes discrete logarithms, which breaks Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves. In 2001 IBM used a seven qubit machine to factor 15 this way. Factoring a 2048 bit number needs error corrected logical qubits, built from many physical ones, which is the engineering challenge still ahead.

Lesson 3 of 9

Lattices and Learning With Errors

Most of the new standards rest on lattices. A lattice is a regular grid of points in many dimensions, all whole number combinations of a few basis vectors. Two problems on lattices seem hard even for quantum computers: finding the shortest non zero vector, and finding the lattice point closest to a given point, once there are hundreds of dimensions. In 2005 Oded Regev introduced a problem called Learning With Errors that is easy to state and is linked to those lattice problems. You are given many random linear equations in a secret vector s, modulo a small number q, but each answer has a small random error added. Without the errors, ordinary Gaussian elimination from school would find s in moments. With the errors, all known algorithms, classical and quantum, take exponential time. Regev proved that solving LWE on average would solve worst case lattice problems with a quantum computer, and he received the Gödel Prize in 2018. Encryption works because the owner of s can remove almost everything except the small noise and the message, which sits either near zero or near q over 2.
What it is
Learning With Errors (LWE): given A and b = A s + e mod q, with random A and small error e, find s. Its decision version: tell b apart from random.
Why we need it
It gives encryption and signatures believed secure against quantum computers, with worst case to average case reductions to lattice problems, and it is fast.
How it works
To encrypt a bit, combine a random subset of the public samples and add the bit times q/2. To decrypt, subtract ⟨u, s⟩. What is left is the bit times q/2 plus a small sum of errors. Round to the nearest of 0 or q/2.

Ring and module LWE
replace numbers by polynomials in Zq[x]/(xn + 1)
One polynomial product does the work of an n by n matrix, so keys shrink from megabytes to about a kilobyte.
Going deeper: Why the noise is essential

If e = 0, then b = A s mod q is a linear system. With m ≥ n equations, Gaussian elimination mod q recovers s in O(n3) steps. Adding small noise makes each equation slightly wrong, and errors compound when equations are combined, so elimination fails. The best known attacks instead look for short vectors in a related lattice using BKZ lattice reduction, whose cost grows exponentially with the dimension. Parameters are chosen so this cost exceeds 2128 or more, also against quantum versions of these attacks.

Lesson 4 of 9

ML-KEM (Kyber): the new key exchange

ML-KEM, published as FIPS 203 in August 2024, is the standardised form of CRYSTALS-Kyber, designed by an international team and chosen by NIST in 2022 after a six year public competition that started with 82 submissions. It is a key encapsulation mechanism, or KEM, which is slightly different from Diffie-Hellman. The receiver publishes a public key. The sender runs Encaps, which makes a fresh random 32 byte secret and a ciphertext that wraps it. The receiver runs Decaps on the ciphertext and gets the same secret. Inside, ML-KEM uses module LWE over polynomials with 256 coefficients modulo the prime 3329, and a transform by Fujisaki and Okamoto that makes it secure even against attackers who can feed it chosen ciphertexts. It is fast, often faster than X25519, but its keys and ciphertexts are about a kilobyte each instead of 32 bytes. Because the new schemes have had less time under attack than elliptic curves, the first deployments are hybrids: X25519MLKEM768 combines both shared secrets, so the connection is safe if either one holds. Chrome turned it on by default in 2024, and OpenSSH 10 made a similar hybrid its default in 2025.

What it is
ML-KEM (FIPS 203) is a lattice based key encapsulation mechanism: KeyGen gives (ek, dk), Encaps(ek) gives (K, c), Decaps(dk, c) gives K.
Why we need it
It replaces Diffie-Hellman and RSA key transport for establishing session keys, safe against quantum computers as far as anyone knows.
How it works
Public key t = A s + e with A from a seed. Encrypt with u = AT r + e1, v = tT r + e2 + ⌈q/2⌋ m. Decrypt with v − sT u. The FO transform re-encrypts during Decaps to reject tampered ciphertexts.

Lesson 5 of 9

Post quantum signatures: ML-DSA, SLH-DSA and FN-DSA

For signatures, NIST standardised two schemes in 2024 and has a third on the way. ML-DSA, FIPS 204, is the standard form of CRYSTALS-Dilithium. It is a lattice signature built with a technique called Fiat-Shamir with aborts, introduced by Vadim Lyubashevsky: the signer computes a candidate signature, and if it would leak information about the secret key, throws it away and tries again. ML-DSA is fast and is the general purpose choice, but its signatures are about 2.4 to 4.6 kilobytes, compared with 64 bytes for Ed25519. SLH-DSA, FIPS 205, is the standard form of SPHINCS+. It relies only on hash functions, the most conservative assumption in cryptography, with public keys of just 32 bytes, but signatures of 8 to 50 kilobytes and slow signing. It grew from Leslie Lamport’s one time signatures of 1979 and Ralph Merkle’s tree of 1979. FN-DSA, based on Falcon, gives much smaller lattice signatures but needs delicate floating point code. Not every candidate survived: in 2022 Ward Beullens broke the multivariate scheme Rainbow over a weekend on a laptop, and Castryck and Decru broke the isogeny scheme SIKE in about an hour.

Migrating to post quantum cryptography

Replacing the public key cryptography of the whole world is one of the largest engineering projects in computing, and it is happening now. The steps are well understood. First, make an inventory: find every place that uses RSA, Diffie-Hellman or elliptic curves, in protocols, libraries, certificates, hardware and contracts. Second, build crypto agility, the ability to swap algorithms by configuration instead of rewriting code. Third, protect key exchange first, because recorded traffic is at risk today, while signatures only need to be replaced before a quantum computer exists. Hybrids are the bridge. Signal added the PQXDH protocol in 2023, Apple’s iMessage moved to PQ3 in 2024, and Chrome, Firefox, Cloudflare and OpenSSH all negotiate hybrid key exchange by default. Governments have set deadlines: the NSA’s CNSA 2.0 expects national security systems to use quantum resistant algorithms by about 2033, and NIST’s draft IR 8547 would disallow RSA and elliptic curves after 2035. One alternative often mentioned, quantum key distribution, uses physics instead of maths, but it needs special hardware and trusted relay points, and agencies such as the NSA and the UK’s NCSC do not recommend it for general use.
A migration plan
  1. Inventory every use of RSA, DH, ECDH, ECDSA and EdDSA, including in vendors’ products and hardware.
  2. Rank by data lifetime: anything secret for 10 or more years goes first (harvest now, decrypt later).
  3. Turn on hybrid key exchange (X25519MLKEM768) in TLS, SSH and VPNs wherever supported.
  4. Design for crypto agility: algorithm choice in configuration, larger buffers for kilobyte keys and signatures.
  5. Plan the signature move: ML-DSA for general use, SLH-DSA or LMS for firmware and roots of trust.
  6. Raise symmetric strength: AES-256, SHA-384 or larger, for long term secrets.
Remember
Post quantum keys and signatures are larger. A TLS handshake with ML-KEM-768 adds about 2 KB, which can push the first flight over one network packet and expose old middleboxes that drop large ClientHellos. Test before rollout.
Lesson 7 of 9

Post quantum options compared

The families of post quantum schemes trade size, speed and confidence. Lattice schemes, ML-KEM and ML-DSA, are fast with keys of around one or two kilobytes, and they are the default choices. Hash based signatures are the most trusted, because they only need a good hash function, but they are large or need careful state handling. Code based encryption such as Classic McEliece, from 1978, has never been broken, but its public keys are hundreds of kilobytes to over a megabyte. HQC, another code based KEM, was chosen by NIST in March 2025 as a backup to ML-KEM built on different maths. Multivariate and isogeny schemes have suffered recent breaks, so they are mainly research topics today. The guiding idea is diversity: if a new attack hits lattices, a hash based or code based fallback must be ready.

Lesson 8 of 9

From beginner to expert

Post quantum cryptography is where the newest standards meet active research. A beginner learns that a future quantum computer could break today’s public key cryptography, that data recorded now could be decrypted later, and that new standards already exist and are switched on in browsers. A college student can explain Shor’s period finding and Grover’s square root speed up, run toy LWE and Lamport signatures, and describe what a KEM is and why hybrids are used. A masters student studies lattice problems and reductions, ring and module LWE, the number theoretic transform that makes polynomial multiplication fast, the Fujisaki-Okamoto transform, Fiat-Shamir with aborts, and the BKZ algorithm that sets the parameters. At the research level, people estimate the real cost of lattice attacks, prove security in the quantum random oracle model, design side channel resistant implementations, verify them formally, and estimate how many qubits and how much time Shor’s algorithm really needs.
One idea, four depths

Quantum computers could one day break RSA and elliptic curves. New post quantum standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) are already used by browsers and messaging apps, often together with the old ones.

Explain Shor and Grover, Mosca’s inequality, LWE encryption, the KEM interface, Lamport and Merkle signatures, the parameter tables, and how hybrid key exchange works.

SVP, CVP and Regev’s reduction, ring and module LWE, the NTT, FO and its implicit rejection, Fiat-Shamir with aborts, BKZ and the core SVP cost model, and constant time implementation (KyberSlash).

Concrete lattice attack estimates, QROM proofs, masking against side channels, formally verified PQC (libcrux, the Jasmin and EasyCrypt projects), isogeny research after SIKE, and quantum resource estimates for Shor.

Intermediate

Expert

Chapter summary

  1. Shor (1994) breaks RSA, DH and ECC by period finding. Grover (1996) halves symmetric strength: use AES-256 for long term secrets.
  2. Harvest now, decrypt later. Mosca: if x + y > z, act now.
  3. Shor’s maths: period r of ax mod N, then gcd(ar/2 ± 1, N). 15 with a = 7: r = 4, factors 3 and 5.
  4. LWE: b = A s + e mod q. Noise makes it hard. Decrypt by rounding to 0 or q/2 while |noise| < q/4.
  5. ML-KEM (FIPS 203): module LWE, q = 3329, n = 256. ML-KEM-768: 1184 byte key, 1088 byte ciphertext. Deploy as hybrid X25519MLKEM768.
  6. Signatures: ML-DSA (FIPS 204, 2.4 to 4.6 KB), SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, hash based, 8 to 50 KB), FN-DSA (Falcon). Lamport keys sign once.
  7. Broken candidates: Rainbow and SIKE (2022). HQC chosen as a backup KEM (2025).
  8. Migrate: inventory, crypto agility, key exchange first, then signatures. CNSA 2.0 by about 2033, NIST IR 8547 draft disallows RSA and ECC after 2035.
