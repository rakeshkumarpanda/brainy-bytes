Lesson 1 of 11

What a blockchain is, and what it protects

Imagine a notebook that the whole village keeps together. Every time someone pays someone, the payment is written on a new page, and every family keeps an identical copy. Nobody can rip out an old page, because everyone else still has it, and every page carries a seal that depends on the page before. That shared notebook is a blockchain. The idea has two parents. In 1991 Stuart Haber and Scott Stornetta showed how to time stamp documents by linking each one to the fingerprint of the one before, and their company later printed those fingerprints every week in the classified adverts of the New York Times so that nobody could quietly change history. Then on 31 October 2008 someone using the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a nine page paper called Bitcoin, a peer to peer electronic cash system. It added the missing piece: a way for strangers on the internet who do not trust each other to agree on one notebook, without a bank in the middle. The first block was mined on 3 January 2009, and it carries a newspaper headline about a bank bailout, a quiet comment on why the system was built. For security people a blockchain is interesting because it protects integrity and availability very strongly, but it gives almost no confidentiality, and the people and programs around it are often the weak point. Most money lost in this world was not lost by breaking the chain. It was lost through stolen keys, buggy programs and tricked people.

Source: Haber and Stornetta (1991), Nakamoto (2008), the Bitcoin genesis block (2009), Merkle (1979), Eyal and Sirer (2014), Heilman et al. (2015), Meiklejohn et al. (2013), BIP 32 and BIP 39, public reports on Mt. Gox (2014), Bitcoin Gold (2018), Ethereum Classic (2019, 2020), WazirX (2024) and Bybit (2025).

What it is
A blockchain is a list of records (blocks) shared by many computers, where each block includes the hash of the one before it, and a consensus rule decides which new block everyone accepts next.
Why we need it
It lets people who do not trust each other keep one shared history that nobody can secretly rewrite, without a central bank, court or company keeping the book.
How it works
Hash links make old blocks tamper evident, Merkle trees summarise every payment in one hash, digital signatures prove who may spend, and proof of work or proof of stake makes rewriting history very expensive.

Real world scenario: Around you

When a friend sends you money on UPI, your bank and theirs keep the record and the NPCI system settles it. In Bitcoin there is no bank. Thousands of computers each keep the record, and they agree on it using maths. That is why a lost Bitcoin password cannot be reset by calling customer care.

Lesson 2 of 11

Blocks and hash links: why old pages cannot be changed

Let us build a chain ourselves. A block is a small box of data. In Bitcoin it holds a header of 80 bytes and the list of payments. The header includes the hash of the previous block header. A hash function such as SHA-256, which you met in the hashing chapter, turns any input into a 256 bit fingerprint, and changing even one letter of the input changes about half of the output bits. So if a cheater edits a payment in block 1, the fingerprint of block 1 changes, and block 2, which stored the old fingerprint, no longer matches. Every computer checking the chain spots this in microseconds. Of course the cheater could recompute the fingerprint of block 2 and every later block too. On its own a hash chain only makes tampering visible, it does not make it hard. That is why the next lesson adds work to every block. In the animation the hashes are real SHA-256 values computed in your browser, so try typing your own payments and see every later fingerprint change. In August 2010 a bug let someone create 184 billion bitcoins in block 74638 through an integer overflow, a mistake in the software rather than in the chain. Developers released a fix within hours and the network moved to a corrected chain that left the bad block behind, which shows that the chain is only as safe as the code that checks it.

How a node checks a chain
  1. Start at the genesis block, which every node knows by heart.
  2. For each block, recompute its hash from its contents and compare with the stored hash.
  3. Check that its "previous" field equals the hash of the block before.
  4. Check every payment inside: valid signatures, no coin spent twice, amounts add up.
  5. Check the proof of work (or proof of stake signatures) meets the rule.
Lesson 3 of 11

Proof of work: making the past expensive to rewrite

Proof of work turns a cheap hash chain into one that is very expensive to rewrite. The rule is simple. A block is valid only if its hash is below a target, which in practice means it starts with a certain number of zeros. The miner cannot choose the hash, so they change a number called the nonce and hash again, over and over, like rolling dice until all of them show six. Each extra hex zero makes the search about sixteen times longer. Checking the answer takes one hash. In the animation, two hex zeros need about 256 tries on average. Bitcoin today needs roughly 78 zero bits, which is about 2 to the 78 hashes, so the whole world’s mining machines together find one block about every ten minutes. Every 2016 blocks, roughly two weeks, Bitcoin adjusts the target so that blocks keep coming every ten minutes whether miners join or leave. The miner who finds a block earns new coins, the block reward, which halves every 210,000 blocks, from 50 bitcoin in 2009 to 3.125 since April 2024, so there will never be more than 21 million. Rewriting a block buried under six others means redoing all that work faster than everyone else, and that cost is what protects the past. The price is energy. Bitcoin mining uses about as much electricity as a medium sized country, which is why Ethereum moved away from proof of work in 2022.

Lesson 4 of 11

Merkle trees: one fingerprint for every payment in a block

A block can hold thousands of payments, and a phone wallet cannot download them all. The trick comes from Ralph Merkle, who patented hash trees in 1979. Hash every payment, then hash the results in pairs, then pair again, until one hash is left at the top. That top hash, the Merkle root, sits in the 80 byte block header. Change any payment and the root changes. To prove that your payment is in a block, a full node sends you only the sibling hash at each level, the orange boxes in the animation, and you recompute the path to the root yourself. For a block with four thousand payments that is just twelve hashes. Satoshi called this simplified payment verification, section 8 of the Bitcoin paper, and it is how light wallets work. Certificate Transparency logs, Git and many file systems use the same tree. One real lesson from history: Bitcoin pairs an odd last hash with itself, and in 2012 developers found that this let two different lists of payments give the same root, a weakness tracked as CVE-2012-2459 that nodes now guard against by rejecting duplicated payments.

Lesson 5 of 11

Consensus, forks, double spending and the 51% attack

Sometimes two miners find a block at almost the same moment, and for a short while the network has two competing tips, a fork. The rule is to follow the chain with the most total work. Within a block or two one side pulls ahead and the other block becomes an orphan, so a payment is never treated as final the instant it appears. Now picture a cheater. They pay a shop, and at the same time they secretly mine their own chain in which that payment never happened. If their secret chain ever catches up and pulls ahead, they publish it, every node switches, and the coins return to the cheater, who has the goods as well. That is a double spend. The chance depends on q, the attacker’s share of all mining power, and z, the number of blocks the shop waits for. Nakamoto worked out the formula in section 11 of his paper. With ten percent of the power and six confirmations, the chance is about 0.02 percent. With more than half the power, the attacker wins in the end whatever the shop does, which is why it is called a 51 percent attack. This is not only theory. Bitcoin Gold lost about 18 million dollars to double spends in May 2018. Ethereum Classic was attacked in January 2019, then three times in August 2020, once with a reorganisation of more than 3,600 blocks. Small chains are at risk because renting enough computing power for an hour can cost only a few thousand dollars.

Lesson 6 of 11

Proof of stake: security from locked money instead of electricity

Proof of stake replaces electricity with money put at risk. People who want to help run the chain, called validators, lock up coins as a deposit. On Ethereum each validator locks 32 ether. Time is cut into twelve second slots, and in each slot one validator is picked at random to propose a block while committees of others vote, called attestations, that it is correct. Thirty two slots make an epoch of 6.4 minutes, and once two thirds of all staked ether has voted for a checkpoint across two epochs, it is finalised, which means reversing it would destroy at least one third of all the stake. If a validator cheats, for example by signing two different blocks for the same slot, anyone can submit the two signatures as proof and the validator is slashed, losing part of the deposit and being thrown out. Ethereum switched from proof of work to proof of stake on 15 September 2022 in an upgrade called the Merge, and its energy use fell by more than 99.9 percent. Proof of stake brings its own risks. Validators must keep their signing keys online, so key security matters even more. A few large staking services control a big share of the stake, which raises questions of centralisation. And long range attacks, where old keys are bought to build a fake history from long ago, are handled by checkpoints that new nodes must trust.

Lesson 7 of 11

Wallets and keys: where most money is actually lost

Here is the most important truth of this chapter. A wallet does not hold coins. The coins are entries on the chain. A wallet holds the private key that can sign a payment moving them. Bitcoin and Ethereum both use the elliptic curve secp256k1 from the elliptic curve chapter. The private key is a 256 bit random number, the public key is that number times a fixed point on the curve, and the address is a hash of the public key. Whoever knows the private key owns the coins, and there is no reset button. To make keys easier to back up, BIP 39 turns 128 random bits plus a 4 bit checksum into 12 words from a list of 2048, and BIP 32 grows a whole tree of keys from that one seed. Guessing 128 random bits is impossible, but a phrase that a person makes up, a so called brain wallet, can be found in seconds, and early brain wallets were emptied by bots within minutes. History is full of key disasters. In February 2014 Mt. Gox, then the biggest exchange, collapsed after losing about 850,000 bitcoin over years of poor key handling. In July 2024 the Indian exchange WazirX lost about 230 million dollars when attackers tricked the signers of a multi signature wallet into approving a malicious change. On 21 February 2025 the exchange Bybit lost about 1.5 billion dollars in ether, the largest theft in this field so far, after attackers broke into a developer machine of its wallet provider and changed what the signers saw on screen. The FBI attributed it to North Korea. None of these broke the maths. They broke computers and people around the keys.

Lesson 8 of 11

Privacy and crime: pseudonyms, tracing and ransomware

People often think Bitcoin is anonymous. It is not. It is pseudonymous: your name is not written on the chain, but every payment between addresses is public forever. Researchers led by Sarah Meiklejohn showed in 2013 that simple rules link many addresses to one owner. The best known is the common input rule: when several coins are spent together in one transaction, the same person almost always controls all of them. Add a few addresses whose owners are known, such as exchange deposit addresses, and whole flows of money become visible. Companies now sell this blockchain analysis to police and banks. In May 2021 the Colonial Pipeline company in the United States paid a ransom of 75 bitcoin. A month later the Department of Justice announced it had seized 63.7 of those bitcoin by following them to a wallet whose key it obtained. Criminals respond with mixers, which pool coins from many users, and privacy coins such as Monero, which hide amounts and senders with cryptography. Governments respond with sanctions and with rules that exchanges must know their customers, called KYC. Two other crimes are worth knowing. Cryptojacking is malware or a hidden script in a website that uses your computer to mine coins for someone else, which the Coinhive script did on thousands of hacked sites until 2019. And investment scams and rug pulls, where a project’s creators run off with investors’ money, take more money from ordinary people every year than technical hacks.

Lesson 9 of 11

Blockchain compared with an ordinary database

A blockchain is a tool with a high price, so it is worth asking when it is the right one. If one trusted organisation runs the system, an ordinary database with backups and signed audit logs is faster, cheaper and private. A blockchain makes sense when many parties who do not trust each other must share one record and nobody should be able to change it alone.

Bottom line: The hash chained logs of the SIEM chapter give tamper evidence without any of the cost. Reach for a blockchain only when no single party may hold the pen.

Lesson 10 of 11

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that a blockchain is a shared notebook with linked pages and that whoever holds the private key owns the coins. A student can compute a hash chain, a Merkle proof and the double spend probability. A professional secures keys and wallets, judges confirmation counts and traces funds. Experts design consensus protocols and analyse attacks on them.
One idea, four depths

A blockchain is a shared notebook. Each page includes a seal made from the page before, so nobody can change old pages without everyone noticing. Your secret key is your money, so never share your recovery words.

Hash links hi = H(i ‖ hi−1 ‖ data), proof of work with 16k expected tries, retarget every 2016 blocks, Merkle proofs of log2 n hashes, Nakamoto’s double spend formula, BIP 39 sizes.

Run key ceremonies, hardware security modules and multisig policies, verify transactions on independent screens, monitor reorganisations, and use chain analysis in incident response.

Research consensus security (selfish mining, eclipse, long range attacks), formal proofs of protocols, privacy with zero knowledge proofs, and the economics of attacks on small chains.

Chapter summary

  1. What: a shared list of blocks, each holding the hash of the one before (Haber and Stornetta 1991, Nakamoto 2008). Strong integrity and availability, almost no confidentiality.
  2. Proof of work: find a nonce so the hash is below a target, 16k tries for k hex zeros, retarget every 2016 blocks, reward 50 / 2n, at most 21 million.
  3. Merkle trees: one root for all payments, proofs of about log2 n hashes, used by light wallets.
  4. Consensus attacks: double spend race, (q/p)z and Nakamoto’s formula, 51% attacks on Bitcoin Gold and Ethereum Classic, selfish mining, eclipse, Sybil.
  5. Proof of stake: 32 ether validators, slots, epochs, finality at two thirds, slashing. Ethereum Merge 15 September 2022.
  6. Keys: whoever holds the key owns the coins. BIP 39 12 words = 128 + 4 bits. Mt. Gox, WazirX and Bybit were key and people failures, not broken maths.
  7. Privacy: pseudonymous, not anonymous. Common input clustering, Colonial Pipeline recovery, mixers and KYC.
