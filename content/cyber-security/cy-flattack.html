Lesson 1 of 8

Who can attack a federated system?

Federated learning keeps raw data on the devices, which removes one big risk. But it opens new ones, because the server and every client now take part in training. A threat model asks who might be malicious and what they want. The server might be honest but curious, following the protocol while trying to learn about users from their updates, or fully malicious, sending clients a specially crafted model to make their updates leak more. Clients might be malicious too. In cross device FL anyone can install the app, so an attacker can control some phones, or create thousands of fake ones, which is called a Sybil attack after a 2002 paper by John Douceur. Malicious clients can try to poison the model, either to ruin it, called an untargeted or availability attack, or to plant a backdoor that only they can trigger. Some clients just want the final model without contributing, the free rider problem. And outsiders can attack the finished model with the methods from Advanced 1.2. Two goals pull against each other here. For privacy we want the server to see as little as possible about each update. For robustness we want the server to inspect updates and reject bad ones. This chapter studies the attacks on both sides on toy models, and the layered defences used in practice.

What it is
Attacks on federated learning either pull private information out of the updates (privacy attacks) or corrupt the shared model through malicious updates (poisoning and backdoors).
Why we need it
FL is used for keyboards, medical imaging and finance. A curious server could learn users’ data, and a few bad clients could plant a backdoor in a model used by millions.
How it works
Hide individual updates with secure aggregation and DP noise, limit each client’s influence with clipping and robust aggregation, authenticate clients and detect Sybils, and validate every new model before release.

Lesson 2 of 8

Privacy attacks: what one update reveals

An early surprise in federated learning was how much a single update can reveal. In 2017 Le Trieu Phong and colleagues pointed out that for a single example passing through a dense layer with a bias, the input can be read straight from the gradient. The gradient of a weight row is the error signal times the input, and the gradient of the bias is the error signal itself, so dividing one by the other gives the input back exactly. Our animation and program show it. In 2019 Ligeng Zhu, Zhijian Liu and Song Han went further with Deep Leakage from Gradients. They start with a random fake image and label, compute the gradient it would produce, and adjust the fake until its gradient matches the real one. The fake turns into the real training image, pixel for pixel, for small batches. In 2020 Jonas Geiping and colleagues rebuilt recognisable ImageNet photos from realistic updates. Other attacks learn properties rather than full inputs. Luca Melis and colleagues showed in 2019 that updates can reveal, for example, whether people in a client’s photos wear glasses, or when a certain person appears in a client’s data. And if the server is malicious it can send clients a specially chosen model that makes their updates leak even more. The defences follow from the maths. Never let the server see a single client’s update, which is what secure aggregation guarantees. Add differential privacy noise, which bounds what anyone can learn about one example or one user. Larger batches and more local steps make simple inversion harder, but they are not a defence on their own.

Going deeper: Why batches only blur

For a batch of B examples the shared gradient is the average of the per example gradients, so the division gives Σ δi xi / Σ δi, a weighted mix of inputs whose weights can be negative. Optimisation attacks treat all B inputs as unknowns and solve for them together, which works for small B. Zhao et al. (2020) also showed the label of a single example can be read from the sign of the last layer’s gradient.

Lesson 3 of 8

Poisoning and backdoors in federated learning

Poisoning is easier in federated learning than in central training, because the server cannot see the clients’ data and cannot check their labels. Data poisoning means a malicious client trains honestly on bad data, for example with flipped labels. Model poisoning is stronger: the client sends whatever update it likes. In 2020 Eugene Bagdasaryan and colleagues showed model replacement. The server adds the average of the clients’ changes to the global model. If an attacker wants the global model to become its own backdoored model X, it can send its change multiplied by n over eta, where n is the number of clients in the round and eta the server learning rate. When everyone else’s changes are small, they nearly cancel, and the new global model lands almost exactly on X, after a single round. The backdoor might make a keyboard suggest a chosen brand after certain words, or a classifier label cars with racing stripes as birds. Arjun Bhagoji and colleagues showed similar boosting attacks in 2019, and Hongyi Wang and colleagues in 2020 showed backdoors on rare edge case inputs are both effective and hard to spot. The first line of defence is norm clipping: no client’s change may be longer than a bound M. Ziteng Sun and colleagues at Google showed in 2019 that clipping plus a little Gaussian noise, the same steps used for differential privacy, stops most simple backdoors when attackers are a small fraction.

Robust aggregation: surviving Byzantine clients

Byzantine is the name computer science gives to a participant that may behave in any arbitrary way, after the Byzantine generals problem described by Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease in 1982. Robust aggregation replaces the plain average with a rule that a minority of Byzantine clients cannot drag far away. The coordinate wise median takes the middle value of each weight separately. It stays among the honest values as long as attackers are fewer than half, which is called a breakdown point of 50 percent. The trimmed mean, studied by Dong Yin and colleagues in 2018 together with the median, drops the beta largest and beta smallest values of each coordinate and averages the rest, which is safe while there are no more than beta attackers. Krum, from Peva Blanchard and colleagues in 2017, picks the single client update whose nearest neighbours are closest, and needs n greater than 2f plus 2. FLTrust, from Xiaoyu Cao and colleagues in 2021, lets the server keep a small clean dataset and trust clients in proportion to how well their update agrees with the update from that data. These rules are not magic. They throw away information from unusual but honest clients, which hurts non IID users, and clever attackers can craft updates that sit just inside the accepted range. Moran Baruch and colleagues showed in 2019 with A Little Is Enough that small coordinated shifts inside the normal spread can still steer the model, and Minghong Fang and colleagues in 2020 built attacks tuned to each robust rule.

Sybils, free riders and client checks

Robust aggregation assumes attackers are a minority. In cross device FL that assumption can fail if creating a client is free: an attacker could run thousands of fake phones and become the majority. John Douceur named this the Sybil attack in 2002, after a book about a woman with many personalities, and showed that without some central check it cannot be fully prevented. So the first defences are about identity. Real deployments ask devices to prove they are genuine, unmodified phones using hardware backed attestation, limit how many updates any account or device can send, and sample clients randomly so an attacker cannot choose to be in every round. A second defence looks at behaviour. Sybils controlled by one attacker push the same goal, so their updates point in the same direction round after round, while honest users with different data point in different directions. FoolsGold, from Clement Fung, Chris Yoon and Ivan Beschastnikh in 2018, measures the cosine similarity between clients’ update histories and lowers the weight of clients that are too similar to someone else, as our animation and program show in simplified form. A careful attacker can add noise to look diverse, so this is one layer, not a complete answer. Free riders are a quieter problem: clients who send random or stale updates to receive the final model without spending their data or battery. Checking each update’s contribution on a validation set and rewarding honest work are the usual answers.

Privacy and robustness together: a defence stack

Here is the tension in one sentence: secure aggregation hides each update from the server, but robust aggregation needs to look at each update. If the server only sees the sum, it cannot compute a median. There are three ways forward, and real systems mix them. First, rules that work on hidden updates: each client clips its own update before masking, so even the server cannot be fooled by a huge update, and validity proofs let clients prove in zero knowledge that their masked update is within the bound. Second, central differential privacy: clip, aggregate securely, then add noise, which limits both what the model reveals about any user and how much any user can change the model. Third, layered system defences that do not need to see updates at all: authentication and attestation, random client sampling, minimum round sizes, and testing every new model on a trusted validation set before release, with automatic rollback if accuracy or backdoor checks fail. Research continues on secure robust aggregation that gets the best of both. The stack in the table below is a practical starting point for a production system.

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that bad phones could teach a shared model bad habits and that updates can reveal data. A student can rebuild an input from a gradient, compute a model replacement boost, and run median, trimmed mean and Krum. A professional designs defence stacks for production FL. Experts research attacks and provable defences.
One idea, four depths

If some phones lie during training, the shared model can learn the lie. And the small changes a phone sends can sometimes reveal what was on it, so they must be hidden and limited.

Build defence stacks: attestation, client side clipping, secure aggregation with DP, robust aggregation where possible, validation and rollback. Red team FL systems with adaptive attacks.

Research gradient inversion at scale, provable robustness under non IID data, secure robust aggregation with zero knowledge proofs, and incentive design against free riders.

Chapter summary

  1. Threat model: curious or malicious server, malicious clients, Sybils, free riders, outsiders. Privacy and robustness pull in opposite directions.
  2. Leakage: one example through a dense layer: x = ∂L/∂Wr / ∂L/∂br. DLG (2019), Inverting Gradients (2020), property inference (2019). Defences: SecAgg, DP.
  3. Poisoning: label flipping, boosted model replacement L = G + (n/η)(X − G), backdoors and edge cases. Norm clipping min(1, M/‖Δ‖) plus noise.
  4. Robust aggregation: median (50% breakdown), trimmed mean (β ≥ f), Krum (n > 2f + 2), FLTrust. Adaptive attacks (A Little Is Enough) still work.
  5. Sybils and free riders: attestation, rate limits, random sampling, cosine similarity weights (FoolsGold), contribution checks.
  6. Stack: identity, client clipping, secure aggregation, DP noise, robust aggregation where visible, validation and rollback.
