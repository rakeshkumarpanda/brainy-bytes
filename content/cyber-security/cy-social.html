Lesson 1 of 9

Social engineering: hacking the person instead of the computer

Every chapter so far has been about computers. This one is about people, because attackers know that it is often easier to trick a person than to break a computer. Social engineering means persuading someone to do something that helps the attacker: click a link, share a password or an OTP, open a door, or send money. It works because it uses the same instincts that make us kind and helpful. In 1984 the psychologist Robert Cialdini described six principles of influence. Reciprocity, we want to return a favour. Commitment, once we agree to something small we keep going. Social proof, we do what others seem to do. Authority, we obey people who seem important, like a manager, a police officer or a bank. Liking, we say yes to people who are friendly or similar to us. And scarcity, we rush when something seems about to run out. Every scam message you have ever received uses at least one of these. The famous hacker Kevin Mitnick, who later became a security consultant, wrote that he got most of his access just by asking people for it on the phone. The numbers today agree. The Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report for 2024 found that a person, through an error or by being tricked, was involved in about 68 percent of breaches. In India, people receive fake KYC update messages, fake electricity disconnection notices, fake job offers and, since 2024, "digital arrest" calls, where scammers pretend to be police or customs officers on a video call and frighten people into paying. The good news is that a few simple habits stop almost all of these.

Source: Cialdini (1984), Mitnick and Simon (2002), Verizon DBIR 2024, FBI IC3 2023, RFC 7208, RFC 6376, RFC 7489, Gmail and Yahoo sender rules (2024), Zheng (2017), Tischer et al. (2016), public reports on Twitter (2020), Uber (2022), MGM Resorts (2023), the 2019 voice deepfake and the 2024 Arup deepfake call, and Indian government advisories.

What it is
Social engineering is manipulating people into giving up information, access or money. Phishing is social engineering by message: email, SMS, chat, calls or QR codes.
Why we need it
It goes around firewalls and encryption by targeting the person who already has the access. It is cheap, it scales, and it was involved in most breaches.
How it works
Slow down, check the sender, the link and the request, confirm unusual requests on a second channel, report anything odd, and use technical controls (passkeys, email authentication, filters) that still protect you when someone slips.

Real world scenario: Around you

A stranger in a delivery uniform carrying a heavy box asks you to hold the office door. Most people help. That is tailgating, and it beats a door lock without touching it. The same instinct to help makes people read out an OTP to a polite caller who says they are from the bank.

Lesson 2 of 9

Phishing in all its forms: how to spot one

Phishing comes in many shapes. Mass phishing sends the same message to millions, hoping a few bite. Spear phishing is written for one person or team, using details from LinkedIn or the company website. Whaling targets senior leaders. Smishing arrives by SMS, vishing by voice call, and quishing hides the link inside a QR code, so you cannot hover over it. Clone phishing copies a real email you received earlier and swaps the link. Some famous cases show how simple it can be. In March 2016 John Podesta, chair of a US presidential campaign, received a fake Google security alert asking him to change his password. A staffer called it legitimate by mistake, and his emails were stolen and published. On 15 July 2020 attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT support, got them to log in on a fake page, and then used internal tools to post from the accounts of famous people, asking followers to send bitcoin. The animation walks through the five clues to check every time: who really sent it, whether it is pushing you to hurry, where the link really goes, what it asks for, and how it greets you. Try the CEO message. It has no link and no attachment, so spam filters often let it through, but the request itself is the giveaway. If in doubt, do not click. Open the bank’s app or type the address yourself.

Common mistake
Scanning a QR code to receive money is always a scam. With UPI you only scan or enter your PIN to pay.
Lesson 3 of 9

Reading links and lookalike domains

Most phishing links try to look like a real address. The first skill is to read a web address from the right. In indiapost dot gov dot in dot track parcel dot top, the part that matters is track parcel dot top, because that is the domain that was actually registered. Anyone who owns it can put indiapost dot gov dot in in front as a subdomain. Attackers also use typosquatting, registering common mistakes such as gooogle or flipkrt, and homoglyphs, letters that look the same. The digit one looks like a small L, the letters r and n together look like m, and some Cyrillic letters are pixel for pixel identical to Latin ones. In April 2017 the researcher Xudong Zheng registered a domain made of Cyrillic letters that looked exactly like apple dot com in several browsers. Browsers now show such names in punycode, an ASCII form that starts with xn dash dash, when the letters mix alphabets. Our animation lets you type any address. It finds the registered part, swaps lookalike letters back, and measures the edit distance, the number of single letter changes, to well known brands. Security teams use the same idea to watch for new lookalike registrations of their own brand so they can get them taken down quickly.

Lesson 4 of 9

Email authentication: SPF, DKIM and DMARC

The original email system from the 1980s let anyone write any name in the From line, the way anyone can write any return address on a postcard. Three standards were added later to fix this. SPF, the Sender Policy Framework, lets a domain publish in DNS the list of servers allowed to send its mail. DKIM, DomainKeys Identified Mail, has the sending server sign each message with a private key, and receivers check the signature with a public key published in DNS, which also proves the message was not changed on the way. DMARC ties the two to the address people actually see. It passes only if SPF or DKIM passes for the same domain as the From line, and it lets the domain owner choose what receivers should do with failures: none, which only sends reports, quarantine, which sends them to spam, or reject. In February 2024 Gmail and Yahoo began requiring all three from anyone sending large volumes of mail. The animation shows four cases. A spoofed From address fails and is refused under p equals reject. A forwarded genuine message fails SPF but keeps its DKIM signature and still passes. And a lookalike domain passes everything, because the attacker really owns it. So email authentication stops exact spoofing of your domain, and only careful people and good filters stop lookalikes.

Rolling out DMARC safely for a company domain
  1. List every service that sends mail as your domain: mail server, newsletters, ticketing, payroll.
  2. Publish SPF for them and turn on DKIM signing for each.
  3. Publish DMARC with p=none and collect the daily reports for a few weeks.
  4. Fix every legitimate sender that fails.
  5. Move to p=quarantine, then to p=reject once failures are only fakes.
Lesson 5 of 9

Business email compromise, voice cloning and deepfakes

Business email compromise, BEC for short, is the most expensive kind of social engineering. The attacker pretends to be a boss, a supplier or a lawyer and asks for a payment or a change of bank details. Often there is no link and no malware at all, just a convincing request at a busy moment. Sometimes the attacker has really broken into the supplier’s mailbox, so the message comes from the genuine address and replies inside a real conversation. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center recorded about 2.9 billion dollars of reported BEC losses in the United States in 2023 alone. Now add artificial intelligence. In 2019 the head of a UK energy company transferred 220,000 euros after a phone call from someone who sounded exactly like his German boss, reportedly a cloned voice. In early 2024 an employee in the Hong Kong office of the engineering firm Arup joined a video call with the company’s chief financial officer and several colleagues, and made fifteen transfers totalling about 25 million US dollars. Every other person on the call was a deepfake. The defence does not depend on spotting the fake, which keeps getting harder. It depends on process. Any request to move money or change bank details is confirmed on a second channel you already trust, such as a phone number from your own records, and large payments need two people to approve. Families can agree a code word for emergencies, so a panicked call from a "relative" can be checked with one question.

Lesson 6 of 9

Pretexting, baiting, tailgating and MFA fatigue

Not all social engineering comes by email. Pretexting means inventing a believable story, a pretext, to get help. A caller says they are a new employee locked out on their first day, or an auditor who needs a file urgently. In September 2023 attackers reportedly found an MGM Resorts employee on LinkedIn, called the IT help desk pretending to be that person, and got their access reset. The resulting attack shut down hotel and casino systems for days and cost the company about 100 million dollars. Baiting leaves something tempting for the victim to pick up. In a 2016 study at the University of Illinois, researchers dropped 297 USB drives around the campus. Almost all were picked up and people opened files on at least 45 percent of them. Tailgating, or piggybacking, means following someone through a secure door. And MFA fatigue, also called push bombing, floods a person with login approval requests until they tap approve just to make it stop. In September 2022 an attacker who had an Uber contractor’s password sent repeated push requests, then messaged on WhatsApp claiming to be Uber IT and saying the approvals would stop once accepted. The contractor accepted. Defences are partly people and partly design: help desks that verify identity through a manager or a video check before any reset, a rule never to plug in found USB drives, badges that everyone must show, and MFA that cannot be approved by mistake, such as number matching or, best of all, passkeys.

Lesson 7 of 9

Defending people: training, reporting and phishing resistant login

Telling people to be careful is not enough on its own, because even careful people get tired and busy. Good organisations combine three layers. The first is technical controls that catch most attacks before a person sees them: spam and malware filters, sandboxes that open attachments safely, DMARC on your own domains, warnings for external senders and lookalike domains, and browsers that block known phishing sites. The second is phishing resistant login, which protects people even when they do click. Passkeys and FIDO2 security keys, from the authentication chapter, sign in only to the real website, so a fake page receives nothing useful. Google reported in 2018 that after it gave security keys to its more than 85,000 employees in early 2017, none of their work accounts had been taken over by phishing. The third layer is people who know what to look for and, most importantly, report quickly. Practice campaigns, where the security team sends harmless fake phishing emails, can lower click rates and raise report rates, as long as they teach rather than shame. One fast report lets the team pull the message from every inbox before others click. The program shows why the report rate matters more than the click rate: in a big campaign someone almost always clicks, so the race is to notice fast.

Practise safely and legally
Practise spotting phishing with free quizzes from Google (Jigsaw phishing quiz) or your bank’s awareness pages. Only run phishing simulations inside your own organisation with written approval, and never send fake messages to people outside it.

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows the red flags and never shares passwords or OTPs. A student can explain why the tricks work and how SPF, DKIM and DMARC fit together. A professional runs awareness programmes, deploys email authentication and builds payment verification rules. Experts study human factors, measure interventions properly and fight deepfake fraud.
One idea, four depths

Scammers pretend to be someone you trust and rush you. Stop, check the sender and link, never share an OTP or PIN, and ask someone you trust before paying anything.

Cialdini’s six principles, kinds of phishing, reading the registered domain, edit distance, SPF, DKIM and DMARC alignment, BEC callback rules, 1 − (1 − p)n.

Deploy DMARC to p=reject, run respectful phishing simulations, design help desk identity checks and payment controls, roll out passkeys, and handle reported phishing quickly.

Research human factors and usable security, measure training effects with proper experiments, detect deepfakes and coordinated scam operations, and shape policy against scam centres.
