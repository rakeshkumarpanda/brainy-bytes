Lesson 1 of 9

How security certifications work

Security certifications grew up with the industry. ISC2 was founded in 1989 and gave the first CISSP exams in 1994. In 2004 the CISSP became the first information security credential accredited under the international standard ISO 17024, which sets rules for how a certification body must work. CompTIA launched Security+ in 2002 as a vendor neutral entry exam. Both are on the list of approved certifications for the cyber workforce of the United States Department of Defense, first under directive 8570 and now under 8140, so many employers worldwide use them as a hiring filter. A certification does not make you an expert. It proves you know a broad, agreed body of knowledge, in the words the industry uses. That is why this subject keeps using the same terms as the exam outlines. This chapter maps every chapter of the subject to the main exams, collects the facts and formulas you must recall quickly, shows how scenario questions are marked, and ends with a mock exam. One warning. Exam outlines are revised every few years. Always open the official page of your exam and check the current outline code before you book.

Source: CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 and CySA+ CS0-003 exam objectives (2023), ISC2 CISSP exam outline (April 2024), ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity, EC-Council CEH exam information, US DoD Manual 8140.03.

What it is
A security certification is an exam, run by an independent body, that checks you know an agreed outline of security knowledge, often with an experience requirement and yearly continuing education.
Why we need it
Employers and governments use certifications as a shared measure of skill. Preparing for one also gives your study a clear outline, so you do not leave gaps.
How it works
Read the official outline, map each domain to what you have learned, revise with active recall and spaced repetition, practise timed questions, then book the exam.

Common mistake
The codes SY0-701 and CS0-003 are the 2023 outlines. CompTIA replaces an outline about every three years, so check whether a newer code is live before you book. CISSP adaptive testing gives harder questions as you answer correctly and stops once it is sure you have passed or failed.
How to answer a certification question
  1. Read the last sentence first. It tells you what is asked: the FIRST step, the BEST control, the MOST likely cause.
  2. Read the scenario and underline the facts that matter: the asset, the threat, what has already been done.
  3. Remove the two answers that are wrong or illegal. Two remain.
  4. Of the two, choose the one that fixes the cause, follows the process and is in your role. For CISSP think like a manager: protect people first, follow policy, reduce risk to the business.
  5. Skip performance based or long questions on the first pass and return to them, so the easy marks are not lost to the clock.

Lesson 4 of 9

Every formula in one place

Most security exams have a few numerical questions, and university and interview exams have many more. They nearly always come from the same small set of formulas: risk in money, password strength in bits, the birthday bound for hashes, modular arithmetic for RSA and Diffie Hellman, Bayes for alerts, availability for service levels, and the noise scale of differential privacy. This lesson puts them all on one page. The program computes every one of them on example numbers, so you can change a number and check your own answer.
Risk in moneyExam favourite
SLE = AV × EF, ALE = SLE × ARO
Asset value, exposure factor, annual rate of occurrence. A control is worth buying if ALE before − ALE after − yearly cost > 0.
Password strengthExam favourite
H = L log2 N bits, average guesses 2H / 2
Length L from an alphabet of N symbols, only if every character is random.
Birthday bound
collision after about 2n/2 tries
An n bit hash gives n/2 bits of collision resistance.
RSAExam favourite
N = pq, φ = (p − 1)(q − 1), ed ≡ 1 (mod φ), c = me mod N, m = cd mod N
Security rests on factoring N.
Diffie-Hellman
A = ga mod p, B = gb mod p, K = Ba = Ab mod p
Security rests on the discrete logarithm.
Alert precision (Bayes)Exam favourite
P(bad | alert) = TPR · p / (TPR · p + FPR · (1 − p))
When the base rate p is tiny, most alerts are false.
Availability
A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR), downtime = (1 − A) × 525600 minutes a year
99.9% is 8.76 hours a year, 99.99% is 52.6 minutes.
Differential privacy
Laplace b = Δf / ε, attack advantage ≤ eε − 1
Smaller ε means more noise and more privacy.

Going deeper: Why these formulas keep returning

Each formula answers one of the questions a security team asks every week. Is this control worth its cost (ALE). Will this password or key survive a guessing attack (entropy and the birthday bound). Can an attacker read or forge this message (RSA and Diffie-Hellman). Should an analyst trust this alert (Bayes). Can we promise this uptime to a customer (availability). Can we publish this statistic without exposing a person (differential privacy). If you can say which question a formula answers, you will know when to use it in an exam.

Lesson 5 of 9

Scenario questions: what to do first

The hardest exam questions describe a situation and ask what you should do first, next or best. Several answers are good actions, but only one is right at that moment. Two orders solve most of them. The first is the incident response life cycle from NIST: preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, and lessons learned. The second is the order of volatility from RFC 3227: collect the evidence that disappears fastest first. Add the rule that human safety comes before everything, and the rule that you follow the documented process and your own role, and you can answer almost any scenario.

Pairs that exams love to confuse

Many wrong answers are the right answer to a slightly different question. An exam will offer encryption when the question is about integrity, an intrusion detection system when it asks for prevention, or recovery time when it asks about lost data. Going deeper: Question 1. Prove that running an ε1-DP and an ε2-DP mechanism on the same data is (ε1 + ε2)-DP.

For independent mechanisms the output pair has probability P[M1(D) = a] P[M2(D) = b]. Each factor is at most eεi times the same factor for D′. Multiply the two bounds: the product is at most eε1 + ε2 times the product for D′. Summing over any set S of output pairs keeps the bound.

Going deeper: Question 2. Why is textbook RSA (c = me mod N with no padding) insecure even with a 4096 bit key?

It is deterministic, so the same message always gives the same ciphertext and an attacker can test guesses. It is malleable: c × 2e decrypts to 2m, so an attacker can change a message in a predictable way. Small messages with e = 3 can be recovered by an ordinary cube root. Padding such as OAEP adds randomness and structure to fix all three.

Going deeper: Question 3. A detector has 99% recall and a 0.1% false positive rate. Attacks are 1 in 100000 events. What share of alerts are real, and what does this mean for design?

P(bad | alert) = 0.99 × 0.00001 / (0.99 × 0.00001 + 0.001 × 0.99999) ≈ 0.0098, so about 1 alert in 100 is real. Design must add context to cut false positives (correlation, allow lists, a second stage model) and must measure precision, not accuracy.

Going deeper: Question 4. Show that FedAvg with one local gradient step per round and equal sized clients equals ordinary gradient descent on the pooled data.

Each client computes wk = w − η ∇Fk(w). The average is w − η (1/K) Σ ∇Fk(w). With equal sizes, (1/K) Σ Fk is the loss on the pooled data, so this is one gradient step on it. The difference appears only with several local steps, where client drift on non IID data pulls the models apart.

Going deeper: Question 5. Why does reusing a nonce in AES-GCM break both confidentiality and authenticity?

GCM encrypts with a counter mode keystream that depends only on the key and nonce. Two messages under the same nonce share the keystream, so c1 ⊕ c2 = m1 ⊕ m2. Their authentication tags are polynomials in the same secret H, and subtracting them gives an equation an attacker can solve for H, which then lets them forge tags for any message.

A study plan that works

People who pass security exams on the first attempt rarely study more hours. They study in a better way. Two methods have strong evidence from memory research. Active recall means testing yourself instead of rereading. Spaced repetition means returning to a fact just before you would forget it, with the gap growing each time you get it right. Sebastian Leitner turned this into a simple box system in 1972. The program simulates it with twelve exam facts. Cards you know move to boxes you review rarely, and cards you miss come back every day, so your time goes where it is needed.

Revision summary

  1. Exams: ISC2 CC and Security+ to start, CySA+ for analysts, CISSP after five years. Check the current outline code before booking.
  2. Domains: weight your time by domain weight. Security operations is the largest Security+ domain (28%).
  3. Recall: ports, crypto sizes, control types (preventive, detective, corrective, deterrent, compensating, directive) and access models.
  4. Maths: ALE = AV × EF × ARO, H = L log2 N, birthday 2n/2, RSA and DH, Bayes for alerts, availability, Laplace b = Δf/ε.
  5. Scenarios: safety first, then the NIST order (prepare, detect, contain, eradicate, recover, learn) and the order of volatility.
  6. Method: active recall, spaced repetition, timed mocks and a review of every wrong answer.
