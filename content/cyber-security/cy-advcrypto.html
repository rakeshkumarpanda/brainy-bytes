Lesson 1 of 9

Cryptography beyond encryption

So far cryptography has done two jobs: keeping messages secret and proving who sent them. Since the late 1970s researchers have built tools for harder jobs, where people who do not fully trust each other still need to work together. Adi Shamir showed in 1979 how to split a secret into pieces so that only a group can rebuild it. Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff showed in 1985 that you can convince someone a statement is true without telling them anything else, and Goldwasser and Micali later received the Turing Award partly for this work. Andrew Yao asked in 1982 how two millionaires could find out who is richer without revealing their wealth, which started secure multiparty computation. In 2009 Craig Gentry built the first fully homomorphic encryption, which lets a server compute on data it cannot read. For decades these ideas were too slow for real use. Today they protect cryptocurrency wallets, private contact discovery, census style statistics, password breach checks and federated learning on phones. This chapter explains each tool with small numbers you can check by hand and Python programs that run them.

What it is
Advanced cryptographic tools let several parties hold, check or compute on secrets without any one of them seeing everything: secret sharing, commitments, zero knowledge proofs, homomorphic encryption and secure multiparty computation (MPC).
Why we need it
Real systems often need trust spread across people or machines: no single admin should hold the master key, a server should add votes without seeing them, a user should prove age without showing a birth date.
How it works
Each tool rests on maths you already know: polynomials over a prime field, discrete logarithms and modular arithmetic from the RSA and Diffie-Hellman chapters.

Lesson 2 of 9

Shamir secret sharing

Shamir secret sharing uses one fact from school algebra: two points fix a line, three points fix a parabola, and in general k points fix a polynomial of degree k minus 1. To share a secret, put it as the constant term of a random polynomial of degree k minus 1, then hand out points on the curve, one per person. Any k people can join their points and rebuild the polynomial with Lagrange interpolation, then read off the value at x equals 0, which is the secret. Fewer than k people learn nothing at all, because for every possible secret there is exactly one polynomial that passes through their points and that secret. This is called information theoretic security: it holds even against an attacker with unlimited computing power, quantum or not. All the arithmetic is done modulo a prime, so the numbers never grow and no information leaks through sizes. George Blakley published a scheme with the same goal in the same year using intersecting planes. Shamir sharing protects the root keys of certificate authorities and DNSSEC, where several key holders must meet, and it is the base of threshold signatures such as FROST, published as RFC 9591 in 2024.

What it is
A (k, n) threshold scheme splits a secret s into n shares so that any k shares rebuild s and any k − 1 shares reveal nothing about it.
Why we need it
It removes the single point of failure of one key holder, and also the single point of theft. Losing up to n − k shares is still safe.
How it works
Pick f(x) = s + a1x + … + ak−1xk−1 mod p with random ai. Share i is (i, f(i)). Rebuild with Lagrange interpolation at x = 0.
Sharing polynomialExam favourite
f(x) = s + a1x + a2x2 + … + ak−1xk−1 (mod p)
Toy: s = 7, f(x) = 7 + 3x + 2x2 mod 11 gives shares (1, 1), (2, 10), (3, 1), (4, 7), (5, 6).
Lagrange at zeroExam favourite
s = f(0) = ∑i yi ∏j≠i xj / (xj − xi) (mod p)
Two shares (1, 5) and (2, 9) of a line mod 11: s = 5 · 2/1 + 9 · 1/(−1) = 10 − 9 = 1.
Perfect secrecy
for k − 1 shares, every s in Zp is equally likely
Each candidate s fixes one polynomial through the known points, so the shares give no hint.
Going deeper: Why Lagrange interpolation works

For each share i build the basis polynomial ℓi(x) = ∏j≠i (x − xj) / (xi − xj). It equals 1 at xi and 0 at every other xj. Then f(x) = ∑ yi ℓi(x) passes through all k points and has degree k − 1, so by uniqueness it is the sharing polynomial. Putting x = 0 gives the formula above. Division means multiplying by a modular inverse, which exists because p is prime. Shamir sharing is also linear: adding two people’s shares point by point gives shares of the sum of the secrets, which MPC protocols use heavily.

Common mistake
Plain Shamir sharing trusts the dealer and the share holders to be honest. A cheating holder can hand in a wrong share and change the rebuilt secret. Verifiable secret sharing (Feldman 1987, Pedersen 1991) publishes commitments to the coefficients so every share can be checked.

Commitments: lock now, reveal later

A commitment is the digital version of writing a choice on paper, sealing it in an envelope and putting it on the table. Later you open the envelope and everyone can see you did not change your mind. It needs two properties. Hiding means nobody can read the choice from the sealed envelope. Binding means you cannot open the envelope to a different choice. The easiest commitment is a hash of a random salt followed by the value. The salt matters: without it, a commitment to yes or no, or to a number from 1 to 100, is broken by hashing every possible value. Torben Pedersen proposed in 1991 a commitment built from discrete logarithms: C equals g to the m times h to the r, modulo a prime. It is perfectly hiding, because for any m there is an r that gives the same C, and it is binding as long as nobody knows the discrete log of h to the base g. Pedersen commitments have a useful extra property: multiplying two commitments gives a commitment to the sum of the values. This lets confidential transactions in some cryptocurrencies prove that inputs equal outputs without showing amounts. Commitments are also the first move in most zero knowledge proofs.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 commit.py.
What it is
A commitment scheme has Commit(m, r) = C and Open(C, m, r) = accept or reject. It must be hiding (C reveals nothing about m) and binding (no one can open C to a different m).
Why we need it
It enforces fairness when moves happen in turn: sealed bids, coin flips over a network, voting, and the first message of a zero knowledge proof.
How it works
Hash commitment: C = SHA-256(r ‖ m) with a fresh random r. Pedersen commitment: C = gm hr mod p, where logg h is unknown.
Pedersen commitmentExam favourite
C = gm hr (mod p)
Toy group of order 11 inside Z23: g = 4, h = 9, m = 2, r = 3 gives C = 16 · 16 = 256 ≡ 3 (mod 23).
Additive propertyExam favourite
C(m1, r1) · C(m2, r2) = C(m1 + m2, r1 + r2)
The program commits to 20 and 22 and checks that the product opens to 42.
Why binding holds
gmhr = gm′hr′ gives logg h = (m − m′)/(r′ − r) mod q
Opening two ways would solve a discrete log, which is assumed hard.

Zero knowledge proofs

A zero knowledge proof lets a prover convince a verifier that a statement is true while revealing nothing beyond that fact. The classic story, told by Jean-Jacques Quisquater and colleagues in 1989, is a cave shaped like a ring with a magic door at the back. Peggy says she knows the password that opens the door. Victor waits outside while she walks down the left or right path. Then he shouts which side she must come out of. If she knows the password she can always obey. If she is bluffing she gets it right only half the time, so after 20 rounds a bluffer survives with chance about one in a million. Victor ends up convinced, yet he learns nothing about the password, and a video of the rounds would not convince anyone else, because Peggy and Victor could have agreed the sides in advance. A proof must have three properties: completeness, where an honest prover always passes, soundness, where a cheater almost always fails, and zero knowledge, where the verifier could have produced the same transcript alone. Claus Schnorr gave in 1989 a short proof that you know x with y equal to g to the x. Fiat and Shamir showed in 1986 how to replace the verifier by a hash, which turns the proof into a digital signature. Modern succinct proofs, called SNARKs and STARKs, prove that a whole program ran correctly with a proof of a few hundred bytes to a few hundred kilobytes.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 schnorr_zkp.py.
Schnorr identification, one round
  1. Commit: the prover picks a random r, sends t = gr mod p.
  2. Challenge: the verifier sends a random c.
  3. Respond: the prover sends s = r + c x mod q.
  4. Check: the verifier accepts if gs = t · yc mod p, which holds because gr + cx = gr(gx)c.
Schnorr checkExam favourite
gs ≡ t · yc (mod p), with s = r + cx mod q
Toy p = 23, q = 11, g = 4, x = 7, y = 8. r = 3, c = 5: t = 18, s = 38 mod 11 = 5, g5 = 12 and 18 · 85 = 18 · 16 = 288 ≡ 12.
Soundness errorExam favourite
Pr[cheater passes n rounds] = (1/|C|)n
Binary challenges: 2−20 ≈ one in a million after 20 rounds. With c from a group of size 2256, one round is enough.
Fiat-Shamir
c = H(t, y, message)
The hash plays the verifier. The result is a Schnorr signature, the ancestor of EdDSA.
Going deeper: Why Schnorr is zero knowledge and sound

Zero knowledge (honest verifier): a simulator who does not know x picks c and s at random and sets t = gs y−c. The triple (t, c, s) has exactly the same distribution as a real one, so a transcript teaches nothing. This is exactly the cheater’s trick in the program, which only works when the challenge is known before t is sent. Soundness (special soundness): if a prover can answer two different challenges c and c′ for the same t, then s − s′ = (c − c′)x, so x = (s − s′)/(c − c′) mod q. A prover who passes often must know x. The same algebra explains why reusing r in ECDSA or Schnorr signatures leaks the private key, the flaw behind the 2010 PlayStation 3 break.

Homomorphic encryption

Homomorphic encryption lets someone compute on encrypted numbers and get an encrypted answer, without ever seeing the numbers. You have met a small version already: textbook RSA is multiplicatively homomorphic, because the product of two ciphertexts decrypts to the product of the messages. Pascal Paillier published in 1999 a scheme that is additively homomorphic, where multiplying ciphertexts adds the hidden numbers. It also has semantic security, because each encryption uses fresh randomness, so the same salary never encrypts to the same ciphertext twice. That makes Paillier a good fit for encrypted voting tallies and for summing values from many parties. For thirty years nobody knew how to support both addition and multiplication without limit, which would let a server run any program on encrypted data. Craig Gentry solved this in 2009 in his Stanford PhD thesis, using lattices. His key idea, bootstrapping, refreshes the noise that grows in a ciphertext after each operation by running the decryption circuit homomorphically. Later schemes, BGV, BFV, CKKS for approximate real numbers and TFHE for fast bit operations, made it practical for some tasks. It is still slow, often thousands to a million times slower than computing on plain data, but Apple used BFV in 2024 for private lookups such as caller ID, and libraries such as Microsoft SEAL and OpenFHE are open source.

What it is
Homomorphic encryption: there are operations ⊕ on ciphertexts and + on plaintexts with Dec(Enc(a) ⊕ Enc(b)) = a + b. Partially homomorphic schemes support one operation, fully homomorphic (FHE) schemes support addition and multiplication any number of times.
Why we need it
It lets you hand data to an untrusted cloud or a counting server and still get useful results, while the data stays encrypted the whole time.
How it works
Paillier: c = gm rn mod n2. Multiplying ciphertexts multiplies g powers, which adds exponents. FHE schemes hide messages in lattice noise and add and multiply polynomials.
Paillier encryptionExam favourite
c = gm rn (mod n2), with g = n + 1
Toy n = 35 (p = 5, q = 7), n2 = 1225, g = 36, random r coprime to n.
Paillier decryptionExam favourite
m = L(cλ mod n2) · μ (mod n), with L(u) = (u − 1)/n
λ = lcm(4, 6) = 12, μ = λ−1 mod 35 = 3, because 12 · 3 = 36 ≡ 1.
Additive homomorphismExam favourite
Enc(a) · Enc(b) = Enc(a + b mod n), and Enc(a)k = Enc(k a mod n)
Toy: a = 20, b = 22 decrypt to 42 mod 35 = 7. Sums wrap round n, so real keys use a 2048 bit n.
Why g = n + 1 works
(1 + n)m ≡ 1 + m n (mod n2)
By the binomial theorem every term with n2 vanishes, so the message sits in a linear term that L reads off.

Secure multiparty computation

Secure multiparty computation, or MPC, lets several parties compute a function of their private inputs and learn only the result. Andrew Yao posed the millionaires problem in 1982 and in 1986 gave garbled circuits, a way for two parties to evaluate any circuit where one party encrypts the gates and the other evaluates them blind, using oblivious transfer to get its input keys. In 1987 Goldreich, Micali and Wigderson showed that any function can be computed securely by any number of parties. The easiest version to understand uses additive secret sharing: each party splits its number into random parts that add up to it, sends one part to each other party, and everyone publishes the sum of what they received. The total comes out right, but each part alone looks random. The first large real use was in Denmark in January 2008, when about 1200 farmers sold sugar beet contracts in an auction run with MPC, so no one, not even the organisers, saw individual bids. In 2015 and later, Boston employers used MPC from Boston University to measure the gender wage gap without revealing any company’s payroll. Today MPC protects keys in cryptocurrency wallets, where threshold signatures such as FROST let several devices sign without the full key ever existing in one place. And Bonawitz and colleagues at Google showed in 2017 how phones can send masked model updates so that the server only learns their sum, which is secure aggregation for federated learning.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 mpc_sum.py.
What it is
MPC: n parties with inputs x1, …, xn compute y = f(x1, …, xn) so that each learns y and nothing more than y implies. Security is defined by comparison with an ideal trusted third party.
Why we need it
It replaces the trusted middleman when competitors, hospitals or governments must combine data that law or business keeps private.
How it works
Secret share each input, compute on shares (addition is free, multiplication needs interaction such as Beaver triples), and reveal only the output. Two party protocols often use garbled circuits and oblivious transfer instead.
Additive sharingExam favourite
x = x1 + x2 + … + xn (mod M), with x1 to xn−1 random
Toy M = 101: x = 40 with parts 77 and 15 needs a last part (40 − 77 − 15) mod 101 = 49.
Secure aggregation maskExam favourite
client a sends ua + mab, client b sends ub − mab
The masks cancel in the sum. With n clients there are n(n − 1)/2 pairwise masks, 4950 for 100 clients.
Threat models
semi honest (follow the protocol, try to learn more) versus malicious (deviate at will)
Malicious security costs more: MACs on shares (SPDZ, 2012) or cut and choose for garbled circuits.

Lesson 7 of 9

The privacy tools compared

These tools overlap, so choosing between them is a common exam and interview question. Secret sharing is the cheapest and protects data at rest across holders, but someone must rebuild the secret to use it, unless it is combined with MPC. Homomorphic encryption needs only one server and no interaction, but it is the slowest, and the key holder can decrypt everything. MPC is faster than FHE for many tasks but needs several parties online who are trusted not to all collude. Zero knowledge proofs do not compute on hidden data for someone else. They prove that a computation on your own hidden data was correct. Trusted execution environments such as Intel SGX and AMD SEV take a hardware route instead of maths, which is fast but depends on the chip vendor and has suffered side channel attacks such as Foreshadow in 2018. Many real systems combine tools: federated learning uses MPC style masks plus differential privacy, and private payment systems combine commitments with zero knowledge proofs.

Lesson 8 of 9

From beginner to expert

These topics run from puzzles a child can follow to some of the most active research in computer science. A beginner can explain the cave story, splitting a password among friends and the idea of adding numbers while they stay locked. A college student can share and rebuild a secret by Lagrange interpolation, run a Schnorr proof and its Fiat-Shamir version, check Paillier’s algebra and sum numbers with additive shares. A masters student studies formal definitions with simulators, special soundness and rewinding, verifiable secret sharing, oblivious transfer and garbled circuits, Beaver triples, SNARK arithmetisation with R1CS and polynomial commitments, and FHE noise growth and bootstrapping. Research covers faster provers, post quantum zero knowledge, FHE hardware accelerators, maliciously secure MPC at scale and the composition of these tools with differential privacy.
One idea, four depths

A group can share a secret so that only enough members together can open it. You can prove you know something without showing it. Some encryption lets a computer add numbers it cannot read.

Shamir sharing and Lagrange interpolation mod p, hash and Pedersen commitments, Schnorr rounds and Fiat-Shamir, Paillier encryption and its additive property, additive shares and masked sums.

Simulation based definitions, special soundness and rewinding, Feldman and Pedersen VSS, OT and garbled circuits, Beaver triples and SPDZ, R1CS, QAP and KZG commitments, FHE noise budgets and bootstrapping.

Prover efficiency (folding schemes, lookup arguments), lattice and hash based post quantum ZK, FHE accelerators, MPC with dishonest majorities at scale, and combining MPC, HE and differential privacy in federated learning.

Chapter summary

  1. Shamir (1979): secret s = f(0) of a random degree k − 1 polynomial mod p. Any k shares rebuild by Lagrange, k − 1 reveal nothing.
  2. Commitments: hiding and binding. Hash with salt, or Pedersen C = gmhr, which is perfectly hiding and additive.
  3. Zero knowledge (GMR 1985): completeness, soundness, zero knowledge. Cave story, cheater passes n binary rounds with 2−n.
  4. Schnorr: t = gr, challenge c, s = r + cx, check gs = t yc. Fiat-Shamir hashes the challenge and gives a signature.
  5. SNARKs and STARKs: Groth16 about 128 to 192 bytes with trusted setup, STARKs larger but hash based and transparent.
  6. Paillier (1999): c = gmrn mod n2. Multiplying ciphertexts adds plaintexts. Gentry (2009) gave FHE with bootstrapping.
  7. MPC: Yao garbled circuits, GMW, additive shares. Sugar beet auction 2008, wage gap study, FROST wallets.
  8. Secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al. 2017): masks that cancel in the sum, the bridge to federated learning.
