Lesson 1 of 10

Machine learning systems are a new attack surface

The last chapter used machine learning to defend. This chapter asks how machine learning systems themselves can be attacked, and how to protect them. A model is software, so all the usual risks apply, but it adds new ones because its behaviour comes from data rather than from code a person wrote. Researchers saw this early. In 2004 Nilesh Dalvi and colleagues studied spammers adapting to classifiers, and in 2012 and 2013 Battista Biggio and colleagues showed how to poison support vector machines and evade classifiers at test time. Then in 2013 Christian Szegedy and colleagues found that tiny changes to an image, invisible to people, could make a neural network see something completely different. Real incidents followed. In March 2016 Microsoft’s chatbot Tay learned from public tweets and was taught to post offensive messages within a day. In 2019 researchers at Skylight Cyber made malware pass Cylance’s ML antivirus by appending strings taken from a popular online game. Today the US National Institute of Standards and Technology publishes a taxonomy, NIST AI 100-2, MITRE keeps a knowledge base of real attacks on AI called ATLAS, and OWASP lists the top ten risks for applications built on large language models. The EU AI Act of 2024 even requires high risk AI systems to be resilient against these attacks. The main families are evasion at test time, poisoning at training time, privacy attacks that pull out training data or copy the model, and abuse of language model assistants through prompt injection. We study each one on toy models, with the defence alongside.

Lesson 2 of 10

Evasion: adversarial examples and FGSM

An evasion attack changes the input at test time so the model gives the wrong answer, while the input still does its job. For images that means changes too small for a person to notice. For malware it means a file that still runs but now looks clean. In 2014 Ian Goodfellow, Jonathon Shlens and Christian Szegedy explained why this is so easy for many models: they behave almost linearly, and in high dimensions many tiny changes add up. If every one of a thousand features moves by a small amount in the direction that lowers the score, the total change is a thousand times larger. Their fast gradient sign method, FGSM, takes exactly that step: move each feature by epsilon in the direction of the sign of the gradient of the loss. For a linear model the gradient is just the weight vector, which our animation and program use. In 2018 Kevin Eykholt and colleagues printed stickers that made a road sign classifier read a stop sign as a speed limit sign from many angles. The good news is that for a linear model a defender can compute a guarantee. The distance from a point to the boundary, measured the right way, is a certified radius: no change smaller than that can flip the decision, whatever the attacker tries. For a point with score z and weights w, the radius in the max norm is the absolute value of z divided by the sum of absolute weights. Security models also have a natural advantage: an attacker cannot change every feature freely, because the malware must still work.

Fast gradient sign methodExam favourite
x′ = x + ε · sign(∇x L(x, y))
For a linear score the attacker lowers z with x′ = x − ε · sign(w).
How much a linear score can moveExam favourite
Δz = ε ‖w‖1 = ε Σ |wi|
w = (2, 1.5, 1.8, 1.2, −2.5), ε = 0.1: Δz = 0.1 × 9 = 0.9.
Certified radius of a linear modelExam favourite
r∞ = |z| / ‖w‖1, r2 = |z| / ‖w‖2
z = 2.99, ‖w‖1 = 9: r∞ ≈ 0.332. Smaller changes can never flip the verdict.
Going deeper: Why high dimensions make it worse

Suppose every weight has size about m and there are n features. An ε change in each feature moves the score by about ε n m. For a 224 by 224 colour image n is about 150000, so an ε of 1 in 255 grey levels, invisible to the eye, can move the score by hundreds of units. This is the linearity explanation of Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy. Stronger attacks such as projected gradient descent (PGD) take many small FGSM steps and project back into the allowed region each time.

Lesson 3 of 10

Defending against evasion

Many defences against adversarial examples were proposed and then broken. In 2018 Anish Athalye, Nicholas Carlini and David Wagner found that seven of nine defences at a top conference relied on hiding the gradient, and attacks adapted to each defence broke six of them fully and one partly. So the field now values two things. The first is adversarial training, made popular by Aleksander Madry and colleagues in 2018. During training, for every example, find the worst change inside the allowed region with a few PGD steps, and train the model to get that worst case right. This is a min max game: the attacker maximises the loss, the defender minimises the worst case loss. It works, but it costs several times more training and usually lowers accuracy on clean data a little. The second is certified robustness, which gives a proof rather than a test. For linear models the radius is exact, as you saw. For deep networks, randomised smoothing by Jeremy Cohen, Elan Rosenfeld and Zico Kolter in 2019 adds random noise many times and takes a vote, which gives a provable radius in the L2 norm. In security there are extra tools. Monotonic classifiers only let features that indicate malware raise the score, so an attacker cannot lower it by adding harmless looking content, which defeats the Cylance style trick of appending game strings. Ensembles of different models, input checks, and above all layered defences mean one fooled model is not the whole story.
Adversarial training as a min max problemExam favourite
minθ E(x,y) [ max‖δ‖ ≤ ε L(fθ(x + δ), y) ]
The inner max is the attacker (found with PGD), the outer min is the defender.
Randomised smoothing radius
r = σ Φ−1(pA)
σ = 0.5 and the top class wins with pA = 0.9: Φ−1(0.9) ≈ 1.2816, so r ≈ 0.64 in L2.

Lesson 4 of 10

Poisoning and backdoors

Poisoning attacks the training data. Many models learn from data that outsiders can influence: user reports of spam, public web pages, crowdsourced labels, shared malware feeds, open datasets and pretrained models downloaded from the internet. An attacker who adds or changes a small part of that data can damage the model. Availability poisoning tries to make the model bad overall, as when users taught Tay offensive replies in 2016. Targeted poisoning flips labels on certain examples so one kind of attack slips through, which our animation and program show. Backdoor attacks are sneakier. In 2017 Tianyu Gu, Brendan Dolan-Gavitt and Siddharth Garg showed BadNets: a model trained with a few images carrying a small yellow square behaves normally on clean inputs but follows the attacker whenever the trigger appears, for example reading a stop sign with a sticker as a speed limit. Because the model scores well on ordinary tests, the backdoor can hide for years. In 2023 researchers showed that buying expired domains linked from popular web scale datasets could let someone poison a small share of the images in them for only about sixty US dollars. Defences start with the supply chain: control who can add training data, keep records of where each example came from, check hashes of downloaded datasets and models, and prefer trusted sources. Statistical cleaning, such as the neighbour vote in our program, removes points whose labels disagree with their surroundings. Finally, compare every new model with the previous one on a trusted test set, and watch for strange behaviour on rare patterns.

Lesson 5 of 10

Privacy attacks: membership inference and data extraction

A trained model can leak the data it learned from. In membership inference, the attacker wants to know whether one particular person was in the training data. That alone can be sensitive: if a model was trained on patients with a certain disease, being a member reveals the disease. Reza Shokri and colleagues showed in 2017 that models that overfit are more confident on their training examples than on new ones, so a simple threshold on the confidence already gives the attacker an edge. Samuel Yeom and colleagues showed in 2018 that this edge, called the membership advantage, is tied to the gap between training and test accuracy. Our animation and program show it with a nearest neighbour model. When k is 1, the model has memorised every patient, gets 100 percent on training data and much less on new people, and the attacker’s advantage is large. When k is 25 the gap and the advantage both shrink. Model inversion, studied by Matt Fredrikson and colleagues in 2015, goes further and rebuilds recognisable faces from a face recognition model. In 2021 Nicholas Carlini and colleagues pulled hundreds of memorised training strings, including names, phone numbers and email addresses, out of the GPT-2 language model. Defences are less overfitting, regularisation, returning labels instead of full confidence scores, and above all differential privacy, which puts a mathematical cap on the advantage. Differential privacy has its own chapter later in this section.

gap = training accuracy − test accuracy
1.00 − 0.71 = 0.29, the same as the threshold attack’s advantage here.
Lesson 6 of 10

Model extraction: copying a model through its API

Training a good model costs data, time and money, so the model itself is valuable intellectual property. Many companies sell access through a prediction API: send an input, get back a score. In 2016 Florian Tramer and colleagues showed that such APIs can leak the model. For logistic regression, each probability returned can be turned back into the linear score with the logit function, and each score is a linear equation in the unknown weights. With d features there are d plus 1 unknowns, so d plus 1 well chosen queries solve the model exactly, as our program shows with 4 queries for 3 features. For decision trees and neural networks more queries are needed, but the attack still worked against real services at the time. A stolen model is a problem twice over: the attacker saves the cost, and can now test evasion attacks offline on the copy and use them against the real model, because adversarial examples often transfer between similar models. Defences from the defender’s side: return only labels or rounded scores, rate limit and charge for queries, watch for query patterns that look like systematic probing, such as unit vectors or points very near the boundary, add watermarks that let you prove a copy was stolen, and keep the most sensitive models behind authentication.

Lesson 7 of 10

Attacks on language model applications

Large language model applications add a new problem: the model reads instructions and data in the same stream of text and cannot reliably tell them apart. Direct prompt injection, often called jailbreaking, is when the user themselves writes text that talks the model out of its rules. Indirect prompt injection, described by Kai Greshake and colleagues in 2023, is more dangerous for security: the attacker hides instructions in content the assistant will read later, such as a web page, a document or an email. If the assistant can also act, for example send email, run code or make payments, those hidden instructions can steal data or take actions with the user’s authority. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications puts prompt injection first, and also lists sensitive information disclosure, supply chain risks, data and model poisoning, improper output handling, excessive agency, system prompt leakage, vector and embedding weaknesses, misinformation, and unbounded consumption. The key lesson is that defences must sit outside the model, because no prompt wording is a guarantee. Give the assistant the least privilege it needs, require a person to approve risky actions, treat everything the model outputs as untrusted input to the next system, keep untrusted content clearly marked, log tool calls, and test with red teams. Our program shows a small policy gate that blocks a hidden send email request even though the toy model was fooled.

Common mistake
No instruction in a prompt is a security boundary. Assume an attacker can make the model say or request anything, and design the tools and permissions so that this is still safe.
Lesson 8 of 10

The attacks compared

These attacks differ in when they strike, what access the attacker needs, and what they break. Evasion needs only the ability to send inputs. Poisoning needs a way into the training data. Privacy attacks and extraction need many queries. Prompt injection needs only a place where the assistant will read the attacker’s text.

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that AI can be fooled by odd inputs and bad training data. A student can run FGSM on a linear model, compute certified radii, measure membership advantage and extract a linear model. A professional threat models AI systems, adds data provenance and robust training, and secures LLM apps. Experts research certified defences and attacks on large models.
One idea, four depths

A few stickers can fool a sign reading camera, and bad examples in training teach a model bad habits. AI assistants can be tricked by hidden text.

FGSM, Δz = ε ‖w‖1, certified radius |z| / ‖w‖, label flipping and kNN cleaning, membership advantage TPR − FPR, d + 1 query extraction, OWASP LLM01.

Threat model AI systems with MITRE ATLAS, run adversarial training, secure the data and model supply chain, red team LLM apps, design tool permissions with least privilege and approvals.

Research certified robustness at scale, backdoor detection, privacy of large models, and provable isolation of instructions from data in language model agents.
