Lesson 1 of 9

Secret writing: the words and the one big rule

People have hidden messages for as long as they have written them. Spartan generals wound a strip of leather round a stick of a fixed thickness, called a scytale, and wrote along it. Julius Caesar shifted the letters of his letters. For two thousand years, most ciphers were invented by clever people and broken by cleverer ones. In the ninth century the Arab scholar al-Kindi wrote the first known description of frequency analysis, which broke almost every cipher of his time. In the sixteenth century Mary, Queen of Scots, was executed partly because her cipher letters were broken. In the twentieth century, Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski, and then Alan Turing and the team at Bletchley Park, broke the German Enigma machine, and historians believe this shortened the Second World War. Every one of these stories teaches the same lesson, which the Dutch linguist Auguste Kerckhoffs wrote down in 1883: a cipher must stay secure even if the enemy knows exactly how it works. Only the key may be secret. This chapter shows you the classical ciphers, how each one was broken, and the maths that tells us when a cipher is truly safe.

Source: Katz and Lindell, Introduction to Modern Cryptography, Stinson and Paterson, Cryptography Theory and Practice, Shannon (1949), Kahn, The Codebreakers.

What it is
Cryptography is the science of keeping messages secret and unchanged when an enemy can read the channel. A cipher turns plaintext into ciphertext using a key. Decryption turns it back. Cryptanalysis is the science of breaking ciphers.
Why we need it
Every password, bank payment, phone call and message you send relies on encryption. The classical ciphers are broken, but the ways they broke are the same ways modern systems fail.
How it works
Encryption E uses key k to turn a message m into c = Ek(m). Decryption D gives back m = Dk(c). The design is public. The key is secret, chosen at random from a set of keys called the key space.

Lesson 2 of 9

The Caesar cipher and modular arithmetic

The Roman historian Suetonius tells us that Julius Caesar wrote private letters by replacing each letter with the one three places further along the alphabet. A became D, B became E, and near the end of the alphabet, X wrapped round to A. That wrapping is the first piece of maths every cryptographer learns: arithmetic modulo 26, sometimes called clock arithmetic. On a clock, 3 hours after 11 is 2, not 14. With 26 letters, 3 places after X, which is letter 23, is 26, which wraps to 0, which is A. The Caesar cipher was probably good enough in Rome, because few enemies could read at all. Today it is a toy: there are only 26 possible shifts, so a computer, or a patient child, tries them all. A version called ROT13, a shift of 13, is still used online to hide spoilers, because applying it twice gives the original text.
What it is
The Caesar cipher replaces each letter with the letter k places later, wrapping from Z back to A. The shift k is the key.
Why we need it
It is the simplest example of a key, encryption, decryption and brute force, and it introduces modular arithmetic, which RSA and elliptic curves also use.
How it works
Number the letters A = 0 to Z = 25. Encrypt with C = (P + k) mod 26 and decrypt with P = (C − k) mod 26.
EncryptExam favourite
C = (P + k) mod 26
P and C are letter numbers, A = 0. X (23) with k = 3: 26 mod 26 = 0 = A.
DecryptExam favourite
P = (C − k) mod 26
Python’s % always gives a result from 0 to 25, even for negative numbers: (0 − 3) % 26 = 23.
Mod
a mod n = the remainder after dividing a by n
29 mod 26 = 3, because 29 = 1 × 26 + 3.

Lesson 3 of 9

Substitution ciphers and frequency analysis

A smarter idea than shifting is to scramble the whole alphabet. The key is any arrangement of the 26 letters, so A might become Q, B might become M, and so on. The number of possible keys is 26 factorial, about 4 times 10 to the power 26, more than the number of grains of sand on Earth. Nobody can try them all. For centuries people thought this made the cipher unbreakable. But al-Kindi noticed something in the ninth century. In any language, some letters are common and some are rare. In English, E is about 12.7 percent of letters, T about 9 percent, and Z less than a tenth of a percent. A substitution cipher changes the letters but not how often they appear. So the most common ciphertext letter probably stands for E. Then you look at common pairs like TH and common words like THE, and the whole message falls apart like a crossword puzzle. The lesson is deep: a huge key space is necessary, but it is not enough. A cipher must also hide the patterns in the message.
What it is
A monoalphabetic substitution cipher maps each plain letter to one fixed cipher letter. Frequency analysis breaks it by matching letter counts with the known counts of the language.
Why we need it
It proves that a big key space alone does not make a cipher safe. Modern ciphers are designed so that the ciphertext looks random, with no patterns left to count.
How it works
Count each ciphertext letter, compare with English, guess the most common letters first, then use common pairs and words to fix the rest. For a Caesar cipher, the chi-squared score picks the shift automatically.

The Vigenère cipher, Kasiski and the index of coincidence

To beat frequency analysis, sixteenth century cryptographers used several alphabets in turn. Giovan Battista Bellaso described the method in 1553, but it is named after Blaise de Vigenère, who wrote about a stronger version later. A key word such as LEMON sets the shift for each letter: L shifts the first letter by 11, E the second by 4, and so on, and then the key repeats. Now the letter A in the message turns into different ciphertext letters depending on where it falls, so the counts are smeared out. For three hundred years it was called le chiffre indéchiffrable, the unbreakable cipher. Then Charles Babbage broke it in the 1850s and kept quiet, and Friedrich Kasiski published a method in 1863. The key idea: because the key repeats, letters that are a key length apart are encrypted with the same shift. If you can find the key length, the cipher splits into a few simple Caesar ciphers. In 1922, the American cryptologist William Friedman gave a statistical tool for finding that length, the index of coincidence, which is the chance that two letters picked from a text are the same.
What it is
The Vigenère cipher adds a repeating key word to the message, letter by letter, modulo 26. It is a polyalphabetic cipher because it uses several Caesar alphabets in turn.
Why we need it
It hides single letter frequencies, so plain frequency analysis fails. Breaking it shows a general rule: any repetition in the key is a weakness.
How it works
Encrypt with Ci = (Pi + Ki mod m) mod 26. To break it, find the key length m (repeated pieces in the ciphertext appear at distances that are multiples of m, or the index of coincidence jumps at the right m), then break each of the m columns as a Caesar cipher.
VigenèreExam favourite
Ci = (Pi + Ki mod m) mod 26
m is the key length. Letters m apart use the same key letter.
Index of coincidenceExam favourite
IC = Σi ni(ni − 1) / ( N(N − 1) )
ni is the count of letter i, N the text length. English text gives about 0.066. Random letters give 1/26 ≈ 0.038.
Why IC finds m
columns at the right m are Caesar ciphers of English, so their IC ≈ 0.066
At a wrong m the columns mix shifts and look random, so IC stays near 0.038.

Lesson 5 of 9

The one time pad and perfect secrecy

Is any cipher truly unbreakable? Yes, and it is surprisingly simple. In 1917, Gilbert Vernam at AT&T built a teleprinter that combined each character of a message with a character from a paper tape of key. Joseph Mauborgne of the US Army added the crucial rule: the key must be truly random, as long as the message, and never used again. That is the one time pad. In 1949, Claude Shannon, the father of information theory, proved that it gives perfect secrecy: the ciphertext tells an attacker absolutely nothing about the message, no matter how much computing power they have. Every message of the same length is equally possible. But the price is heavy. You need as much secret key as you have messages, and you must deliver it safely in advance. And the rule of never reusing it is absolute. During the Second World War, Soviet pad factories under pressure printed some pages twice. American and British analysts in the Venona project spotted the reuse and, over many years, read thousands of Soviet intelligence messages. A perfect cipher was broken by one human shortcut.
What it is
The one time pad combines the message with a truly random key of the same length, usually with XOR: c = m ⊕ k. The key is used only once and then destroyed.
Why we need it
It is the only cipher proven to be unbreakable, and it explains why modern stream ciphers try to imitate a random pad with a short key.
How it works
XOR each message bit with a key bit. Decrypt with the same operation, since (m ⊕ k) ⊕ k = m. If a pad is used twice, c1 ⊕ c2 = m1 ⊕ m2, the key disappears, and guessing parts of one message reveals the other.

Perfect secrecy (Shannon)Exam favourite
P(M = m | C = c) = P(M = m)
Seeing the ciphertext does not change the attacker’s belief about any message.
Shannon’s key size boundExam favourite
|K| ≥ |M|
Perfect secrecy needs at least as many keys as messages. So the key must be as long as the message.
Two time pad
c1 ⊕ c2 = (m1 ⊕ k) ⊕ (m2 ⊕ k) = m1 ⊕ m2
The pad cancels. A guessed word in one message gives the matching letters of the other (crib dragging).
Going deeper: Why the one time pad is perfect: a short proof

Take any message m and ciphertext c of n bits. Exactly one key gives c from m, namely k = m ⊕ c. Every key has probability 2−n. So P(C = c | M = m) = 2−n for every m, which does not depend on m. By Bayes’ rule, P(M = m | C = c) = P(C = c | M = m) P(M = m) / P(C = c) = P(M = m), because P(C = c) is also 2−n. The ciphertext changes nothing.

Real world scenario: the Venona decrypts

In 1943 the US Army’s Signal Intelligence Service began studying thousands of coded Soviet telegrams. Analyst Richard Hallock noticed that some messages had been encrypted with pad pages already used for other messages. Using that reuse, a small team including Meredith Gardner slowly recovered parts of about 3,000 messages over almost 40 years, revealing Soviet spies inside the atomic bomb project. NSA released the Venona translations to the public in 1995.

The maths of breaking: key space, redundancy and unicity distance

Shannon’s 1949 paper did more than prove the one time pad perfect. It gave a way to measure how much ciphertext an attacker needs to break a cipher. The idea starts with redundancy. English is very predictable: after Q almost always comes U, and after TH comes E very often. Shannon estimated that English carries only about one to one and a half bits of real information per letter, even though a random letter could carry log two of 26, about 4.7 bits. The rest, around 3.2 bits per letter, is redundancy, and redundancy is what an attacker uses to tell a right key from a wrong one. If the key has H bits of uncertainty, then after about H divided by 3.2 letters of ciphertext, only one key will give sensible English. This number is called the unicity distance. For the simple substitution cipher, with about 88 bits of key, that is only about 28 letters. Below that, many keys give sensible text, and even a perfect attacker cannot be sure. Above it, the message is determined, and the only question is how much work finding it takes.
What it is
The key space size sets the brute force cost. Redundancy is how predictable a language is. The unicity distance is roughly how much ciphertext is needed before only one key makes sense.
Why we need it
These numbers tell you whether an attack is possible in principle (enough ciphertext) and practical (small enough key space or a shortcut).
How it works
Key entropy H(K) = log2(number of keys) for a random key. English redundancy D ≈ 3.2 bits per letter. Unicity distance U ≈ H(K) / D.
Key entropyExam favourite
H(K) = log2 |K|
Caesar: log2 26 ≈ 4.7 bits. Substitution: log2 26! ≈ 88.4 bits. AES-128: 128 bits.
Redundancy of English
D = log2 26 − HEnglish ≈ 4.7 − 1.5 = 3.2 bits per letter
A common textbook estimate. Shannon measured 0.6 to 1.3 bits for HEnglish with long context.
Unicity distanceExam favourite
U ≈ H(K) / D
Substitution: 88.4 / 3.2 ≈ 28 letters. Caesar: 4.7 / 3.2 ≈ 1.5 letters, so two letters already pin down the key.
Brute force time
average time = |K| / (2 × speed)
Half the key space on average. 2128 keys at 1012 per second still needs about 5 × 1018 years.
How to judge a cipher
  1. Count the keys: if a computer can try them all, it is broken.
  2. Look for patterns that survive encryption: letter counts, repeated blocks, repeated keys.
  3. Ask how much ciphertext the attacker gets compared with the unicity distance.
  4. Check the attack models: can the attacker choose plaintexts or get ciphertexts decrypted.
  5. Prefer ciphers whose security has been studied publicly for years.
Lesson 7 of 9

Classical ciphers compared

Lined up side by side, the classical ciphers show how cryptography grew up. Each new cipher fixed the weakness that broke the one before. Caesar had too few keys, so substitution added more keys. Substitution kept letter counts, so Vigenère used several alphabets. Vigenère repeated its key, so the one time pad used a key that never repeats. But the pad was too heavy to use widely. Machines like Enigma tried to get the best of both: a short key setting that produced a very long, non repeating stream of alphabets. The army Enigma, with three rotors and ten plugboard cables, had about 1.6 times 10 to the power 20 daily settings, yet it fell to a mix of mathematics, captured material, operator mistakes and the electromechanical bombes. Modern ciphers like AES take the final step. They are built so that, without the key, the output cannot be told apart from random, even when the attacker chooses the inputs.

Lesson 8 of 9

From beginner to expert

Classical cryptography is the gateway to the whole subject. A beginner learns the words and why short keys fail. A college student can break Caesar and Vigenère with code and explain frequency analysis and the index of coincidence. A masters student understands Shannon’s perfect secrecy proof, entropy and unicity distance, and the jump to modern security definitions, where we prove that no efficient attacker can tell the ciphertexts of two chosen messages apart. Researchers go further. Modern cryptography rests on reductions: a proof that breaking the cipher would solve a problem believed to be hard. And historians of cryptography still find new things. In 2023 a team of codebreakers decrypted more than fifty letters written in cipher by Mary, Queen of Scots, that had sat unread in the French national library for centuries.
One idea, four depths

A cipher scrambles a message with a key. Caesar shifts letters and has only 26 keys, so anyone can try them all. Counting letters breaks more complex letter swaps.

Use C = (P + k) mod 26, break Caesar by brute force or chi-squared, break substitution by frequency analysis, break Vigenère with the index of coincidence and per column analysis, and explain why one time pads must never be reused.

Prove the one time pad perfectly secret and the bound |K| ≥ |M|. Use entropy, redundancy and unicity distance. Move to computational security: indistinguishability under chosen plaintext attack (IND-CPA), where an efficient attacker must not tell apart encryptions of two messages they choose, with advantage only negligibly above 1/2.

Reduction proofs link a scheme’s security to hard problems. Pseudorandom generators stretch a short key into a long pad, and stream cipher security is defined by indistinguishability from a truly random string. Historical cryptanalysis continues with computer search: in 2023 Lasry, Biermann and Tomokiyo decrypted 57 letters of Mary, Queen of Scots, found in the Bibliothèque nationale de France.

Explain keys, ciphertext and why Caesar is weak.
Intermediate

Expert
Prove perfect secrecy, compute unicity distance and state IND-CPA security.
Lesson 9 of 9

Chapter summary

  1. A cipher turns plaintext into ciphertext with a key. Kerckhoffs: the method is public, only the key is secret.
  2. Caesar: C = (P + k) mod 26. Only 26 keys, so brute force breaks it at once.
  3. Substitution has 26! ≈ 288 keys but keeps letter counts, so frequency analysis breaks it. χ2 = Σ (O − E)2 / E scores how English a text looks.
  4. Vigenère: Ci = (Pi + Ki mod m) mod 26. Find m with Kasiski or the index of coincidence (English ≈ 0.066, random ≈ 0.038), then break each column.
  5. The one time pad (c = m ⊕ k, random key as long as the message, used once) has perfect secrecy: P(M | C) = P(M). It needs |K| ≥ |M|.
  6. Reusing a pad gives c1 ⊕ c2 = m1 ⊕ m2. Venona read Soviet messages this way.
  7. Unicity distance U ≈ H(K) / D with D ≈ 3.2 bits per English letter. Substitution: about 28 letters.
  8. A big key space is necessary but not enough. A good cipher must also leave no patterns.
