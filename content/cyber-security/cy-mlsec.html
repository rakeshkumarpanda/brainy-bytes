Lesson 1 of 10

Why security teams use machine learning

Security teams face more data than people can read. A medium sized company can log billions of events a day, receive millions of emails, and see hundreds of thousands of new malware files every day around the world. Machine learning, or ML, means letting a program learn patterns from examples instead of writing every rule by hand. The idea is old in security. In 1987 Dorothy Denning published a model for intrusion detection that learned each user’s normal behaviour and flagged big changes, and the IDES system at SRI was built on it. In 2002 Paul Graham’s essay A Plan for Spam showed that a simple Bayesian filter could catch more than 99 percent of his spam with very few mistakes, and spam filters everywhere adopted the idea. In 2019 Google said its TensorFlow models were blocking an extra 100 million spam messages a day in Gmail. Today ML sits inside email filters, antivirus and endpoint detection, fraud checks on card payments, user behaviour analytics in the SOC, and tools that rank which vulnerabilities to patch first. But security is a hard place for ML. Attacks are rare, so even a small false alarm rate floods analysts. Labels are expensive and often wrong. And the other side adapts: attackers change their emails and malware to slip past whatever the model learned. Robin Sommer and Vern Paxson warned in 2010 that most of the hard work is in the data, the labels and honest testing. This chapter builds small versions of the main ideas so you can see both the power and the limits.

Source: Denning (1987), Graham (2002), Axelsson (1999 and 2000), McHugh (2000), Tavallaee et al. (2009), Sommer and Paxson (2010), Liu, Ting and Zhou (2008), Iglewicz and Hoaglin (1993), Anderson and Roth, EMBER (2018), Google blog on Gmail and TensorFlow (2019), APWG reports, Lundberg and Lee, SHAP (2017).

What it is
Machine learning for security means training models on examples of normal activity and attacks so they can score new emails, files, logins or network flows as suspicious or safe.
Why we need it
There is too much data for people and fixed rules alone, attackers change small details to dodge signatures, and models can spot patterns across thousands of signals at once.
How it works
Collect data, label it or learn what normal looks like, turn raw data into features, train and test honestly, pick a threshold, put the model in front of analysts, take their feedback, and retrain as the world drifts.

Lesson 2 of 10

Naive Bayes: the classic spam and phishing filter

Naive Bayes is the simplest useful text classifier and still a strong baseline. It comes straight from Bayes rule, which you met in the foundations maths chapter. We want the probability that an email is phishing given its words. Bayes rule turns that around: it is proportional to the prior chance of phishing times the chance of seeing those words in phishing mail. The naive part is the assumption that words appear independently once we know the class. That is clearly false, because urgent and verify often appear together, but the method still ranks emails well. Multiplying many small probabilities underflows to zero on a computer, so we add logarithms instead, and we compare phishing against safe mail as a log odds score. A word never seen in phishing would give a probability of zero and wipe out all other evidence, so we use Laplace smoothing, which adds one to every count. Paul Graham’s 2002 filter and SpamAssassin’s Bayes module worked this way, and the user’s own Report spam clicks became the training labels. Attackers answered with tricks such as Bayesian poisoning, padding spam with innocent words, and putting the text inside images, which pushed filters to add image analysis and sender reputation. Bayes rule for a class given the wordsExam favourite
P(phish | words) ∝ P(phish) × Π P(wi | phish)
The product runs over the words of the email, assumed independent given the class.
Log odds scoreExam favourite
score = log(P(phish) / P(safe)) + Σ log(P(wi | phish) / P(wi | safe))
score above 0 means phishing is more likely, and P(phish) = 1 / (1 + e−score).
Laplace smoothingExam favourite
P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + 1) / (Nc + |V|)
"urgent" twice in 23 phish words with 31 words in the vocabulary: 3 / 54 ≈ 0.0556.
Going deeper: Why the log and why smoothing

Fifty words with probabilities near 0.01 multiply to about 10−100, close to the limit of floating point numbers, while their logs simply add to about −230. Smoothing is a prior belief that every word is possible in every class. With add one smoothing the estimate is the mean of a Beta (or Dirichlet) posterior with a uniform prior, which links this trick to Bayesian statistics.

Lesson 3 of 10

Features and logistic regression for phishing links

A model cannot read a web address the way you do. It needs numbers, called features, that capture what an expert would notice. For a phishing link those include the length of the address, how many dots and subdomains it has, whether it uses an at sign or hyphens that confuse readers, whether it uses HTTPS, digits in the host name, and how random the host name looks, measured by Shannon entropy. Logistic regression is the standard first model for this. It multiplies each feature by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and squashes the total into a probability with the sigmoid function. Training adjusts the weights a little after each example in the direction that lowers the log loss, which is called gradient descent. The program below trains on twelve made up addresses. Look at what it learns. Every phishing address in our training set used plain HTTP, so the model leans heavily on the no HTTPS feature, and a phishing address with HTTPS slips through. This is called shortcut learning. It happened in real life too. In 2017 the Anti Phishing Working Group found under a third of phishing sites used HTTPS, and by 2021 the share was over 80 percent, because free certificates made it easy. A model trained on old data would fail in exactly this way. Good teams check what features a model relies on, train on recent data, and add features attackers find hard to fake, such as the age of the domain and its registration details.

Logistic regressionExam favourite

Log loss and its gradientExam favourite
L = −[y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p)], ∂L/∂wi = (p − y) xi
p = 0.8 for a phish (y = 1) and feature 0.5: gradient (0.8 − 1) × 0.5 = −0.1, so the weight grows.
Shannon entropy of a string
H = −Σ pc log2 pc
"aabb": two letters each with p = 0.5, so H = 1 bit per character.
Common mistake
Shortcut learning is one of the most common failures of security ML. A model can score 99% on a test set drawn from the same old data and still fail on next month’s attacks. Always test on data collected after the training data.
Lesson 4 of 10

Anomaly detection: learning what normal looks like

Supervised learning needs labelled attacks, and new attacks have no labels yet. Anomaly detection takes the other road: learn what normal looks like and flag what is far from it. This was Denning’s 1987 idea, and it powers user and entity behaviour analytics today. The simplest version is a z score: how many standard deviations a value is from the mean. But attacks themselves pull the mean and standard deviation, which can hide them. The program shows this with an employee’s daily uploads. Two huge days inflate the standard deviation so much that one of them looks normal. The fix is robust statistics: use the median and the median absolute deviation, called MAD, which barely move when a few values are extreme. Boris Iglewicz and David Hoaglin suggested flagging a modified z score above 3.5. For many features at once, a popular method is the isolation forest, from Fei Tony Liu, Kai Ming Ting and Zhi-Hua Zhou in 2008. Instead of describing normal data, it tries to isolate each point with random cuts. Odd points sit alone in empty space, so a few cuts separate them, while normal points need many. Averaging the number of cuts over many random trees gives an anomaly score. The weakness of all anomaly detection is that unusual is not the same as malicious. A finance team closing the books, a new backup job, or staff working from a new city all look odd. So anomaly scores are usually combined with context and sent to analysts as leads, not as proof.

z scoreExam favourite

Modified (robust) z scoreExam favourite
M = 0.6745 (x − median) / MAD, flag when |M| > 3.5
x = 900, median 127.5, MAD 7.5: M ≈ 69.5, a clear outlier.
Isolation forest score
s(x, n) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(n), c(n) = 2H(n − 1) − 2(n − 1)/n
H(i) ≈ ln i + 0.5772. n = 256: c ≈ 10.24. E[h] = 2 gives s ≈ 0.87, an anomaly.
Going deeper: Why 0.6745

For normally distributed data the MAD is about 0.6745 times the standard deviation, because 0.6745 is the 75th percentile of the standard normal distribution. Multiplying by 0.6745 puts the robust score on the same scale as an ordinary z score, so 3.5 means roughly the same thing.

Lesson 5 of 10

Measuring a detector honestly

Accuracy is the wrong number for security. If one event in a thousand is an attack, a detector that never alerts is 99.9 percent accurate and useless. Instead we use the confusion matrix: true positives are attacks caught, false positives are false alarms, false negatives are attacks missed, and true negatives are normal events left alone. Precision is the share of alerts that are real, which decides how much analyst time is wasted. Recall, also called the true positive rate or detection rate, is the share of attacks caught. F1 is their harmonic mean. Every threshold trades one for the other. The ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate for every threshold, and the area under it, AUC, is the chance that a random attack scores higher than a random normal event. Stefan Axelsson showed in 1999 why intrusion detection is so hard: because attacks are so rare, even a one percent false positive rate means most alerts are false. Testing also needs care. The famous KDD Cup 1999 intrusion dataset was criticised by John McHugh in 2000 and by later researchers for duplicate records and traffic that did not look like real networks, and NSL-KDD in 2009 removed the duplicates. Models that score near perfectly on such data often fail on real networks. Test on recent data from your own environment, split by time rather than at random, and report precision at the alert volume your team can really handle.

F1 scoreExam favourite
F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)

False positive rateExam favourite
FPR = FP / (FP + TN)
FP 4, TN 7: 4 / 11 ≈ 0.364.
AUC as a ranking chance
AUC = P(score of a random attack > score of a random normal event)
47 winning pairs out of 5 × 11 = 55: AUC ≈ 0.855.

precision = r · TPR / (r · TPR + (1 − r) · FPR)

Lesson 6 of 10

Classifying malware by what it does

Antivirus started with signatures: exact byte patterns or file hashes of known malware. They are precise but miss anything new, and changing one byte changes the hash. Machine learning adds two kinds of features. Static features come from the file without running it: the size of each section of a Windows program, the libraries and functions it imports, printable strings, and the entropy of each section, where very high entropy suggests the code is packed or encrypted. Dynamic features come from running the file in a sandbox and recording its behaviour: which files it opens, which registry keys it changes, which network addresses it contacts, and which Windows API calls it makes. Ransomware, for example, tends to list files, encrypt them, write new copies and delete shadow copies with vssadmin. The program compares the API calls of new samples with known families using Jaccard similarity, the size of the overlap divided by the size of the union, and picks the nearest neighbour. In 2018 Hyrum Anderson and Phil Roth released EMBER, a public set of features from 1.1 million Windows programs, so researchers could compare models fairly. Malware authors respond with packing, delaying behaviour until the sandbox gives up, and checking whether they are inside a virtual machine. So real products combine signatures, static ML, behaviour monitoring on the endpoint and cloud reputation, and every model verdict on an unknown file is backed by a way to undo damage.

Lesson 7 of 10

Drift, adaptive attackers and explaining the model

A security model starts to go stale the day it is deployed. Data drift means the inputs change, for example attackers switching from small Word attachments to large ISO and ZIP files, which happened in 2022 after Microsoft started blocking macros in files from the internet by default. Concept drift means the link between inputs and labels changes, so the same features now mean something different. In security much of the drift is deliberate, because attackers test their emails and malware against popular products before they send them. Teams watch for drift with simple statistics. The population stability index, PSI, compares the share of data in each bin today with the share at training time. A common rule of thumb says below 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 needs watching, and above 0.25 needs action. Analysts also need to know why a model flagged something, both to trust it and to act fast. Methods such as SHAP, by Scott Lundberg and Su-In Lee in 2017, split a score into the contribution of each feature, so an alert can say this login was flagged mainly because of the new country and the 3 AM hour. Finally, a model can itself be attacked: poisoned through its training data, fooled by carefully changed inputs, copied, or made to leak its training data. That is the subject of the next chapter.

Rules, supervised and unsupervised detection compared

No single method wins. Rules written by experts are precise and easy to explain but only catch what someone thought of. Supervised models learn from labelled examples and generalise to variants of known attacks. Unsupervised anomaly detection can find new things but raises many false alarms. Mature teams layer all three and let analysts decide.
Three ways to detect attacks

Bottom line: Start with rules for known bad, add supervised models where you have good labels, and use anomaly scores as leads for analysts to investigate.

Lesson 9 of 10

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that spam filters and antivirus learn from examples. A student can build a naive Bayes filter, train logistic regression, compute robust z scores and judge a detector with precision, recall and AUC. A professional runs these models in production with honest evaluation, drift monitoring and analyst feedback. Experts research robust and explainable security models and how attackers adapt to them.
One idea, four depths

Your email app learns which messages are spam from millions of examples, and antivirus learns what bad programs look like.

Naive Bayes with log odds and smoothing, logistic regression and gradient descent, robust z scores and isolation forest, precision, recall, F1, ROC AUC and the base rate, Jaccard similarity, PSI.

Build detection models with time split evaluation, tune thresholds to analyst capacity, monitor drift, explain alerts with SHAP, and run feedback loops with the SOC.

Research robustness to adaptive attackers, learning with few and noisy labels, graph and sequence models for intrusion detection, and language models as SOC assistants with their new risks.
