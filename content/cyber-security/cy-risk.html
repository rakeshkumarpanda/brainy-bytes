Lesson 1 of 10

What risk is and why we measure it

Every day you manage risk without thinking about it. You lock your bicycle, you carry an umbrella when the sky is grey, and you do not keep all your money in one pocket. You are weighing two questions: how likely is something bad, and how bad would it be? Companies must answer the same two questions, but they have thousands of assets and limited money. They cannot protect everything perfectly, so they must choose. Risk management is the method for choosing well. It grew out of insurance, where people have priced risk for hundreds of years, and it was brought into computing by standards bodies such as NIST in the United States and ISO internationally. In this chapter you will learn to put a number on risk, both with simple scores and with money, and to decide what to do about each risk.

Source: NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1, NIST SP 800-37 Rev. 2, ISO/IEC 27005:2022, the CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 objectives and the Open Group FAIR standard.

What it is
Risk is the chance that a threat uses a vulnerability to harm an asset, combined with how much harm it would do. In one line: risk = likelihood × impact.
Why we need it
Money, people and time are limited. Without measuring risk, teams fix whatever is loudest, not what is most dangerous. Measuring risk lets you compare very different problems (a flood, a phishing attack, a lost laptop) on one scale and spend where it matters most.
How it works
List the assets, the threats and the weaknesses. Score each risk for likelihood and impact, with words (low to critical) or with money (expected loss per year). Sort the list, compare with the risk the business is willing to accept, and pick a response for each risk above that line.
Real world scenario: the school server room

A school keeps its marks server in a small room. The head teacher worries about three things: a hacker, a flood and a stolen laptop. Floods are rare here (once in 20 years) but would destroy the server. Laptops are stolen about once a year but hold only a few files. Phishing emails arrive every week, and one success could leak every student’s data. Ranking these by likelihood and impact shows that phishing training and backups matter far more than a waterproof door, even though the flood sounds scarier.

RiskExam favourite
risk = likelihood × impact
Likelihood: how often. Impact: how bad one event is. Both can be scores (1 to 5) or numbers (events per year, rupees).
Inherent and residual risk
residual risk = inherent risk − what the controls remove
Inherent risk is the risk before any control. Residual risk is what is left after controls. Some residual risk always stays.
Remember
Risk is never zero. The goal is to bring it down to a level the business accepts, called the risk appetite, at a sensible cost.

Qualitative risk: the risk matrix

The quickest way to talk about risk is with simple words and a coloured chart. You ask a group of people who know the system: how likely is this, from very rare to almost certain? And how bad would it be, from small to disastrous? You turn each answer into a number from one to five and multiply. This is called qualitative risk assessment, because it uses judgement rather than exact money. It is fast and everyone understands the colours. Its weakness is that the numbers are opinions, and a score of twelve is not truly twice as bad as a score of six. Still, almost every company starts here, and the risk matrix you are about to build is the picture you will see in board meetings all over the world.

Common mistake
Matrix scores are labels, not real measurements. Do not add or average them across risks, and do not say a 12 is exactly twice a 6. Use them to sort and to start a conversation.
Lesson 3 of 10

Quantitative risk: SLE, ARO and ALE

Managers and finance teams like money more than colours. Quantitative risk assessment turns each risk into rupees or dollars per year. The idea comes straight from insurance. If something worth ten lakh rupees loses forty percent of its value each time it is hit, one hit costs four lakh. If that happens once every two years, then on average it costs two lakh per year. This yearly number is called the annualised loss expectancy, or ALE. Once every risk has an ALE, you can compare it directly with the price of a control. If a firewall costs one lakh a year and cuts the expected loss by three lakh a year, it pays for itself. These short names, SLE, ARO and ALE, are asked in almost every security certification exam, so learn them well.
What it is
Quantitative risk assessment gives each risk a money value per year, so it can be compared with the cost of controls.
Why we need it
A board must decide between buying a backup system, more training or cyber insurance. Money makes these choices comparable and easy to explain.
How it works
Find the asset value (AV) and the share lost in one incident, the exposure factor (EF). Their product is the single loss expectancy (SLE). Multiply by the number of incidents expected per year, the annualised rate of occurrence (ARO), to get the annualised loss expectancy (ALE).
Single loss expectancyExam favourite
SLE = AV × EF
AV is the asset value in money. EF is the fraction of that value lost in one incident, from 0 to 1.
Annualised loss expectancyExam favourite
ALE = SLE × ARO
ARO is how many incidents are expected per year. Once in 4 years is 0.25.
Value of a control (safeguard)Exam favourite
value = ALEbefore − ALEafter − annual cost of the control
Positive means the control saves more than it costs.

Worked example: should a shop buy a backup service?
  1. The online shop’s order database is worth AV = 10,00,000 rupees. Ransomware would destroy 60% of its value, so EF = 0.6.
  2. SLE = 10,00,000 × 0.6 = 6,00,000 rupees
  3. Ransomware is expected once every 5 years, so ARO = 0.2.
  4. ALE = 6,00,000 × 0.2 = 1,20,000 rupees per year
  5. A backup service costs 30,000 a year and cuts EF to 0.05 (only a few hours of orders are lost). New SLE = 50,000, new ALE = 10,000.
  6. value = 1,20,000 − 10,000 − 30,000 = 80,000 rupees per year
  7. The value is positive, so the backup service is worth buying.
In the exam
ARO is a rate per year, not a probability. An event expected twice a year has ARO = 2. For rare events the ARO and the yearly probability are close, but they are not the same thing.
Lesson 4 of 10

What to do with a risk: four responses and control types

Once you know a risk is too high, you have only four choices, and they apply to life in general. Suppose you are worried about losing your phone. You could stop carrying a phone, which avoids the risk. You could buy a strong case and turn on tracking, which reduces it. You could buy phone insurance, which transfers the money loss to someone else. Or you could decide the phone is cheap and simply accept the risk. Companies do exactly the same with cyber risk. Then, to reduce a risk, they use controls, and security people sort controls in two ways: by what kind of thing they are, and by what job they do. Exams ask about both, so we will build a table you can memorise.
ResponsePlain meaningExample
AvoidStop doing the risky thingDo not store card numbers at all, let a payment company handle them
Reduce (mitigate)Add controls to lower likelihood or impactPatching, MFA, backups, training
Transfer (share)Move the money loss to someone elseCyber insurance, a contract that makes a supplier pay for its mistakes
AcceptLive with it, with a written sign off by someone seniorAn old internal printer with a known bug, behind a firewall, worth little

Technical (logical)Firewalls, encryption, MFA, antivirus, access control lists
Managerial (administrative)Policies, risk assessments, security awareness programmes, hiring checks
OperationalGuards checking IDs, backup procedures, patch routines, done by people

In the exam
One control can belong to a category and a type at once. A CCTV camera is a physical control that is both deterrent (people see it) and detective (it records).
Common mistake
Accepting a risk is a real decision only when an owner with authority signs it, with a date to review. Ignoring a risk is not accepting it.
Lesson 5 of 10

Threat modelling: STRIDE and attack trees

Before a building is built, an architect thinks about fire exits, earthquakes and burglars. Threat modelling is the same habit for software and systems: you think like an attacker before the attacker does. In 1999 two Microsoft engineers, Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg, gave the world a simple checklist called STRIDE, six kinds of threats, one for each letter. The same year, the cryptographer Bruce Schneier described attack trees. You write the attacker’s goal at the top, and below it all the ways to reach it. Some ways need several steps together, and some are alternatives. Then you put a cost or a chance on each leaf and work upwards, like adding up a family tree. The cheapest route tells you where to put your money first.
STRIDE threatIt breaksExampleControl
SpoofingAuthenticityLogging in with someone else’s stolen passwordMFA, strong authentication
TamperingIntegrityChanging a price in a web requestSignatures, server side checks, hashes
RepudiationNon repudiationA user says "I never sent that order"Signed records, secure logs
Information disclosureConfidentialityAn error page shows the database passwordEncryption, least privilege, safe error messages
Denial of serviceAvailabilityFlooding the login pageRate limits, DDoS filtering, capacity
Elevation of privilegeAuthorisationA normal user gets admin rights through a bugLeast privilege, patching, input checks

Attack tree with costsExam favourite
AND node: cost = sum of children    OR node: cost = smallest child
The attacker pays for every step of an AND route, and picks the cheapest OR route.
Attack tree with chances
AND node: p = p1 × p2 × ...    OR node: p = 1 − (1 − p1)(1 − p2)...
Assumes the steps are independent and that the attacker tries every OR route.
Remember
Fix the cheapest route first. Making an expensive route even more expensive does nothing while a cheap route is still open.

Better numbers: FAIR and Monte Carlo simulation

A single ALE number hides something important. A risk that costs one lakh every year and a risk that costs nothing for ninety nine years and then ten crore once can have the same average, but they are very different for a business. Real losses are uncertain, so modern risk analysts use ranges instead of single numbers. The FAIR method, which stands for Factor Analysis of Information Risk, was created by Jack Jones in the early 2000s and is now an Open Group standard. It breaks risk into how often loss events happen and how big each loss is, gives each a range, and then uses a computer to play out thousands of possible years. This is called Monte Carlo simulation, named after the famous casino, because it is driven by random numbers. The result is not one number but a picture: the average year, a bad year, and a very bad year.
What it is
Monte Carlo risk simulation plays out thousands of random years using ranges for how often incidents happen and how much each costs, then reports the average and the bad tail.
Why we need it
Averages hide rare but huge losses. A company needs to know how bad a 1 in 10 or 1 in 100 year could be, to choose insurance and reserves.
How it works
For each simulated year, draw whether incidents happen (for example a small chance each month) and draw a cost for each one from its range. Repeat many times, sort the yearly losses and read off the average and the high percentiles.

FAIR top levelExam favourite
risk = loss event frequency × loss magnitude
Loss event frequency = threat event frequency × vulnerability (the chance a threat event becomes a loss).
Percentile of yearly loss
90th percentile = the loss that only 1 year in 10 is worse than
Also called loss exceedance. Insurers and boards often ask for the 1 in 10 and the 1 in 100 year loss.
Going deeper: Why the average alone is dangerous

In the simulation above most years have no breach at all, yet the average is far from zero because of the rare bad years. Cyber losses have heavy tails: a few events cause most of the total loss. Research on breach sizes finds distributions such as the log normal or power law, where the mean can be much larger than the typical (median) event. This is why FAIR reports a loss exceedance curve, the chance that yearly loss exceeds each amount, instead of one ALE.

Availability numbers: RTO, RPO, MTBF and the nines

Some risks are not about stolen data but about time. If a hospital system stops, how long can doctors manage without it? If a bank loses its database, how many minutes of transactions can it afford to lose? These questions lead to a set of time numbers that every business continuity plan uses. The recovery time objective is how fast you must be back. The recovery point objective is how much data, measured in time, you can lose. Engineers also measure how often things break and how long repairs take. From these comes the famous language of nines: three nines means up ninety nine point nine percent of the time, which still allows almost nine hours of downtime a year.
TermPlain meaningExample
RTO (recovery time objective)The longest the service may be down before it must be backOnline banking RTO 2 hours
RPO (recovery point objective)The most data, counted in time, that may be lostRPO 15 minutes means back up at least every 15 minutes
MTD (maximum tolerable downtime)After this long, the business suffers severe damage. RTO must be shorterA shop may survive 3 days without its website
MTBF (mean time between failures)Average time a system runs before it fails1000 hours
MTTR (mean time to repair)Average time to fix it after a failure2 hours
Availability from MTBF and MTTRExam favourite
A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)
The share of time the system is working.
Downtime allowedExam favourite
downtime per year = (1 − A) × 525,600 minutes
99.9% gives about 525.6 minutes (8.76 hours). 99.99% gives about 52.6 minutes. 99.999% gives about 5.3 minutes.
Two parts in a chain (both needed)
A = A1 × A2
A chain is weaker than each part.
Two copies in parallel (either works)
A = 1 − (1 − A1)(1 − A2)
A spare copy raises availability, the same maths as an OR node.

Risk frameworks used in the real world

Big organisations do not invent their own way of managing risk. They follow published frameworks so that auditors, customers and regulators can trust the process. The two most used families are from NIST, the American standards institute, and ISO, the international standards organisation. NIST’s Risk Management Framework describes seven steps that run in a cycle, from preparing the organisation to monitoring controls forever. ISO 27005 gives guidance on information security risk that fits inside the ISO 27001 management system, which thousands of companies around the world are certified against. You do not need to memorise every page, but you should know the steps and why they repeat.
NIST RMF step (SP 800-37 Rev. 2)What happens
1. PrepareSet roles, risk strategy and appetite for the organisation and the system
2. CategoriseDecide how bad a loss of C, I or A would be for this system (low, moderate, high)
3. SelectChoose controls from the NIST SP 800-53 catalogue that fit the category
4. ImplementPut the controls in place and write down how
5. AssessTest that the controls work as intended
6. AuthoriseA senior official accepts the remaining risk and allows the system to run
7. MonitorKeep watching controls, changes and new threats, and repeat
Ways to assess risk

Bottom line: Start with a matrix to find the big risks fast, use ALE to justify spending on them, and use FAIR style simulation for the few risks where rare huge losses matter, such as ransomware or a major data leak.

Lesson 9 of 10

From beginner to expert

Risk sounds like a manager’s topic, but at the top level it becomes serious mathematics. A beginner learns that risk is likelihood times impact. A college student uses SLE, ARO and ALE and picks controls by cost. A masters student models uncertainty with probability distributions, simulation and decision theory, and asks how risks are linked to each other. Researchers study cyber insurance markets, how risks spread across companies that share the same software, and why companies under invest in security when the harm falls on others. As you move up, notice that the questions stay the same, how likely and how bad, but the tools become sharper.
One idea, four depths

Risk is how likely something bad is and how bad it would be. We fix the biggest risks first, and we can never make risk zero.

Use a risk matrix to rank risks, and SLE = AV × EF, ALE = SLE × ARO to price them. Choose avoid, reduce, transfer or accept for each risk above the appetite, and pick controls by category and type.

Model frequency and severity as distributions (for example Poisson events with log normal losses), simulate yearly loss, and report expected loss and value at risk. Threat models (STRIDE, attack trees) feed the likelihoods. Controls are chosen to maximise risk reduction per rupee, subject to a budget.

Research covers correlated cyber risk (one cloud outage or one software flaw hitting thousands of firms at once, which makes cyber insurance hard to price), game theoretic models of attacker and defender investment, the Gordon and Loeb model and its extensions, and externalities: when a firm’s weak security mainly harms others, the market under invests, which is why laws such as breach notification rules exist.

Beginner
Explain risk, read a risk matrix and name the four responses.
Intermediate
Compute SLE, ALE and control value, availability and downtime, and evaluate an attack tree.
Expert
Run a Monte Carlo loss model, read percentiles, and explain correlated risk and security externalities.

Chapter summary

  1. Risk = likelihood × impact. Inherent risk is before controls, residual risk is after. Risk appetite is how much the business accepts.
  2. A risk matrix scores likelihood and impact from 1 to 5. Scores rank risks but are opinions, so do not add them.
  3. SLE = AV × EF, ALE = SLE × ARO, and a control is worth buying when ALEbefore − ALEafter − cost > 0.
  4. Four responses: avoid, reduce, transfer, accept. Control categories: technical, managerial, operational, physical. Types: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive.
  5. STRIDE: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege.
  6. Attack trees: AND adds costs (multiplies chances), OR takes the cheapest (1 minus the product of failure chances). Fix the cheapest route first.
  7. FAIR and Monte Carlo use ranges and simulation to show the average loss and the bad 1 in 10 or 1 in 100 year.
  8. RTO is time to restore, RPO is data you may lose. A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). 99.9% allows about 8.76 hours of downtime a year.
