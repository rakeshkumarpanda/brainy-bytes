Lesson 1 of 12

What RSA is and where it came from

In April 1977, three young researchers at MIT, Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman, were trying to turn the idea of Diffie and Hellman into a working system. Diffie and Hellman had shown how two people could agree on a key in public, but they had no way to encrypt a message to someone with a key anyone could look up. Rivest and Shamir proposed schemes, and Adleman, a number theorist, broke them one after another for months. Then, after a Passover dinner, Rivest could not sleep and wrote down a scheme that Adleman could not break. It used two large primes, multiplied together. In August 1977 Martin Gardner described it in his Scientific American column and printed a challenge: a 129 digit number to factor, with a prize of one hundred dollars. The authors guessed it would take forty quadrillion years. A team of 600 volunteers factored it in 1994. Later it came out that Clifford Cocks at the British agency GCHQ had found the same scheme in 1973, within weeks of joining, but it was kept secret until 1997. The RSA patent expired in September 2000, and RSA became the most widely used public key system in the world.

Source: Rivest, Shamir and Adleman, A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public Key Cryptosystems (CACM, 1978), RFC 8017 (PKCS #1 v2.2), FIPS 186-5 (2023), Boneh, Twenty Years of Attacks on the RSA Cryptosystem (1999), NIST IR 8547 (draft, 2024).

What it is
RSA is a public key system. The public key is a pair (n, e) where n = p q is the product of two secret primes. The private key is d with e d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)). Encrypt with c = me mod n, decrypt with m = cd mod n.
Why we need it
Anyone can encrypt to you or check your signature using only the public key. Only you can decrypt or sign. It made secure email, software signing, HTTPS certificates and smart cards possible.
How it works
Making the key needs primes, an inverse and Euler’s theorem from Cryptography 2.1. Breaking it seems to need factoring n, which is believed to be infeasible for n of 2048 bits or more.

Lesson 2 of 12

Making a key, encrypting and decrypting

Here is the whole of RSA in five steps. First, pick two large random primes p and q of the same size, for example 1024 bits each. Second, multiply them to get n, the modulus. Third, compute phi of n, which is p minus 1 times q minus 1. Fourth, choose the public exponent e, which must share no factor with phi. Almost everyone uses 65537, a prime equal to 2 to the 16 plus 1, because its binary form has only two ones, so encryption needs just 17 multiplications. Fifth, compute d, the inverse of e modulo phi, with the extended Euclidean algorithm. Publish n and e. Keep d secret, and either keep p and q for fast decryption or delete them. To encrypt, turn the message into a number m smaller than n and compute m to the e modulo n. To decrypt, compute c to the d modulo n. Try the animation with the classic example from many textbooks, p equal to 61 and q equal to 53. Then try your own primes, and see what happens when e shares a factor with phi.

Lesson 3 of 12

Why decryption always works: the proof

A careful student will ask a fair question. Euler’s theorem only promises that m to the phi of n is 1 when m shares no factor with n. What if the message happens to be a multiple of p? Then Euler’s theorem says nothing, and yet RSA still works. The neat way to prove it uses the Chinese remainder theorem. Instead of working modulo n, we check the answer modulo p and modulo q separately. Modulo p, either p divides m, and then both sides are zero, or it does not, and then Fermat’s little theorem does the job. The same holds modulo q. Since the result is right modulo both primes, the Chinese remainder theorem says it is right modulo n. This proof is a favourite in university exams. The program checks it for every single message for the small key with n equal to 55, including the fifteen messages that share a factor with n. In practice, such a message would reveal a factor of n by a simple gcd, but with 1024 bit primes the chance of picking one at random is about one in two to the 1023, so it never happens by accident.
CorrectnessExam favourite
(me)d ≡ m (mod n) for every m from 0 to n − 1
Holds whenever e d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) or (mod λ(n)), and n is a product of different primes.
Going deeper: The full proof, covering every message

Write e d = 1 + k(p − 1)(q − 1) for some whole number k ≥ 0. We show med ≡ m (mod p).

Case 1: p divides m. Then m ≡ 0 (mod p) and med ≡ 0 (mod p). Both sides agree.

Case 2: p does not divide m. By Fermat, mp−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). So med = m · (mp−1)k(q−1) ≡ m · 1 = m (mod p).

The same argument with the roles swapped gives med ≡ m (mod q). Now p and q are different primes, so both divide med − m and so does their product. Therefore med ≡ m (mod n). With λ(n) in place of φ(n) the proof is the same, because λ(n) is still a multiple of p − 1 and of q − 1.

Lesson 4 of 12

RSA signatures

RSA can run backwards. If you raise a value to the private exponent d, anyone can raise the result to the public exponent e and get the value back. Only the owner of d could have made it, so it works as a signature. In practice we never sign the message itself. We first hash it with SHA-256, then pad the hash, then apply d. The hash keeps the signature short, whatever the length of the message, and blocks two simple forgeries. Without a hash, anyone can choose a random number s and compute s to the e. The pair is a valid message and signature, though the message is random gibberish. Signatures also multiply: the product of signatures on a and on b is a signature on a times b. These tricks are called existential forgery, and they show why textbook RSA is not a safe signature scheme on its own. The modern padding for signatures is PSS, proved secure by Mihir Bellare and Phillip Rogaway in 1996. RSA signatures still protect most software updates, code signing certificates and many HTTPS certificates today.
What it is
An RSA signature on message M is s = pad(H(M))d mod n. Anyone checks it by computing se mod n and comparing with pad(H(M)).
Why we need it
It proves who signed and that the message was not changed, and the signer cannot later deny it. This is the basis of certificates, code signing and signed documents.
How it works
Hash the message, pad the hash with PSS (or the older PKCS #1 v1.5 signature padding), raise to d. To verify, raise to e and check the padding and hash.

Lesson 5 of 12

Fast decryption with CRT, and the fault attack

Decryption with a 2048 bit exponent is the slow part of RSA. The owner of the key knows p and q, so they can use the Chinese remainder theorem from the last chapter. They compute the answer modulo p using a reduced exponent, d modulo p minus 1, and modulo q using d modulo q minus 1, and then join the two halves with a short formula found by Harvey Garner in 1959. Each half uses numbers and exponents of half the size, so each costs about one eighth as much, and the whole decryption runs about four times faster. Almost every library does this. There is a catch, found at Bellcore in 1996 by Dan Boneh, Richard DeMillo and Richard Lipton, with a sharper version by Arjen Lenstra. If a fault spoils just one half of a signature, for example from a voltage glitch, heat or a laser aimed at a smart card, the faulty signature is still right modulo p but wrong modulo q. Then a single gcd with n gives away p. One bad signature breaks the key. The defence is simple: check every signature with the public key before releasing it.
CRT exponentsExam favourite
dp = d mod (p − 1), dq = d mod (q − 1), qinv = q−1 mod p
Toy key: dp = 53, dq = 49, qinv = 38.

The private key file in PKCS #1 stores p, q, dp, dq and qinv for exactly this.

Lesson 6 of 12

Attacks on textbook RSA

Plain RSA, exactly as in the textbook, is called textbook RSA, and it is not safe to use as it stands. It is deterministic, so the same message always gives the same ciphertext, and an attacker can test guesses. If the message is yes or no, they encrypt both with your public key and compare. It is malleable: multiplying a ciphertext by 2 to the e makes a valid encryption of twice the message, without knowing it. With a small exponent such as 3 and a short message, m cubed may be smaller than n, so no reduction ever happens and an ordinary cube root reveals the message. In 1985 Johan Håstad showed that if the same message goes to three people who all use e equal to 3, the Chinese remainder theorem plus a cube root recovers it, even when m cubed is bigger than each n. Bad key generation is just as dangerous. If two keys share a prime, a gcd breaks both. If p and q are too close together, Fermat’s old method from 1643 factors n almost instantly. In 2022 Hanno Böck found printers from Canon and Fujifilm whose key library made exactly this mistake. All of these demos run on toy keys made inside the program.
Practise safely and legally
Every attack here runs only on keys the program makes itself. Testing keys or servers you do not own is illegal in most countries. Tools such as testssl.sh check your own servers for weak RSA settings.

Real world scenario: ROCA and the Estonian ID cards

In 2017 researchers from Masaryk University in Czechia found that an RSA library in Infineon chips made primes with a special hidden structure, to save time. Their attack, called ROCA (CVE-2017-15361), could factor a 2048 bit key from these chips for at most about 40,000 US dollars of cloud computing time. About 750,000 Estonian national ID cards, many security keys and TPM chips were affected. Estonia suspended the certificates and issued updates. The lesson: primes must be random, and key generation code needs careful review, not just the RSA formulas.

Lesson 7 of 12

Wiener’s attack on a small private exponent

Decryption speed depends on the size of d, so an engineer might be tempted to choose a small d first and compute e from it. In 1990 Michael Wiener showed that this is fatal. Because e times d is one more than k times phi, and phi is very close to n, the fraction e over n is extremely close to k over d. A tool from the 1700s, continued fractions, finds the best approximations to any fraction, and one of them is exactly k over d when d is smaller than about a quarter root of n, divided by 3. Each guess is easy to test: it gives a guess for phi, which gives a guess for p plus q, and then p and q are the roots of a quadratic. In 1999 Dan Boneh and Glenn Durfee used lattice methods to push the limit up to n to the power 0.292. Standards now insist that d is larger than the square root of n, which a normal key made from e equal to 65537 always is. The animation runs the attack on a small key step by step.
What it is
Wiener’s attack recovers d from (n, e) alone when d < n1/4 / 3 and q < p < 2q.
Why we need it
It shows that speeding up RSA by choosing a small d breaks the key completely, and it introduces lattice style thinking used in many later attacks.
How it works
Expand e / n as a continued fraction. For each convergent k / d, compute φ = (e d − 1) / k, then s = n − φ + 1 = p + q, and check whether x2 − s x + n = 0 has whole number roots.

Lesson 8 of 12

Padding: OAEP, PKCS #1 v1.5, Bleichenbacher and PSS

Every attack on textbook RSA in the earlier lesson is stopped by padding: before encrypting, the message is mixed with fresh random bytes in a structured way. The first standard, PKCS number 1 version 1.5 from 1993, simply put a fixed header, some random non zero bytes, a zero byte and then the message. In 1998 Daniel Bleichenbacher found that if a server tells you whether a ciphertext decrypts to correct padding, even through a different error message or a slightly different response time, you can decrypt any ciphertext with about a million carefully chosen queries. It became known as the million message attack. Servers added patches, but the attack kept coming back. In 2017 the ROBOT study found that servers at major sites, including Facebook and PayPal, were still vulnerable, nineteen years later. The better padding, OAEP, was designed by Bellare and Rogaway in 1994 and uses two rounds of hashing to scramble the message with randomness, so any change is detected on decryption. TLS 1.3, from 2018, removed RSA encryption of keys completely and uses RSA only for signatures, with PSS padding.
What it is
OAEP (Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding) mixes the message with a random seed using a hash based mask, so RSA encryption becomes randomized and non malleable. PSS does the same job for signatures.
Why we need it
Padding turns RSA from a broken textbook formula into a secure scheme with a security proof (IND-CCA2 for OAEP in the random oracle model).
How it works
OAEP: DB = lHash ‖ zeros ‖ 0x01 ‖ M. Pick a random seed. maskedDB = DB ⊕ MGF(seed), maskedSeed = seed ⊕ MGF(maskedDB). Encrypt 0x00 ‖ maskedSeed ‖ maskedDB with RSA.

Lesson 9 of 12

Key sizes, speed and the quantum deadline

How long should an RSA key be? The answer comes from the best factoring method, the number field sieve, together with a safety margin. NIST says a 2048 bit key gives about 112 bits of security, and 3072 bits gives about 128 bits, the same as AES-128. Going higher costs a lot: doubling the key length makes private key operations roughly eight times slower, which is one reason elliptic curves, with 256 bit keys for 128 bit security, took over for new designs. The bigger threat is quantum computing. Shor’s algorithm would factor any RSA modulus in polynomial time on a large fault tolerant quantum computer. No such machine exists yet, but data recorded today could be decrypted later, which is called harvest now, decrypt later. So in November 2024 NIST proposed, in its draft report IR 8547, that RSA keys giving 112 bits of security be deprecated after 2030, and that all RSA, along with the other quantum vulnerable schemes, be disallowed after 2035. The replacement standards, ML-KEM and ML-DSA, are covered in the post quantum chapter of this section.

Lesson 10 of 12

RSA compared with other public key systems

RSA is not the only public key system, and for new designs it is often not the first choice. Diffie-Hellman key exchange, the subject of the next chapter, agrees a fresh key for each session, which gives forward secrecy: stealing the server’s long term key later does not unlock old sessions. RSA key transport, where the client encrypts a key to the server’s RSA key, does not have this property, which is one reason TLS 1.3 dropped it. Elliptic curve schemes give the same security with much smaller keys and faster signing. RSA still wins in one place: verifying a signature is very fast, so it remains popular for certificates and firmware. And all three, RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves, fall to a large quantum computer, while the new lattice based standards do not, as far as anyone knows.

Lesson 11 of 12

From beginner to expert

RSA rewards study at every level. A beginner learns that RSA has a public key for locking and a private key for unlocking, and that its safety rests on how hard it is to split a big number into its two primes. A college student can make a toy key by hand, encrypt and decrypt, prove correctness with Fermat and the Chinese remainder theorem, explain signatures and the CRT speed up, and run the textbook attacks. A masters student studies security definitions such as IND-CCA2 and the proofs for OAEP and PSS in the random oracle model, the Bleichenbacher attack in detail, and side channels on real implementations. At the research level, people use Coppersmith’s method and lattice reduction to attack RSA with partial information, such as knowing a quarter of the bits of p, and study whether breaking RSA is really as hard as factoring. Dan Boneh and Ramarathnam Venkatesan showed in 1998 that for small e this might not be so. Others estimate the quantum resources needed to factor RSA-2048.
One idea, four depths

RSA has a public key (n, e) that anyone can use to lock a message or check a signature, and a private key d that only the owner has. Breaking it seems to need factoring n into its two primes, which is very hard for big numbers.

Make a key from p and q, compute φ and d with extended Euclid, encrypt and decrypt, prove correctness with Fermat plus CRT, sign with hash then d, speed up with CRT, and demonstrate malleability, small e, Håstad, shared primes, Fermat factoring and Wiener.

IND-CPA and IND-CCA2, why textbook RSA fails both, OAEP and PSS proofs in the random oracle model, Bleichenbacher and Manger padding oracles, fault and timing attacks with blinding as the defence, and the RSA assumption compared with factoring.

Coppersmith’s small root method and LLL: stereotyped messages, partial key exposure, Boneh and Durfee’s d < n0.292, factoring with known high bits of p, the open question of whether breaking RSA is equivalent to factoring, and quantum resource estimates for RSA-2048.

Lesson 12 of 12

Chapter summary

  1. Keys: n = p q, φ(n) = (p − 1)(q − 1), e with gcd(e, φ) = 1 (usually 65537), d = e−1 mod φ(n) or mod λ(n).
  2. Encrypt c = me mod n. Decrypt m = cd mod n. Toy example: 65 to 2790 and back with n = 3233, d = 2753.
  3. Proof: Fermat modulo p and modulo q, joined by CRT, covers every m, even those sharing a factor with n.
  4. Signatures: s = pad(H(M))d, verify se. Hash and pad, or forgery is trivial. Use PSS.
  5. RSA-CRT is about 4 times faster. A single faulty half signature leaks p = gcd(s′e − m, n). Verify before release.
  6. Textbook RSA is deterministic and malleable. Small e cube roots, Håstad broadcast, shared primes, close primes (Fermat) and small d (Wiener) all break careless keys.
  7. Padding: OAEP for encryption, PSS for signatures. PKCS #1 v1.5 encryption falls to Bleichenbacher padding oracles (ROBOT, 2017).
  8. Sizes: 2048 bits gives about 112 bit security, 3072 about 128. NIST IR 8547 draft: deprecate 112 bit RSA after 2030, disallow all RSA after 2035 because of quantum computers.
