Lesson 1 of 10

Why phones need their own security

Your phone is probably the most personal computer you own. It goes everywhere with you, it holds your photos, chats, contacts and location history, it has a camera, a microphone and GPS, and in India it is also your bank, your UPI wallet and the place your OTPs arrive. That makes it a prize for attackers. But phones were also designed with more security from the start than desktop computers. Both Android, from Google, and iOS, from Apple, run every app in its own sandbox, a locked room where it can see only its own files. Apps must ask permission to reach anything outside, such as contacts or the camera. Every app must be signed by its developer, and both systems encrypt the storage and protect keys in special hardware. Most mobile attacks therefore do not break these walls. They trick the user into opening a door: installing a fake app from a link, granting permissions that make no sense, typing a password into a fake page, or never updating the phone. A smaller group of very advanced attacks, such as the Pegasus spyware, do break through the walls, and we will see how even those are defended. This chapter goes layer by layer, from the sandbox to the network, and ends with the OWASP list of the ten most common mistakes in mobile apps.

Source: Android security documentation, Apple Platform Security Guide, OWASP Mobile Top 10 (2024), OWASP MASVS and MASTG, Uellenbeck et al. (2013), Citizen Lab (2021), Zimperium (2015), public reports on Joker, Anatsa and predatory loan apps, RBI advisories.

Lesson 2 of 10

The sandbox and permissions: least privilege in your pocket

On Android every app gets its own Linux user ID, so the kernel itself keeps apps apart, and on iOS every app lives in its own container directory with strict rules. Inside the sandbox an app can do what it likes with its own files. To reach anything outside, the camera, microphone, contacts, messages, location or photos, it must hold a permission. Before Android 6.0 in 2015, apps listed all their permissions at install time, and tapping Install granted every one. Nobody read the list. Now both systems ask at the moment the app first needs something, and you can say no. Newer versions go further: Android 11 added one time permissions that expire when you close the app, Android 12 and iOS 14 let you share an approximate location instead of an exact one, and you can share a few chosen photos instead of the whole gallery. Apple’s App Tracking Transparency, from iOS 14.5 in 2021, makes apps ask before tracking you across other companies’ apps. The rule for users is simple. Ask whether the permission makes sense for what the app does. A torch needs the camera flash and nothing else. In India, fake instant loan apps asked for contacts and photos, then used them to shame and threaten borrowers’ families, which led the RBI to warn the public and Google to tighten its rules for loan apps and remove thousands of them.

App signing, app stores and fake apps

Every Android and iOS app is signed by its developer. The developer keeps a private key and signs a hash covering every byte of the app. The phone remembers which key signed the installed version, and it accepts an update only if it is signed by the same key. That is what stops an attacker from replacing your real banking app with a repackaged copy that has hidden malware, which the animation shows with real SHA-256 hashes. On iPhones, apps normally come only from Apple’s App Store, where Apple reviews them and signs them, although the European Union now requires Apple to allow other stores there. On Android you can install apps from outside the Play Store, called sideloading, but only after allowing it for a particular app. Signing does not stop a brand new fake app signed with the attacker’s own key. Attackers send links saying "download the new bank app", "install this to receive your parcel" or "your electricity bill app", and some even get malware into the official store. The Joker family hid in ordinary looking apps on Google Play for years and secretly signed users up for paid subscriptions. Banking trojans such as Anatsa were delivered by harmless looking PDF reader apps that later downloaded the real malware. Google Play Protect scans apps on and off the store and can disable known malware. The user’s best defences are to install from the official store, check the developer name and reviews, and never allow installs from unknown sources because a message asked you to.

Locks, encryption and the hardware that guards keys

What protects your data when the phone itself is stolen? First, the storage is encrypted. Modern Android uses file based encryption and iPhones use a system Apple calls Data Protection, and in both the keys are tied to your lock screen passcode and to secret values inside a special chip. On iPhones that chip is the Secure Enclave. On Android it is a trusted execution environment, and on many newer phones a separate security chip called StrongBox, which holds keys that never leave the chip. Because the keys live in hardware, a thief cannot copy the encrypted storage and guess your passcode on a fast computer. Every guess has to go through the chip, which slows guesses down and can wipe the phone after ten wrong tries if you enable that setting. That is why even a six digit PIN is reasonably strong on a phone, while the same PIN would be weak on a website without limits. The program counts the options: a 4 digit PIN has 10,000 choices, a 6 digit PIN has a million, and the Android pattern grid has 389,112 possible patterns. But people do not choose randomly. Many patterns start in the top left corner and use few dots, and the most common PINs are things like 123456. Biometrics such as fingerprint and face unlock are convenient and are matched inside the secure hardware too, but the passcode remains the master key. Finally, turn on Find My Device or Find My iPhone so you can lock or wipe a lost phone remotely.

Lesson 5 of 10

Phones on hostile networks: TLS and certificate pinning

Phones join many networks we do not control: airport Wi-Fi, café hotspots, hotel networks. HTTPS with TLS, from the cryptography section, already protects most traffic, because the app checks that the server’s certificate is signed by a trusted certificate authority. The weak spot is the list of trusted authorities. If an attacker can get their own certificate authority added to your phone, for example by convincing you to install a "Wi-Fi profile" or a "security certificate" on a captive portal page, then their fake certificates look valid. Since Android 7 in 2016, apps ignore user installed authorities by default unless the developer opts in, which removed much of this risk. Banking and payment apps often go one step further with certificate pinning. The app contains the hash of the bank’s own public key, and it refuses to connect to any server whose key does not match, whoever signed the certificate. The animation shows all three cases. Pinning has a cost. If the bank changes its key without updating the app, the app stops working, so good apps pin a backup key as well and plan their rotations carefully. For users the advice is simple: never install profiles or certificates that a Wi-Fi page asks for, keep apps updated, and use mobile data for banking if a network seems odd.

Lesson 6 of 10

Spyware, zero click attacks and SIM swaps

A few attacks go straight through the walls. In 2015 researchers at Zimperium found Stagefright, a set of bugs in Android’s media library that could be triggered by a video sent in an MMS, putting an estimated 950 million phones at risk. It pushed Google to start monthly Android security updates. The most famous spyware is Pegasus, sold by the Israeli company NSO Group to governments. In 2021 the Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto found an exploit it named FORCEDENTRY, which infected iPhones through a booby trapped file sent over iMessage. The victim did not need to tap anything, which is why it is called zero click. Once inside, Pegasus could read messages, switch on the microphone and track location. Reports that year, called the Pegasus Project, listed possible targets in many countries, including India. Apple patched the bug and in 2022 added Lockdown Mode, which switches off many complex features for people at high risk, such as journalists and activists. A very different attack needs no software at all: the SIM swap. The attacker convinces or bribes a mobile shop to move your number to a new SIM, then receives your OTPs. Defences include a SIM lock PIN, carrier account PINs, and moving important accounts from SMS codes to an authenticator app or passkeys. For everyone, the most important habit is to install updates promptly and restart the phone now and then, which removes some kinds of spyware that cannot survive a reboot.

Common mistake
If you think a phone has spyware, do not try to investigate it yourself first. Back it up, update it, and contact a professional or a digital security helpline. Organisations such as Amnesty Tech and Access Now help people at risk.
Lesson 7 of 10

Building safer apps: the OWASP Mobile Top 10

So far we have looked at the phone. Now let us look at the app, from the developer’s side. The OWASP foundation, which also publishes the web Top 10, keeps a Mobile Top 10 list of the most common risks in mobile apps, last updated in 2024, and a full checklist called MASVS with a testing guide called MASTG. The commonest real problems are surprisingly basic. Apps store tokens, passwords or card numbers in plain files, write secrets to logs, or ship secret keys for cloud services inside the app, where anyone can unpack the app and read them. The program scans a pretend app for exactly these mistakes. The fixes are well known: keep tokens in the Android Keystore or the iOS Keychain, keep secret keys on your server and give the app short lived tokens instead, use HTTPS everywhere, check every input that comes from outside the app, including links that open the app, and treat everything inside the app as visible to an attacker, because it is. Obfuscation and root or jailbreak detection can slow attackers down but must never be the main protection. Finally, the supply chain matters. Most apps include many third party libraries and advertising kits, and some of those have collected data the app developer never intended to share.

Android and iOS security compared

People often ask which phone is more secure. Both are very strong when kept up to date. They make different trade offs between openness and control.

From beginner to expert

A beginner keeps the phone updated, uses a strong lock and installs apps only from the official store. A student understands sandboxes, permissions, signing and encryption. A professional builds and tests apps against MASVS and manages company phones. Experts research mobile exploits, spyware detection and platform defences.
One idea, four depths

Lock your phone, update it, install apps only from the official store, deny permissions that make no sense, and never install profiles or apps that a message asks you to.

Sandbox and per app IDs, runtime permissions, signing and update rules, file based encryption and hardware keys, PIN and pattern maths, TLS and pinning, OWASP Mobile Top 10.

Test apps with MASTG, manage company phones with mobile device management, design pinning with backup keys, keep secrets on the server, and respond to lost devices.

Research exploit chains and sandbox escapes, forensic detection of spyware (as Citizen Lab and Amnesty Tech do), and new platform defences such as memory tagging.
