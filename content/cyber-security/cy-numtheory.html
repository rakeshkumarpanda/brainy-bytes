Lesson 1 of 11

Why public key cryptography needs number theory

Symmetric ciphers have one big problem: both sides must already share a secret key. How do you share a key with a website you have never visited, over a network that anyone can watch? For thousands of years there was no answer. Then in 1976 Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published New Directions in Cryptography, and in 1977 Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman at MIT published RSA. We now know that James Ellis, Clifford Cocks and Malcolm Williamson at the British agency GCHQ had found the same ideas a few years earlier, in secret. All of these schemes rest on number theory, the study of whole numbers, which the great mathematician G. H. Hardy proudly called useless in 1940, because no one had found a military use for it. The key idea is a one way function with a trapdoor: something easy to compute, very hard to undo, unless you know a secret. Multiplying two large primes is easy. Splitting the product back into its primes seems to be extremely hard. Raising a number to a power modulo n is easy. Undoing it is hard, unless you know the trapdoor. This chapter gives you every tool you need to understand RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves completely.

Source: Hoffstein, Pipher and Silverman, An Introduction to Mathematical Cryptography, Shoup, A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra, Menezes et al., Handbook of Applied Cryptography, Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena (2004).

What it is
Public key cryptography uses a pair of keys: a public key anyone may know and a private key kept secret. Its security rests on hard problems from number theory, such as factoring large numbers and computing discrete logarithms.
Why we need it
It solves key exchange over open networks and makes digital signatures possible. Every HTTPS connection, software update and online payment depends on it.
How it works
We need fast operations (modular multiplication, powers, inverses) for the honest users and a problem that seems impossibly slow for everyone else (factoring n = pq, or finding x from gx mod p).

Lesson 2 of 11

Modular arithmetic

You already do modular arithmetic every day with a clock. Five hours after nine o’clock is two o’clock, not fourteen, because the clock wraps round at twelve. Mathematicians write this as 14 is congruent to 2 modulo 12. Carl Friedrich Gauss introduced this notation in 1801, in his book Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, which he wrote at the age of 21. Modular arithmetic has a wonderful property: you can take the remainder at any moment during a calculation and still get the right final remainder. That means even a computation with numbers of millions of digits can be done by keeping only small remainders along the way. Addition, subtraction and multiplication all work. Division is the tricky one, and it only works when the number you divide by shares no factor with the modulus. Powers have patterns too: the powers of 3 modulo 7 cycle through every non zero remainder before returning to 1, which makes 3 a generator. Generators are the heart of Diffie-Hellman key exchange.
What it is
a mod n is the remainder of a divided by n, from 0 to n − 1. Two numbers are congruent, a ≡ b (mod n), when n divides a − b.
Why we need it
Public key schemes work on huge numbers, but only remainders modulo n matter, so all values stay at a fixed size.
How it works
Reduce after every step: (a + b) mod n = ((a mod n) + (b mod n)) mod n, and the same for multiplication. In Python, % always returns a value from 0 to n − 1, even for negative numbers.

Euclid’s algorithm and the extended version

Euclid wrote down his algorithm for the greatest common divisor around 300 BC, in the Elements, which makes it one of the oldest algorithms still in daily use. The idea is short: the gcd of two numbers does not change if you replace the larger by its remainder after dividing by the smaller. Repeat until the remainder is zero, and the last non zero remainder is the gcd. It is astonishingly fast. In 1844, Gabriel Lamé proved that the number of steps is at most five times the number of digits of the smaller number, and the worst case happens for consecutive Fibonacci numbers. That was one of the first results in the analysis of algorithms. The extended version, which keeps track of how each remainder is built from the two starting numbers, gives something even more useful: two whole numbers s and t with s times a plus t times b equal to the gcd. This is Bézout’s identity. When the gcd is 1, it hands us the inverse of b modulo a, which is exactly how RSA computes its private key.
What it is
Euclid’s algorithm computes gcd(a, b) by repeated division with remainder. The extended algorithm also finds s and t with s·a + t·b = gcd(a, b).
Why we need it
gcd tells whether two numbers share a factor, and the extended version gives modular inverses, needed to make RSA private keys and in many other places.
How it works
Keep rows (r, s, t). Start with (a, 1, 0) and (b, 0, 1). Each new row is the row two above minus q times the row above, with q = floor(rold / r). Stop at r = 0. The row before holds the gcd and its s and t.

Lesson 4 of 11

Modular inverses and when they exist

In ordinary arithmetic, dividing by 3 is the same as multiplying by one third. In modular arithmetic there are no fractions, but there can still be a number that acts like one third. Modulo 11, the number 4 does the job, because 3 times 4 is 12, which is 1 modulo 11. So 4 is the inverse of 3 modulo 11. Not every number has an inverse, though. Modulo 9, the number 6 has none, because 6 times anything is a multiple of 3, and no multiple of 3 can leave a remainder of 1 when divided by 9. The rule is clean: a has an inverse modulo n exactly when a and n share no common factor, which means gcd of a and n is 1. When n is a prime, every number from 1 to n minus 1 has an inverse, which is why so much cryptography works modulo primes. RSA relies on this in two places: the public exponent must share no factor with Euler’s phi of n, and the private exponent is its inverse.
What it is
The inverse of a modulo n is the number x with a·x ≡ 1 (mod n). It exists exactly when gcd(a, n) = 1.
Why we need it
Inverses let us "divide" in modular arithmetic. RSA’s private exponent d is the inverse of e modulo φ(n), and the Chinese remainder theorem needs inverses too.
How it works
Run the extended Euclidean algorithm on a and n. If the gcd is 1, then s·a + t·n = 1, so s·a ≡ 1 (mod n), and s mod n is the inverse. In Python 3.8 and later, pow(a, -1, n) does this.
InverseExam favourite
a · a−1 ≡ 1 (mod n), exists ⇔ gcd(a, n) = 1
3−1 mod 11 = 4. 17−1 mod 3120 = 2753. 6 has no inverse mod 9.
Units
the numbers with inverses mod n form the group Zn*, of size φ(n)
For a prime p, Zp* = {{1, ..., p − 1}}.

Lesson 5 of 11

Fast powers: square and multiply

RSA raises a message to a power such as 65537, modulo a number with more than six hundred digits, and decryption uses an exponent with just as many digits. Multiplying the message by itself 65,536 times would already be slow, and multiplying it by itself a number of times with six hundred digits would take longer than the age of the universe. The trick, known for centuries and described in Indian mathematics as early as the Chandah Sutra of Pingala, is to use the binary form of the exponent. Squaring doubles the exponent, and multiplying by the base adds one. So reading the exponent’s bits from left to right, you square at every bit and also multiply when the bit is 1. The number of steps is about twice the number of bits, so a 2048 bit exponent needs only about 3,000 multiplications. And because we reduce modulo n after every step, the numbers never grow beyond twice the size of n. One warning: if the time of each step depends on whether a bit is 0 or 1, a careful attacker who measures the time can read the secret exponent. Paul Kocher showed this timing attack in 1996, so real libraries use constant time methods.
What it is
Square and multiply computes be mod n with about 2 log2 e multiplications by reading e in binary.
Why we need it
Without it, RSA, Diffie-Hellman and every signature would be far too slow to use.
How it works
Start with r = 1. For each bit of e from the left: r = r2 mod n, and if the bit is 1, r = r · b mod n. Python’s three argument pow(b, e, n) does this in fast C code.

e = 13 = 11012: after the first bit, 3 squarings + 2 multiplications. e = 65537 = 216 + 1: 16 squarings + 1 multiplication = 17.
Why it works
b2k = (bk)2 and b2k+1 = (bk)2 · b
Each bit doubles the exponent read so far, and a 1 bit adds one.
Common mistake
A naive implementation leaks the key: a 1 bit costs an extra multiplication, which shows up in timing and power traces. Libraries use constant time techniques such as the Montgomery ladder, which does the same work for every bit.

Fermat’s little theorem, Euler’s phi and Euler’s theorem

In 1640, Pierre de Fermat wrote in a letter that if p is a prime and a is not a multiple of p, then a raised to the power p minus 1 leaves a remainder of 1 when divided by p. As often with Fermat, he did not include a proof. Leonhard Euler proved it in 1736, and in 1763 he went further. He defined a function, now written with the Greek letter phi, that counts how many numbers from 1 to n share no factor with n, and he proved that a raised to the power phi of n is 1 modulo n, whenever a shares no factor with n. For a prime p, phi of p is p minus 1, which gives back Fermat’s theorem. For a product of two different primes, phi of p times q is p minus 1 times q minus 1. This is the single most important fact behind RSA. It means exponents can be reduced modulo phi of n, so raising to e and then to d, where e times d is 1 more than a multiple of phi of n, brings every message back to itself. Anyone who knows p and q can compute phi of n easily. Anyone who only knows n seems to need to factor it first.
What it is
Fermat’s little theorem: ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p) for a prime p and p ∤ a. Euler’s phi φ(n) counts 1 ≤ k ≤ n with gcd(k, n) = 1. Euler’s theorem: aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n) when gcd(a, n) = 1.
Why we need it
Euler’s theorem is why RSA decryption works, why exponents can be reduced, and why knowing φ(n) (that is, knowing p and q) is the trapdoor.
How it works
For n = p q with primes p ≠ q, φ(n) = (p − 1)(q − 1). Then akφ(n)+1 ≡ a (mod n), so choosing e d ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) makes (ae)d ≡ a.

Going deeper: Proof of Euler’s theorem in four lines

Let r1, ..., rk be the k = φ(n) numbers from 1 to n that are coprime to n. Multiply each by a (with gcd(a, n) = 1). The products a r1, ..., a rk are again coprime to n and all different mod n (if a ri ≡ a rj, multiply by a−1). So they are the same numbers in a different order. Multiply all of them: ak r1...rk ≡ r1...rk (mod n). The product of the ri is coprime to n, so cancel it: ak ≡ 1 (mod n). For n = p prime, k = p − 1, which is Fermat’s little theorem.

The Chinese remainder theorem

Around the fourth century, the Chinese mathematician Sun Zi posed a puzzle in his Mathematical Manual: there is an unknown number of things. Counted by threes, two are left over. By fives, three are left over. By sevens, two are left over. How many things are there? The answer is 23, and the general method behind it is the Chinese remainder theorem. It says that if the divisors share no common factors, then the remainders fix the number uniquely, up to the product of the divisors. Think of it as a coordinate system: knowing a number modulo 3, 5 and 7 is the same as knowing it modulo 105. In cryptography this lets us split one big calculation into several smaller ones and join the results. RSA decryption, done modulo p and modulo q separately and then recombined, runs about four times faster than working modulo n directly. The same idea also creates danger: in 1997 Boneh, DeMillo and Lipton showed that if a hardware fault spoils just one of the two halves, a single faulty signature reveals the factors of n.
What it is
The Chinese remainder theorem (CRT): if m1, ..., mk are pairwise coprime and M is their product, then for any remainders ai there is exactly one x from 0 to M − 1 with x ≡ ai (mod mi) for every i.
Why we need it
It lets computations mod n = pq be done mod p and mod q separately, which speeds up RSA decryption and signing by about 4 times, and it appears in many attacks.
How it works
Compute Mi = M / mi and yi = Mi−1 mod mi. Then x = Σ ai Mi yi mod M. Each term is ai modulo its own mi and 0 modulo the others.

Primes: how many there are and how to find them

RSA needs two random primes with about 300 digits each. Are there enough of them, and how do we find one? Euclid proved around 300 BC that there are infinitely many primes. The prime number theorem, proved in 1896 by Jacques Hadamard and Charles de la Vallée Poussin, tells us how common they are: near a number x, roughly one number in every natural log of x is prime. For 1024 bit numbers, that is about one in 710, or one in 355 if we only look at odd numbers. So we pick random odd numbers and test each one, and after a few hundred tries we have a prime. Testing must be fast, and dividing by every smaller number is hopeless. Fermat’s little theorem gives a quick test: if a to the n minus 1 is not 1 mod n, then n is certainly not prime. But some composite numbers, called Carmichael numbers, pass this test for every a. The smallest is 561. The Miller-Rabin test, from 1976 and 1980, fixes this and is wrong with probability at most one in four per round, so 64 rounds make an error less likely than a cosmic ray flipping a bit in your computer. In 2002, Manindra Agrawal and his students Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena at IIT Kanpur proved that primality can be decided with certainty in polynomial time, a famous result, although Miller-Rabin is still used in practice because it is faster.
What it is
A prime has exactly two divisors, 1 and itself. The prime number theorem says about x / ln x primes are below x. Miller-Rabin is a fast probabilistic primality test.
Why we need it
Key generation needs random large primes. Bad primes or weak randomness have broken real keys: in 2012 researchers found that tens of thousands of devices on the internet shared prime factors because of poor randomness at boot.
How it works
Pick a random odd number with the top bit set, test it with Miller-Rabin (after quick trial division by small primes), and repeat until one passes.

The hard problems: factoring and discrete logarithms

Public key cryptography is a bet that certain problems are hard. Multiplying two 1024 bit primes takes a computer a microsecond. Splitting their product back into the two primes has resisted mathematicians for centuries. The best known method, the general number field sieve, is much faster than trying divisors, but still grows too quickly for large numbers. In February 2020 a team factored RSA-250, a number of 829 bits, using about 2,700 years of computer core time. A 2048 bit RSA number is estimated to be harder by a factor of billions. The second hard problem is the discrete logarithm. Raising a generator g to a secret power x modulo a prime p is easy with square and multiply. Given the result, finding x seems just as hard as factoring. Diffie-Hellman and DSA rely on it, and elliptic curves rely on a version of it that is even harder. Neither problem is proven hard. And Peter Shor showed in 1994 that a large enough quantum computer would solve both quickly, which is why the world is now moving to post quantum cryptography, covered later in this section.
What it is
Factoring: given n = pq, find p and q. Discrete logarithm: given g, p and h = gx mod p, find x. Both seem hard for large numbers on classical computers.
Why we need it
RSA’s security rests on factoring, and Diffie-Hellman, DSA and elliptic curve schemes on discrete logarithms. Key sizes are chosen from the best known attacks.
How it works
Trial division needs about √n steps. The general number field sieve takes about exp(1.923 (ln n)1/3 (ln ln n)2/3) steps, sub exponential but still enormous for 2048 bit n.

Lesson 10 of 11

From beginner to expert

Number theory for cryptography is a ladder you can climb a long way. A beginner learns clock arithmetic and that a product of two primes is easy to make but hard to split. A college student can run the extended Euclidean algorithm by hand, compute phi, reduce big powers with Euler’s theorem, solve Chinese remainder problems and explain Miller-Rabin. A masters student studies groups, rings and finite fields properly, the structure of the multiplicative group modulo a prime, and the sub exponential algorithms: the quadratic sieve, the number field sieve, index calculus for discrete logarithms and Pollard’s rho and p minus 1 methods. At the research level, mathematicians study the exact complexity of these problems, lattice methods such as Coppersmith’s technique that find small roots and break RSA with partial information, and quantum algorithms such as Shor’s, together with careful estimates of how many qubits a real attack would need. In 2025, Craig Gidney of Google estimated that RSA-2048 could be factored with under a million noisy qubits in less than a week, far beyond today’s machines but closer than earlier estimates.
One idea, four depths

Modular arithmetic is clock arithmetic. Multiplying two primes is easy, splitting the product is hard. That one sided difficulty is what public key cryptography is built on.

Compute gcd and inverses with the extended Euclidean algorithm, powers with square and multiply, reduce exponents with Euler’s theorem, solve CRT systems, and test primes with Miller-Rabin.

Group theory of Zn*, Lagrange’s theorem, primitive roots, quadratic residues and the Legendre symbol, Pollard’s rho and p − 1, the quadratic sieve and number field sieve, index calculus, and Pohlig-Hellman for discrete logs in groups of smooth order.

Coppersmith’s method for small roots and partial key exposure, lattice reduction (LLL), rigorous analysis of factoring algorithms, Shor’s algorithm and resource estimates (Gidney, 2025: RSA-2048 with fewer than a million noisy qubits in under a week), and the search for problems hard even for quantum computers.

Explain modular arithmetic and why factoring makes public keys possible.
Intermediate

Expert
Analyse factoring and discrete log algorithms, lattice attacks and quantum resource estimates.

Chapter summary

  1. Public key cryptography rests on hard problems: factoring n = pq and the discrete logarithm. Honest users need only fast modular operations.
  2. a ≡ b (mod n) when n divides a − b. Reduce after every step. 3 is a generator mod 7.
  3. Euclid: gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b). The extended version gives s·a + t·b = gcd (Bézout).
  4. a has an inverse mod n ⇔ gcd(a, n) = 1. 17−1 mod 3120 = 2753. Python: pow(a, −1, n).
  5. Square and multiply: square every bit, multiply on 1 bits, about 2 log2 e steps. 65537 needs 17. Use constant time code.
  6. Fermat: ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Euler: aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n), φ(pq) = (p − 1)(q − 1). Exponents reduce mod φ(n).
  7. CRT: pairwise coprime moduli fix x mod M. x = Σ ai Mi yi. Sun Zi’s answer is 23. RSA-CRT is about 4 times faster.
  8. Primes: π(x) ∼ x / ln x, about 355 odd tries for 1024 bits. Miller-Rabin error ≤ 4−k. 561 fools Fermat’s test.
