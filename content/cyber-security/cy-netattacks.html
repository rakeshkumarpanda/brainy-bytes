Lesson 1 of 8

Why networks are attacked and what defenders protect

The internet was designed in the 1970s and 1980s by researchers who mostly trusted each other. Protocols such as ARP, DNS, TCP and BGP believe what they are told, so an attacker who can send packets can often lie about who they are. In 1988 the Morris worm spread across about a tenth of the internet in a day. In 1994 Kevin Mitnick used TCP sequence number prediction to pose as a trusted computer. In 1996 the internet provider Panix was knocked offline for days by a SYN flood, and in 2016 the Mirai botnet of hacked cameras and routers took down the DNS provider Dyn, and with it sites such as Twitter and Netflix for many users. Network attacks fall into a few families. Eavesdropping and man in the middle attacks break confidentiality and integrity. Denial of service breaks availability. Reconnaissance, such as port scanning, is the first step of most intrusions. Spoofing and poisoning attacks, against ARP, DNS and BGP, redirect traffic to the attacker. This chapter shows how each works on toy data and, more importantly, how defenders detect and stop it. Remember that scanning or attacking systems without permission is a crime in most countries, under laws such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, the UK Computer Misuse Act and India’s IT Act 2000.

Source: RFC 826 (ARP, 1982), RFC 4987 (TCP SYN flooding attacks and common mitigations, 2007), Bernstein, SYN cookies (1996), US-CERT alert TA14-017A (UDP based amplification attacks), RFC 2827 (BCP 38), Kaminsky (Black Hat 2008), RFC 5452, RFC 4033 to 4035 (DNSSEC), RFC 8484 (DNS over HTTPS), CVE-2023-44487, Nmap reference guide.

Practise safely and legally
Every program on this page works on a list or dictionary inside the program. To practise on real packets, use your own virtual lab (for example two virtual machines on a host only network) or a legal training range, and only scan systems you own or have written permission to test.
What it is
A network attack abuses how computers talk to each other: it listens to, changes, blocks or redirects traffic, or maps a network to find a way in.
Why we need it
Most protocols were built without authentication, and every service reachable on a network is a possible way in. Knowing the attacks tells defenders where to put controls.
How it works
Attackers forge packets (spoofing), flood resources (denial of service), probe for open services (scanning) and poison the tables that map names and addresses (ARP, DNS, BGP). Defenders answer with switch features, filtering, rate limits, encryption and monitoring.

Sniffing and ARP spoofing

On a local network, computers find each other by MAC address, the hardware address of the network card. When a laptop wants to send a packet to its gateway at 10.0.0.1, it broadcasts an ARP request asking who has that address, and the gateway replies with its MAC. The laptop stores the answer in its ARP cache. The protocol, described in RFC 826 in 1982, has no authentication, and most systems accept a reply they never asked for. So an attacker on the same network can send the laptop a forged reply saying the gateway is at the attacker’s MAC, and send the gateway a forged reply saying the laptop is at the attacker’s MAC. From then on, traffic in both directions passes through the attacker, who forwards it so that nothing seems broken. This is a man in the middle position. The attacker can read anything not encrypted, and can try to change it. On old hubs and open Wi-Fi an attacker could simply listen, which is called sniffing. Switches send each frame only to its destination port, but ARP spoofing defeats that. The defences are at the switch, with DHCP snooping and Dynamic ARP Inspection, on hosts, with tools such as arpwatch, and above all with encryption, because TLS turns a man in the middle into someone holding unreadable bytes.

What it is
ARP spoofing (ARP poisoning): sending forged ARP replies so that a victim maps an IP address, usually the gateway’s, to the attacker’s MAC address.
Why we need it
It turns the attacker into a man in the middle on a LAN, the starting point for stealing sessions, downgrading connections and injecting content.
How it works
Hosts update their cache from any ARP reply. The attacker repeats forged replies every few seconds to both sides and forwards the traffic. Detection watches for an IP that changes MAC or a MAC that claims many IPs.

Lesson 3 of 8

Denial of service: SYN floods and amplification

A denial of service attack tries to make a service unavailable, usually by exhausting some resource: bandwidth, memory, connection slots or CPU. When it comes from many machines at once, often a botnet of hacked devices, it is a distributed denial of service, or DDoS. The SYN flood is a classic. When a client sends SYN, the server reserves a slot in its backlog queue, replies SYN-ACK and waits for the final ACK. An attacker sends SYNs from forged addresses and never answers, so slots fill. If the backlog has 1024 slots and the server waits 30 seconds, 35 forged SYNs a second are enough to keep it full. In 1996 Daniel Bernstein proposed SYN cookies: when the queue is full, the server stores nothing and instead encodes the connection details and a secret keyed hash in the sequence number it sends back. A real client echoes that number plus one, and the server rebuilds the connection from it. Reflection attacks use a different trick. The attacker sends small UDP requests to public servers with the victim’s address as the source, and the servers send large replies to the victim. DNS can amplify about 50 times, NTP’s old monlist command about 557 times, and memcached up to 51000 times. In 2018 GitHub survived a 1.35 terabit per second memcached attack. The root fix is BCP 38: networks should drop outgoing packets whose source address is not their own, which stops spoofing at the edge.

Lesson 4 of 8

Reconnaissance and port scanning

Before attacking, an intruder maps the target: which hosts are alive, which ports are open, and which software and versions are listening. The best known tool is Nmap, released by Gordon Lyon in 1997 and used daily by defenders to check their own networks. A port is open if a service listens on it, closed if the host answers with a reset, and filtered if a firewall drops the probe so nothing comes back. A connect scan uses the ordinary connect call and finishes the handshake, so services usually log it. A SYN scan, also called half open, sends only the SYN and resets as soon as a SYN-ACK arrives, which is faster and leaves fewer application logs, but needs raw packet rights. Further probes guess the service version and the operating system from tiny differences in replies. Defenders run the same scans against themselves to find forgotten services, and they detect hostile scans by noticing one source touching many ports or hosts in a short time. Services such as Shodan and Censys scan the whole IPv4 internet continuously, so anything exposed is found within hours.

What it is
Port scanning sends probes to many ports to learn which are open, closed or filtered, and often which software runs on them.
Why we need it
It is step one of almost every intrusion and of every defensive audit. You cannot protect a service you did not know was exposed.
How it works
Send SYN: SYN-ACK means open, RST means closed, silence or an ICMP unreachable message means filtered. Version detection sends protocol specific probes and matches the answers against a database.

Poisoning names and routes: DNS and BGP

DNS turns names into addresses, and a resolver caches each answer for its time to live. If an attacker can get a false answer into the cache, every user of that resolver is sent to the attacker’s server. Early resolvers accepted any reply whose 16 bit query ID matched, so an attacker who raced the real server with many forged replies had a fair chance. In 2008 Dan Kaminsky showed how to make the race repeatable: ask the resolver for random names under the target domain, such as a1.bank.example, a2.bank.example and so on, each causing a fresh query and a fresh race, and in the forged replies include extra data that claims the whole domain. The industry shipped a coordinated fix within weeks: randomise the source port as well as the query ID, which adds about 16 bits of guessing. The full fix is DNSSEC, which signs DNS records so a resolver can check them. The root zone was signed in 2010. DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS encrypt the path from the user to the resolver. Routing has a similar weakness. BGP, which joins the networks of the internet, believes the routes it is told. In 2008 Pakistan Telecom accidentally took YouTube offline worldwide by announcing a more specific route, and in 2018 attackers hijacked Amazon DNS address space to steal cryptocurrency. RPKI lets networks sign which addresses they may announce.

Network attacks compared

It helps to compare these attacks by what the attacker needs and what they break. ARP spoofing needs a foothold on the same local network but gives full man in the middle power there. DNS poisoning and BGP hijacks work from far away and redirect many users at once. Floods need a lot of traffic, which is why botnets and amplification exist, and they break availability without stealing anything. Scanning breaks nothing by itself but tells the attacker where to aim. A useful habit for exams is to name the protocol weakness behind each attack, usually missing authentication or a resource that is reserved before the other side proves anything, and then the matching defence.

Lesson 7 of 8

From beginner to expert

Network security spans everyday habits and deep protocol research. A beginner learns that public Wi-Fi can be watched, that HTTPS matters, and that floods can knock sites offline. A college student can explain the TCP handshake and why a SYN flood works, read a packet capture in Wireshark, run Nmap against a lab machine and describe ARP and DNS poisoning with their defences. A masters student models attacks with queueing theory, studies SYN cookie and TCP option trade offs, measures amplification across protocols, designs DDoS mitigation with anycast and scrubbing, and configures DNSSEC and RPKI. Researchers find new amplification vectors and protocol flaws, such as HTTP/2 Rapid Reset in 2023, measure the whole internet with tools like ZMap that can scan all of IPv4 in under an hour, and study off path attacks that infer TCP sequence numbers through side channels.
One idea, four depths

Public networks can be watched, so use HTTPS and a VPN. Floods can knock services offline. Attackers scan the internet constantly looking for open doors.

The TCP handshake and SYN floods, ARP and DNS poisoning, amplification factors, Nmap port states, BCP 38, SYN cookies and Dynamic ARP Inspection.

Queueing models of floods (Little’s law), SYN cookie encoding, DDoS architecture with anycast and scrubbing, DNSSEC chains of trust, RPKI, and reading pcap files with Wireshark and Zeek.

New amplification vectors, internet wide measurement (ZMap, 2013), off path TCP attacks through side channels, BGP security beyond origin validation (BGPsec, ASPA), and formal analysis of protocols.

Chapter summary

  1. Why: ARP, DNS, TCP and BGP trust what they hear. Attacks spoof, flood, scan and poison.
  2. ARP spoofing: forged replies make the attacker a man in the middle. Defend with DHCP snooping plus DAI, arpwatch and TLS.
  3. SYN flood: half open H = r × T fills backlog B. SYN cookies keep no state: 5 bits time, 3 bits MSS, 24 bits keyed hash.
  4. Amplification: k = response / request. DNS about 54, NTP monlist 557, memcached up to 51000. BCP 38 stops spoofed sources.
  5. Scanning: SYN-ACK open, RST closed, silence filtered. SYN scan is half open, connect scan completes. Scan only with permission.
  6. DNS poisoning: p ≈ n/N per race. Kaminsky made races repeatable. Fix: random ports (232), DNSSEC, DoT and DoH.
  7. BGP: hijacks redirect address space (YouTube 2008, Route 53 2018). RPKI signs route origins.
  8. Layers: encrypt, authenticate, keep no state for unproven clients, filter, monitor.
