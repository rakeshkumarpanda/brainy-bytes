Lesson 1 of 10

What incident response is, and why a plan beats panic

An incident is any event that harms, or could harm, the safety of your systems or data. A stolen password, a laptop left in a taxi, ransomware on a server, or a website that suddenly shows strange text are all incidents. Incident response is the planned way a team deals with them. The key word is planned. When something bad happens at three in the morning, people under stress make poor choices, such as switching off every machine and destroying the evidence, or paying a ransom before checking the backups. A written plan, practised beforehand, lets people act calmly. Real cases show the cost of being unready. In May 2017 the WannaCry ransomware spread across about 150 countries in a few days and hit hospitals in the UK National Health Service, which had to cancel thousands of appointments. It was slowed when a researcher, Marcus Hutchins, registered a web address hidden in its code that acted as a kill switch. A month later NotPetya hit the shipping company Maersk, which had to reinstall about 45000 computers. Its recovery was saved by one domain controller in Ghana that happened to be offline because of a power cut. In May 2021 Colonial Pipeline shut down the main fuel pipeline on the US east coast after ransomware hit its business systems, and paid a ransom of about 4.4 million dollars, part of which the FBI later recovered. In November 2022 ransomware hit AIIMS in New Delhi, and its servers were down for about two weeks, so staff went back to paper. Each of these teams learned that the plan, the backups and the people matter more than any single tool.

Source: NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2, Computer Security Incident Handling Guide (2012) and Rev. 3 (2025), NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024), SANS Incident Handler’s Handbook, UK National Audit Office report on WannaCry and the NHS (2017), Maersk public statements (2017 to 2019), US Department of Justice statement on the Colonial Pipeline ransom recovery (June 2021), news reports on the AIIMS Delhi attack (November and December 2022), CISA and FBI StopRansomware guide.

The life cycle: NIST SP 800-61 and SANS PICERL

Two models describe the same work. NIST, the US standards body, published SP 800-61 in 2004 and its second revision in 2012. It has four phases: preparation, then detection and analysis, then containment, eradication and recovery, and finally post incident activity. SANS, a training organisation, splits the same work into six steps with the short name PICERL: preparation, identification, containment, eradication, recovery and lessons learned. In April 2025 NIST published revision 3, which ties incident response to the six functions of its Cybersecurity Framework 2.0: govern, identify, protect, detect, respond and recover. The words differ but the order is the same. Preparation means the plan, the contact list, the tools, the logs and the backups are ready before you need them. Detection and analysis means deciding whether an alert is a real incident and how big it is. Containment means stopping the damage from spreading, for example by isolating a machine or disabling an account. Eradication means removing the cause, such as malware, a back door or the hole the attacker used. Recovery means bringing systems back safely and watching them closely. Lessons learned means a meeting, within about two weeks, where the team asks what happened, what worked, and what to change. In real life the phases overlap and loop. A team that finds a second back door during eradication goes back to analysis. Try the animation with three different incidents.

Lesson 3 of 10

Preparation and triage: being ready, and sorting what matters

Most of incident response happens before the incident. A good preparation list includes a written plan approved by management, a team with named roles and backups for each person, a contact list that works even if email is down, playbooks for the common cases such as phishing, ransomware, lost devices and data leaks, logs that are already being collected, backups that have been restored in a test, and tools ready to use, such as EDR that can isolate a machine with one click. Teams also agree in advance who may take big decisions, like taking the website offline, and who talks to the press, the police and the regulators. Then, when alerts arrive, the team must triage, which means sorting by priority, the same word hospitals use for the emergency room. A simple method rates impact, how much harm, and urgency, how fast it is getting worse, each from low to high, and multiplies them. Ransomware spreading on the main server is high and high, so it is priority 1 and wakes people up at night. Adware on one laptop is low and medium, so it waits for normal hours. NIST also suggests asking three questions: what is the functional impact on the business, what is the information impact, meaning was data seen, changed or taken, and how hard will recovery be.

A tabletop exercise in one hour
  1. Pick a realistic scenario, for example: the finance shared drive is encrypted on a Friday evening.
  2. Gather the people who would really respond: IT, security, a manager, legal, communications.
  3. Read the scenario in parts, adding a new fact every ten minutes.
  4. At each part, ask: who does what now, using which tool, and who must be told?
  5. Write down every gap: a missing phone number, an untested backup, an unclear decision.
  6. Fix the gaps and run a new scenario in a few months.
Lesson 4 of 10

Containment, eradication and recovery

Containment means stopping the harm from growing. Speed matters most here, because modern ransomware and worms move from machine to machine in minutes. The animation shows a simple network where the infection reaches the neighbouring machines every minute. If the team isolates infected machines at minute three, ten out of twenty four are hit. At minute nine, all of them are. A firewall that splits the network into segments puts a ceiling on the damage, even if the team is slow. This is why playbooks often let a tier 1 analyst isolate a machine at once without asking a manager. Containment has short term steps, like isolating a host, disabling an account, or blocking an IP address, and long term steps, like moving services to clean machines while the investigation goes on. One important rule: do not just switch everything off. Memory holds valuable evidence, such as running malware and encryption keys, and it disappears when power is cut. EDR network isolation cuts the machine off from the network but keeps it running. Eradication means removing the cause: delete the malware, close the hole the attacker used, remove accounts and back doors they created, and reset passwords, especially for admin and service accounts. Recovery means bringing systems back, often rebuilding from known good images instead of trusting cleaned machines, restoring data from clean backups, and watching closely for the attacker to return, which happens often when a foothold was missed.

Lesson 5 of 10

Backups, RPO and RTO: recovering from ransomware

Backups are the main reason a company can recover from ransomware without paying. Two numbers describe a recovery plan. The recovery point objective, RPO, is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. If you back up every four hours, you may lose up to four hours of work. The recovery time objective, RTO, is how long you can afford to be down. Restoring four terabytes at four hundred megabytes a second takes almost three hours, before counting the time to check and restart everything. Modern attackers know backups are the defence, so they hunt for them first. If the backup share sits on the same network and can be reached with the same admin account, it gets encrypted too, as the animation shows. That is why the old 3 2 1 rule, three copies, on two kinds of storage, one off site, has grown into 3 2 1 1 0: add one copy that is offline or immutable, meaning it cannot be changed or deleted for a set time, and make sure restore tests show zero errors. A backup that has never been restored in a test is only a hope. Agencies such as CISA, the FBI and CERT-In advise against paying ransoms, because payment funds more attacks, does not guarantee the data comes back, and can break sanctions laws if the gang is on a sanctions list.

Common mistake
Keep backup systems in their own security zone with separate admin accounts and MFA. If one stolen admin password can delete both the servers and the backups, you have one copy, not three.
Lesson 6 of 10

Telling the right people: roles, evidence and legal deadlines

An incident is not only a technical problem. People inside and outside the company need to know the right things at the right time. Inside, there is usually an incident commander who leads and decides, technical leads who do the hands on work, a scribe who writes down every action with its time, and someone who handles communication. Outside, the laws set deadlines. In India, the CERT-In directions of April 2022 say many kinds of incidents must be reported to CERT-In within 6 hours of noticing them. In the European Union, GDPR article 33 says a personal data breach must be reported to the data protection authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, and the NIS2 directive asks important companies for an early warning within 24 hours and a fuller report within 72 hours. In the United States, listed companies must file a Form 8-K with the SEC within four business days after deciding an incident is material. India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023 also requires telling the Data Protection Board and the affected people about a personal data breach. Because of these rules, the scribe’s timeline matters: it shows when the company became aware. Evidence also needs care. Each item, such as a disk image, should have a chain of custody record that says who collected it, when, and who held it since, so it can be trusted later. The next chapter, on forensics, goes deeper into that.

Lessons learned and automation

The last phase is the one teams skip most often, and it is the one that makes the next incident smaller. Within about two weeks, everyone involved meets for a blameless review. Blameless means the goal is to fix the system, not to punish a person, because people hide mistakes when they fear blame, and hidden mistakes come back. The review walks through the timeline and asks what happened, why it happened, what helped, what slowed us down, and what we will change. A simple tool is the five whys: keep asking why until you reach a cause you can fix. The ransomware got in through the VPN. Why? It was not patched. Why? The patch needed a reboot and nobody owned that device. Why? It was missing from the asset list. Now the fix is clear: every device needs an owner in the asset list. Each lesson becomes a task with an owner and a date. The five whys for one incident
  1. Ransomware encrypted the file server. Why did it get in? Through the company VPN.
  2. Why could it use the VPN? The VPN had a known flaw with a patch available for three weeks.
  3. Why was it not patched? The patch needed a reboot and nobody owned the device.
  4. Why did nobody own it? It was missing from the asset list.
  5. Fix: every device must have an owner in the asset list, and critical patches have a deadline that is tracked.
Lesson 8 of 10

Response approaches compared

Companies choose how much of incident response to do themselves. Some build their own team. Others hire a managed detection and response company that watches alerts all day and night. Many sign an incident response retainer with a specialist firm that will send experts within hours when something big happens. Cyber insurance often requires one of these, and it may pay for the specialist firm and the lawyers. Each choice trades cost, speed and knowledge of your own systems.

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that a team needs a plan for bad days and that reporting quickly helps. A student can name the phases, triage incidents, and work out RPO, RTO and how spread grows. A professional writes playbooks, leads response calls, and meets legal deadlines. Experts handle large crises across many countries and design how whole organisations recover.
One idea, four depths

When something goes wrong with computers at school or work, tell an adult or the IT team quickly, and do not try to hide it. Fast reporting keeps the problem small.

The NIST and SANS phases, triage by impact and urgency, containment versus eradication, exponential spread, RPO and RTO, 3 2 1 1 0 backups, legal deadlines, MTTD and Little’s law.

Write and test playbooks, lead an incident call as commander, keep a defensible timeline, work with lawyers and insurers, and automate response steps with SOAR.

Crisis management across countries and sectors, response to state sponsored intrusions, recovery engineering for very large estates, and research on automated containment that stays safe.
