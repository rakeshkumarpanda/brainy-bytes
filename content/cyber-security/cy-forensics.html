Lesson 1 of 9

What digital forensics is: evidence that holds up

Digital forensics means finding, collecting, studying and presenting evidence from computers, phones, networks and cloud accounts, in a way that a court, a regulator or a company board can trust. It grew out of police work. The French criminologist Edmond Locard, in the early twentieth century, taught that every contact leaves a trace. A burglar leaves fingerprints and takes away dust. Computers are the same: an intruder who logs in, runs a program or copies a file leaves records in logs, in memory, in file system time stamps and in network devices. A famous example is the BTK serial killer in Kansas, who had escaped police for about thirty years. In 2005 he sent the police a floppy disk. Hidden details inside a Word document on it named a church and a user called Dennis, and that led quickly to Dennis Rader’s arrest. Forensics is also used every day for ordinary cases: an employee who took customer data before leaving, a fraud in accounts, or a ransomware attack where the company must know what data was taken. The work follows a few strict rules. Do not change the original. Work on verified copies. Record everything you do, with the time and the tool. Be able to repeat your steps and get the same result. And keep an unbroken record of who held the evidence, called the chain of custody.

Source: RFC 3227 Guidelines for Evidence Collection and Archiving (Brezinski and Killalea, 2002), NIST SP 800-86 (2006), ISO/IEC 27037:2012, Volatility Foundation documentation, Plaso documentation, Microsoft FAT and NTFS specifications, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 section 63, Supreme Court of India judgments in Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer (2014) and Arjun Panditrao Khotkar (2020), FBI and press reports on the BTK case (2005).

Practise safely and legally
Only examine devices and data you own or have written permission to examine. The programs here use small made up byte strings. For practice images, use the free test images published by NIST’s Computer Forensic Reference Data Sets.
What it is
Digital forensics is the careful collection and study of digital evidence so that the findings can be trusted and repeated by others.
Why we need it
After an incident, people need facts: how the attacker got in, what they touched, what was taken, and who did it. Courts and regulators accept only evidence that was handled properly.
How it works
Collect in the order of volatility, make exact copies through write blockers, prove them unchanged with hashes, analyse the copies with tested tools, build a timeline, and write a clear report.
The forensic process (NIST SP 800-86)
  1. Collection: identify the sources of evidence and collect them without changing them, most fragile first.
  2. Examination: process the data, for example recover deleted files and pull out logs and time stamps.
  3. Analysis: connect the findings into a story that answers the questions asked.
  4. Reporting: write what was done, what was found and how sure we are, so a non expert can follow it.

Collecting evidence: order of volatility, write blockers and hashes

Some evidence disappears in nanoseconds, some lasts for years. RFC 3227, written in 2002, lists the order to collect it: first CPU registers and cache, then memory with the routing table, the ARP cache, the list of processes and the network connections, then temporary files, then the disk, then remote logs, then the physical setup, and last of all backups and archives. In practice the first two are captured together by dumping the memory of the running machine to an external drive with a tool such as WinPmem on Windows or LiME on Linux. That is why responders today usually do not pull the plug straight away, as the animation shows. Pulling the plug loses running malware, open network connections and the keys that unlock encrypted disks. Next the disk is copied bit for bit into an image file. The copy is made through a write blocker, a small device or software switch that lets the computer read the disk but never write to it, because even plugging a disk into a normal computer can change some bytes. Then the image is hashed with SHA-256, and the hash is written on the evidence form. Anyone can later hash the image again. If the hash is the same, the image is the same, down to every single bit. Older cases used MD5 and SHA-1, which are now broken for collisions, so SHA-256 is the safe choice today, and some labs record two hashes.

Disk forensics: deleted files, slack space and carving

When you delete a file, the computer usually does not erase it. It is faster to just mark the space as free and remove the name from the folder list. On the old FAT file system, the first letter of the file name is replaced with the byte E5 in hexadecimal, and the file’s clusters are marked free. The bytes stay on the disk until a new file happens to be written on top of them. That is why deleted files can often be recovered, as the program below shows. Two other hiding places matter. Slack space is the unused end of the last cluster of a file. If clusters are 4096 bytes and a file is 10000 bytes, it needs three clusters, and the last 2288 bytes may still hold pieces of an older file. Unallocated space is all the clusters marked free, full of old data. If the file system itself is damaged or formatted, the names and folders are gone, but files can still be found by carving: scanning raw bytes for the magic numbers that start and end each file type, such as FF D8 FF for a JPEG photo or %PDF for a PDF. Real carvers such as PhotoRec, Foremost and Scalpel do this across whole disks. Two modern changes make recovery harder. Solid state drives use a command called TRIM that tells the drive which blocks are free, and the drive often wipes them soon after. And full disk encryption, such as BitLocker or FileVault, makes the raw bytes unreadable without the key.

Lesson 4 of 9

Time stamps and timelines

Time is the backbone of every investigation. File systems keep several time stamps for each file, often called MAC times: when the content was last Modified, when it was last Accessed, and when the file record was Changed or the file was Created, depending on the system. NTFS, the Windows file system, keeps two sets: one in a part called $STANDARD_INFORMATION, which normal programs can change, and one in $FILE_NAME, which is much harder to change. Attackers sometimes use a trick called timestomping, giving a malicious file a fake old date so it hides among old files. When the $STANDARD_INFORMATION date is earlier than the $FILE_NAME date, or the fraction of a second is exactly zero, an examiner gets suspicious. Windows stores times as a number called FILETIME, which counts 100 nanosecond steps since 1 January 1601. Linux and most logs use Unix time, the seconds since 1 January 1970. Converting between them, and from local time to UTC, is a daily job, and a mistake of one hour from daylight saving or of five and a half hours from Indian time can ruin a timeline. The goal is a super timeline: every event from every source, in one list, in UTC, sorted by time. The free tool Plaso, also called log2timeline, builds one from hundreds of kinds of logs and files, and the story of the attack usually jumps out, as in the animation.

Lesson 5 of 9

Memory and artefact forensics

Memory forensics studies a copy of a computer’s RAM. It matters more every year, because a lot of modern malware never writes a file to disk. It lives only in memory, so a disk image would miss it. From a memory image, an examiner can list running processes, open network connections, loaded program libraries, command lines that were typed, and sometimes passwords and encryption keys. The best known free tool is the Volatility Framework, first released in 2007 by Aaron Walters, now in its third version. One clever trick shows its power. Windows keeps its running processes in a linked list. A rootkit can hide by unlinking itself from the list, a technique called direct kernel object manipulation. Tools that walk the list, such as Task Manager, no longer see it. But Volatility’s psscan ignores the list and scans all of memory for the pattern of a process structure, so the hidden process shows up, as the program below shows. Beyond memory, operating systems leave many useful artefacts. On Windows, Prefetch files show which programs ran and when, the registry Run keys show what starts at boot, the Amcache and ShimCache list programs that were present, and browser history shows websites visited. On Linux, bash history, auth.log and cron files tell a similar story. Phones and cloud accounts have their own artefacts, and cloud forensics often relies on the provider’s audit logs, because you cannot take the provider’s disks.

Going deeper: Why scanning beats walking

Walking a list trusts every pointer in it, so one changed pointer hides an entry. Scanning trusts nothing: it checks every position for the expected pattern, such as the tag and the shape of a process structure. Scanning also finds processes that have exited but whose memory has not yet been reused. The cost is speed and some false hits, so examiners compare both views and look at the differences.

Chain of custody, reports and the law

Evidence is only useful if others can trust it. The chain of custody is a written record of every person who held the evidence, when, where and why, from the moment it was seized to the moment it reaches court. Each hand over is signed, and the hash is checked again, as the animation shows. A gap in the chain, or a hash that no longer matches, lets the other side argue that the evidence was changed. In India, electronic records used in court must come with a certificate. For years this was section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, and the Supreme Court said in Anvar P.V. versus P.K. Basheer in 2014, and again in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar in 2020, that the certificate is required. From 1 July 2024 the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam replaced the old Act, and section 63 now covers electronic records. The final product of forensic work is the report. A good report says what questions were asked, what evidence was collected and how, which tools and versions were used, what was found, what it means, and how sure the examiner is. It separates facts from opinions, and it is written so that a judge or a manager who is not technical can follow it. The examiner must be ready to explain every step and to have another expert repeat it and get the same result.

Kinds of forensics compared

Forensics splits into fields by where the evidence lives. Each needs its own tools and has its own limits. A real investigation usually combines several of them, for example memory and disk from the infected laptop, logs from the SIEM, and audit logs from the cloud.

Lesson 8 of 9

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that computers keep traces and that evidence must not be changed. A student can hash images, carve files, read time stamps and build timelines. A professional examiner collects evidence on site, analyses memory and disks, and writes reports that hold up in court. Experts research new artefacts, defeat anti forensic tricks, and give testimony in difficult cases.
One idea, four depths

Every action on a computer leaves a small trace. Detectives collect those traces carefully, never change them, and write down who looked after them.

Order of volatility, write blockers, SHA-256 verification, FAT deletion and slack space, carving by magic numbers, MAC times and timestomping, FILETIME, memory lists versus scans, chain of custody.

Live response and memory capture, Autopsy and Volatility analysis, super timelines with Plaso, mobile and cloud acquisition, expert reports and courtroom testimony under section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam.

New artefacts in each OS release, anti forensics and its detection, forensics of encrypted and TRIM enabled storage, memory analysis of virtual machines and containers, and the statistics of evidence strength.
