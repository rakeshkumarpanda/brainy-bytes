Lesson 1 of 8

What threat intelligence is, and the intelligence cycle

Threat intelligence means knowledge about attackers that helps you defend. Raw data, such as a list of a million IP addresses, is not intelligence. It becomes intelligence when someone checks it, adds context, and makes it useful for a decision: block this domain today, patch this product first, or train staff on this new kind of scam. The idea comes from military and government intelligence, which works in a loop called the intelligence cycle. First, direction: decide what questions matter, such as which groups target banks in India. Second, collection: gather data from sources such as your own logs, vendor reports, government advisories from CERT-In, sharing groups and the open internet. Third, processing: clean it, remove duplicates and put it in a standard format. Fourth, analysis: connect the pieces and judge what they mean, with a level of confidence. Fifth, dissemination: give the right answer to the right people in the right form. Sixth, feedback: ask whether it helped and adjust the questions. A good example of intelligence in action is the SolarWinds case. In December 2020 the security company FireEye found that its own tools had been stolen, traced the intrusion to a poisoned SolarWinds software update, and published detailed indicators and detection rules within days. Thousands of other defenders used that intelligence to check their own networks.

Indicators and the Pyramid of Pain

An indicator of compromise, IOC for short, is a clue that an attack happened or is happening: a file hash, an IP address, a domain name, a strange file path or registry key. Indicator feeds share these clues, and the program below matches a feed against logs. But not all clues are equal. In 2013 David Bianco drew the Pyramid of Pain. At the bottom are file hashes. They are exact and easy to block, but an attacker can change one byte of the malware and get a new hash in seconds, so blocking a hash causes almost no pain. Above that are IP addresses, which cost a few minutes to change, and domain names, which cost a little money. Higher up are artefacts the attack leaves on the network and on machines, then the attacker’s tools, and at the top their tactics, techniques and procedures, TTPs, which means the way they work. Changing how they work is slow and expensive for an attacker. So the higher up the pyramid a defender can detect, the more pain they cause. Indicators also go stale. Cloud IP addresses are handed to new, innocent owners within days, so an old IP indicator causes false alarms. Good teams give each indicator an expiry date, as the program below does.

Models of an attack: Kill Chain, Diamond and ATT&amp;CK

To describe attacks in the same words, defenders use models. In 2011 three Lockheed Martin analysts, Eric Hutchins, Michael Cloppert and Rohan Amin, described the Cyber Kill Chain, borrowing a military term. An intrusion has seven links: reconnaissance, weaponisation, delivery, exploitation, installation, command and control, and actions on objectives. The attacker needs every link, but the defender only needs to break one, and breaking an early link causes less harm, as the animation shows. In 2013 Sergio Caltagirone, Andrew Pendergast and Christopher Betz published the Diamond Model, which says every intrusion event has four corners: an adversary, a capability such as malware, infrastructure such as servers, and a victim. Knowing one corner helps find the others. For example, a server used against one victim may reveal other victims. The most used model today is MITRE ATT&CK, started inside MITRE in 2013 and made public in 2015. It is a large, free knowledge base built from real incidents. Its Enterprise matrix has 14 tactics, which are the attacker’s goals, from reconnaissance to impact, and hundreds of techniques and sub techniques under them, each with an ID such as T1110 for brute force. Teams tag every detection rule with its technique, which shows coverage and gaps, as in the second animation and program.

Lesson 4 of 8

Sharing intelligence: STIX, TAXII, TLP and MISP

Intelligence is worth more when it is shared, because an attack seen at one bank today may reach another bank tomorrow. Sharing needs a common language and clear rules. STIX, Structured Threat Information Expression, now at version 2.1 and run by the standards body OASIS, is a JSON format for objects such as indicators, malware, threat actors, attack patterns and the relationships between them. TAXII is the matching protocol for sending STIX over the internet. The open source platform MISP, widely used by CERTs and companies, stores and shares intelligence and speaks STIX. Rules about who may see what come from the Traffic Light Protocol. Its version 2.0, published by FIRST in August 2022, has five labels. TLP:RED means only the named people. TLP:AMBER+STRICT means only inside your own organisation. TLP:AMBER means your organisation and clients who need it. TLP:GREEN means your community, but not public. TLP:CLEAR means anyone. Sharing groups exist for many sectors, called ISACs, Information Sharing and Analysis Centres, such as the financial sector FS-ISAC. In India, CERT-In publishes advisories and vulnerability notes, and sector CSIRTs cover areas such as finance and power. A last word on quality: a feed with many false indicators can do harm, for example by blocking a popular content delivery network, so test feeds before you let them block anything automatically.

Analysis with confidence: sources, Bayes and hunting

An intelligence report must say how sure it is. Two simple tools help. The NATO Admiralty code rates the source from A, completely reliable, to F, reliability cannot be judged, and the information itself from 1, confirmed by other sources, to 6, truth cannot be judged. So B2 means a usually reliable source with probably true information. Analysts also use careful words for likelihood, such as likely or almost certain, following standards such as the US ICD 203. Under the words sits Bayes’ rule. Start with a prior chance that an IP is malicious. Each independent report multiplies the odds by a likelihood ratio, which says how much more likely that report is if the IP is really bad. A strong source might have a ratio of 4, your own sandbox seeing it call home a ratio of 9, and a source you know nothing about a ratio of 1, which changes nothing. The program below shows the chance climbing from 20 percent to over 90 percent. One trap is counting the same evidence twice: two feeds that copy each other are one source. Intelligence also drives threat hunting. A hunter starts with a hypothesis from intelligence, such as this group uses scheduled tasks for persistence, searches the logs for it, and either finds an intruder or writes a new rule so the SIEM watches for it from now on. Some clues can be found by pattern alone, like domains that malware makes up by itself, called a domain generation algorithm or DGA. The Conficker worm in 2008 and 2009 made up to 50000 names a day, and defenders had to work out its algorithm to block them in advance.

Kinds of intelligence sources compared

Intelligence comes from many places, each with strengths and weaknesses. Your own telemetry is the most relevant but sees only what reached you. Commercial feeds are broad but costly and vary in quality. Open source intelligence is free but noisy. Government and sharing groups are trusted but can be slower. A mature team blends them and measures which ones actually led to detections.

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that defenders share warnings about attackers. A student can match indicators, map rules to ATT&CK and update confidence with Bayes. A working analyst writes reports with confidence levels, runs hunts and manages feeds. Experts track whole groups across years, link campaigns, and advise leaders on strategic risk.

Defenders tell each other about tricks and bad websites they have seen, so others can block them early, like neighbours warning each other about a thief in the area.
