Lesson 1 of 8

Governance, risk and compliance: steering security

So far this course has looked at how to protect, detect and respond. Governance, risk and compliance, often called GRC, is about steering all of that. Governance means deciding who is in charge, what the rules are, how much risk the organisation is willing to take, which is called its risk appetite, and how leaders check that security is working. In most companies the board of directors is responsible in the end, and a chief information security officer, the CISO, runs the programme day to day. Risk management means finding, measuring and treating risks, which the foundations chapter on risk covered with matrices, ALE and FAIR simulations. Compliance means meeting the laws, regulations, contracts and standards that apply, and proving it to auditors, customers and regulators. GRC can sound like paperwork, but it decides where the money goes, and failures here cause real harm. After the Equifax breach of 2017, US lawmakers asked why a known patch had not been applied for months, and the answers pointed to unclear ownership. In 2023 the US Securities and Exchange Commission even charged SolarWinds and its CISO over how security risks had been described to investors, although a court later dismissed most of those claims. Good governance makes security everyone’s business, with clear owners, written rules, measured results, and regular reports to leaders in words they understand.

What it is
GRC is how an organisation steers security: governance sets owners, rules and risk appetite, risk management decides what to fix first, and compliance proves the rules and laws are met.
Why we need it
Without it, security spending is random, nobody owns the gaps, laws are broken without anyone noticing, and leaders cannot judge whether the organisation is safe.
How it works
Leaders approve policies and risk appetite, a CISO runs a programme based on a framework, risks are registered and treated, controls are audited, and results are reported with clear metrics.

Frameworks: ISO 27001, NIST CSF 2.0, CIS Controls, SOC 2 and PCI DSS

A framework is a ready made structure so you do not have to invent security from nothing. ISO/IEC 27001 is the international standard for an information security management system, an ISMS. It asks you to set the scope, assess risks, choose controls, run them, measure them and improve, in a loop called plan, do, check, act. Its 2022 version lists 93 example controls in Annex A, grouped in four themes: 37 organisational, 8 people, 14 physical and 34 technological. Companies can be certified against ISO 27001 by an accredited auditor, which customers often ask for. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework, first published in 2014, describes security outcomes in functions. Version 2.0, from February 2024, has six: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover, with Govern new and at the centre. Organisations write a current profile and a target profile and plan the gap. The CIS Critical Security Controls, version 8, list 18 controls with 153 practical safeguards, and Implementation Group 1, with 56 safeguards, is a good starting point for small organisations. SOC 2 is an auditor’s report, from the American Institute of CPAs, on a service company’s controls against five trust services criteria: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy. A Type I report checks the design at one point in time, and a Type II report checks that controls worked over a period, usually 6 to 12 months. PCI DSS, now version 4.0, is the rule book for anyone who stores, processes or sends payment card data. Many controls appear in all of these, so teams keep a crosswalk, as the program below shows.

Lesson 3 of 8

Audits, evidence and sampling

An audit checks whether controls are designed well and whether they actually work. Internal auditors work for the company but report independently. External auditors, such as ISO certification bodies, SOC 2 firms and PCI qualified security assessors, come from outside. Auditors ask for evidence: screenshots of settings, exported logs, signed approvals, training records and interview notes. Because an auditor cannot check every one of thousands of changes, user accounts or tickets, they test a random sample. The maths decides how big the sample must be. If a fraction p of items are bad and the auditor tests n items picked at random, the chance of finding at least one bad item is one minus the chance that all n are good, which is one minus (1 minus p) to the power n. With a 5 percent failure rate and a sample of 25, that is only about 72 percent, so more than a quarter of audits would wrongly come out clean. To reach 95 percent confidence, the auditor needs about 59 items, as the animation and program show. Findings are rated by severity, and each gets an owner, a fix and a due date. A finding that repeats from the last audit is treated more seriously, because it shows the fix did not work.

Sample size for a confidence CExam favourite
n = ⌈ ln(1 − C) / ln(1 − p) ⌉
C = 0.95, p = 0.05: ln 0.05 / ln 0.95 = 58.4, so n = 59.
Going deeper: Why the formula is only approximate

Picking without putting items back changes the odds a little after each pick, which the hypergeometric distribution handles exactly. When the sample is small compared with the population, the simple formula is close enough, and it errs on the safe side. Auditing standards publish tables built on the exact maths.

Lesson 4 of 8

Cyber law in India and around the world

Laws set the minimum that organisations must do and punish people who break into systems. In India, the Information Technology Act of 2000, strengthened in 2008, is the main cyber law. Section 43 makes a person who accesses, damages or downloads from a computer without permission liable to pay compensation, and section 66 makes doing it dishonestly or fraudulently a crime, with up to three years in prison or a fine up to five lakh rupees. Section 66C covers identity theft, such as using someone’s password or digital signature, and 66D covers cheating by pretending to be someone else using a computer, which includes many online scams. Section 66F covers cyber terrorism and can carry life imprisonment. Section 43A required companies handling sensitive personal data to keep reasonable security, and section 72A punishes disclosing personal information in breach of a contract. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023 now sets rules for personal data of people in India: get valid consent, use data only for the stated purpose, keep it safe, tell the Data Protection Board and the affected people about breaches, and respect rights such as correction and erasure. Its penalties go up to 250 crore rupees for failing to keep reasonable security safeguards. CERT-In’s 2022 directions require reporting many incidents within 6 hours and keeping logs for 180 days. Regulators add sector rules, such as RBI for banks and SEBI for market firms. Around the world, the EU’s GDPR, in force since May 2018, allows fines up to 20 million euros or 4 percent of worldwide turnover, and the largest so far is 1.2 billion euros against Meta in 2023. The US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986 covers unauthorised access, and the Budapest Convention of 2001 helps countries cooperate on cyber crime.

Lesson 5 of 8

Privacy, third parties and people

Privacy is not the same as security. Security keeps data safe from people who should not have it. Privacy is about whether the organisation should collect and use the data at all, and whether it is fair to the person it belongs to. GDPR article 5 lists the principles: lawful, fair and transparent use, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality, and accountability. India’s DPDP Act follows similar ideas. Privacy by design, a term from Ann Cavoukian, a privacy commissioner in Canada, means building these protections in from the start. For risky new projects, such as face recognition or tracking large numbers of people, a data protection impact assessment, DPIA, is done before launch, as the program below screens for. Next, third parties. Most organisations depend on hundreds of suppliers, and many breaches start there. The 2013 Target breach began with stolen credentials from a heating and air conditioning contractor. Third party risk management means tiering suppliers by how much data and access they have, asking for evidence such as SOC 2 reports or ISO certificates, writing security duties into contracts, and reviewing the riskiest ones every year. Last, people. Security awareness training, phishing simulations and a culture where staff report mistakes quickly are controls too, and they can be measured, as the metrics program shows.

Lesson 6 of 8

Compliance and security compared

Being compliant and being secure are related but not the same. Compliance proves you meet a written set of rules at the time of the audit. Security is the real ability to resist and recover from attacks every day. Target was certified as PCI compliant shortly before its 2013 breach. A good programme uses compliance as a floor and a source of discipline, and keeps going beyond it.

Bottom line: Aim to be secure, and let compliance be the evidence. Map controls once to many frameworks, so the same work satisfies every audit.

Lesson 7 of 8

From beginner to expert

A beginner knows that organisations have security rules, that laws protect personal data, and that breaking into computers is a crime. A student can compare frameworks, compute sample sizes and fines, and explain the main Indian and EU laws. A professional runs an ISMS, audits, vendor programmes and board reporting. Experts shape policy and law, lead large compliance programmes and research how to measure security well.
One idea, four depths

Schools and companies write rules about using computers safely, and the law punishes people who break into computers or misuse other people’s data.

Policy, standard, procedure, guideline. ISO 27001 Annex A themes, NIST CSF 2.0 functions, CIS IGs, SOC 2 types, PCI DSS. Audit sampling maths, GDPR and DPDP fines, IT Act sections.

Run an ISMS to certification, lead SOC 2 and PCI audits, manage third party risk, write DPIAs, report KPIs and KRIs to the board, and earn CISSP, CISM or CISA.

Cyber risk quantification for boards and insurers, measuring control effectiveness with data, regulation design and its economics, and international law on state cyber operations (the Tallinn Manual).
