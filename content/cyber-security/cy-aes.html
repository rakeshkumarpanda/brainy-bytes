Lesson 1 of 10

Symmetric encryption and block ciphers

Modern encryption began in the 1970s. Banks and governments needed a public standard, so in 1977 the United States adopted the Data Encryption Standard, DES, based on an IBM design called Lucifer. DES used a 56 bit key. At the time that was a lot, but computers kept getting faster. In 1998 the Electronic Frontier Foundation built a machine called Deep Crack for about 250,000 dollars that found a DES key in 56 hours, and in 1999, working with thousands of volunteers over the internet, it took less than a day. The world needed a replacement, and this time NIST ran an open competition. Fifteen designs from around the world were attacked in public for three years. In October 2000 the winner was announced: Rijndael, by two Belgian researchers, Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen. It became the Advanced Encryption Standard, AES, in 2001. Today AES protects your Wi-Fi, your disk, your phone, your messages and almost every secure web page, and modern processors have special instructions that run it in a few cycles per byte. In more than twenty years, nobody has found a practical way to break it.

Source: FIPS 197, NIST SP 800-38A and 800-38D, SP 800-131A Rev. 2, RFC 8439, RFC 5652, Vaudenay (2002), Bhargavan and Leurent (2016).

What it is
Symmetric encryption uses the same secret key to encrypt and decrypt. A block cipher such as AES encrypts one fixed size block (128 bits for AES) at a time. A mode of operation says how to use it for longer messages.
Why we need it
Symmetric ciphers are fast, so they encrypt almost all real data. Public key cryptography, covered later, is mostly used to agree on a symmetric key.
How it works
A block cipher is a keyed permutation: for each key it shuffles all 2128 possible blocks in a way that looks random. It is built from many rounds of simple steps that create confusion (the key is mixed in non linearly) and diffusion (each input bit affects every output bit).

Lesson 2 of 10

The maths inside AES: the field GF(2<sup>8</sup>)

AES treats every byte as a small polynomial. The byte 0x57, which is 0101 0111 in binary, stands for x to the 6 plus x to the 4 plus x squared plus x plus 1, one term for each 1 bit. Adding two bytes means adding polynomials whose coefficients are only 0 or 1, where 1 plus 1 is 0. That is exactly the XOR operation. Multiplying is like school long multiplication of polynomials, but the result can grow past 8 bits, so we divide by a fixed polynomial of degree 8 and keep the remainder, much like taking a number modulo 26 in the Caesar cipher. AES uses x to the 8 plus x to the 4 plus x cubed plus x plus 1, which is 0x11B. This set of 256 bytes with these rules is a finite field, named GF of 2 to the 8, after Évariste Galois, the French mathematician who died at 20 in a duel in 1832 and left the theory behind. In a field, every non zero element has an inverse, so we can divide. AES uses that inverse to build its S-box, because inversion is very non linear, which is just what a cipher needs.
What it is
GF(28) is a set of 256 elements (the bytes) where addition is XOR and multiplication is polynomial multiplication modulo m(x) = x8 + x4 + x3 + x + 1.
Why we need it
AES needs byte operations that can be undone (a field has inverses) and that mix bits in a non linear way. GF(28) gives both, and it fits exactly in one byte.
How it works
Add with XOR. Multiply by 2 with xtime: shift left one bit and, if a 9th bit appears, XOR with 0x11B. Multiply by any byte by adding shifted copies. The inverse of a is the byte b with a · b = 1.

Going deeper: Why the S-box resists attacks

Linear cryptanalysis (Matsui, 1993) looks for linear equations between input, output and key bits that hold more often than half the time. Differential cryptanalysis (Biham and Shamir, 1990) looks for input differences that cause one output difference with high probability. Inversion in GF(28) has the best possible behaviour for an 8 bit map: no input difference leads to any output difference with probability above 4/256, and the best linear approximation holds with probability at most 1/2 + 16/256. AES then uses ShiftRows and MixColumns so that any 4 round path must pass through at least 25 S-boxes, which makes both attacks far more expensive than brute force.

Lesson 3 of 10

How AES works: rounds, state and key schedule

AES works on a block of 16 bytes arranged as a 4 by 4 grid called the state. Each round applies four simple steps. SubBytes swaps each byte through the S-box. ShiftRows slides the rows sideways by different amounts. MixColumns multiplies each column by a fixed matrix in the field you just met. AddRoundKey XORs in 16 bytes of key material. A 128 bit key gets 10 rounds, a 192 bit key 12, and a 256 bit key 14. The key schedule stretches the one main key into a different round key for each round. Why so many rounds? Each round mixes a little, and the designers proved that after a few rounds every output bit depends on every input bit and every key bit in a complicated way. The best known attack on full AES-128, the biclique attack from 2011, is only about four times faster than trying every key, which still means 2 to the 126 operations. That is far beyond any computer that exists or is planned.
What it is
AES encrypts a 128 bit block with a 128, 192 or 256 bit key, using 10, 12 or 14 rounds of SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns and AddRoundKey on a 4 × 4 byte state.
Why we need it
It is the most used cipher in the world. Understanding its steps tells you why a key alone is not enough: the mode, the IV and the integrity check matter just as much.
How it works
Start with AddRoundKey using the first round key. Then do the full round 9 times (for AES-128) and a last round without MixColumns. Decryption runs the inverse steps in reverse order.

Lesson 4 of 10

Modes of operation: ECB, CBC and CTR

A block cipher only encrypts 16 bytes. Real messages are longer, so we need a mode of operation, a recipe for using the cipher many times. The obvious recipe, called electronic codebook or ECB, encrypts each block separately. It has a famous flaw. When someone encrypted a picture of Tux, the Linux penguin, with ECB, the encrypted picture still clearly showed the penguin, because every identical block of the picture became the same encrypted block. Cipher block chaining, CBC, invented at IBM in 1976, fixes this by mixing each block with the previous encrypted block, starting from a random value called the initialisation vector, or IV. Counter mode, CTR, turns the block cipher into a stream cipher: it encrypts a counter to make a long key stream, which is XORed with the message, just like a one time pad made from a short key. CTR can encrypt many blocks at once in parallel, which is why it is the basis of the fastest modern modes. But each mode has its own rule that must never be broken. For CBC the IV must be unpredictable. For CTR, a nonce and counter pair must never repeat with the same key, or you are back to the two time pad.
What it is
A mode of operation turns a block cipher into a way to encrypt messages of any length. ECB encrypts blocks separately, CBC chains each block to the previous ciphertext, CTR XORs the message with an encrypted counter.
Why we need it
The mode decides what an attacker can see. ECB shows repeated blocks. A repeated CTR nonce reveals the XOR of two messages. A predictable CBC IV allowed the BEAST attack on TLS 1.0 in 2011.
How it works
ECB: Ci = E(Pi). CBC: Ci = E(Pi ⊕ Ci−1), C0 = IV. CTR: Ci = Pi ⊕ E(nonce ‖ i).

Lesson 5 of 10

Padding and the padding oracle attack

ECB and CBC can only encrypt whole blocks, so a message that is 13 bytes long needs 3 extra bytes. The usual rule, called PKCS 7 padding, fills the gap with bytes that all have the value of the gap: three bytes of value 3. If the message already fills the last block exactly, a whole extra block of sixteen 16s is added, so the receiver can always remove the padding without guessing. That sounds harmless, but in 2002 the Swiss cryptographer Serge Vaudenay showed a frightening attack. If a server tells you, even indirectly, whether the padding of a ciphertext you sent was correct, then you can decrypt any CBC message byte by byte, with about 128 tries per byte on average, without ever learning the key. The leak can be an error message, a different status code, or even a few microseconds of difference in response time. Padding oracle attacks hit ASP.NET in 2010, and TLS suffered related attacks such as Lucky Thirteen in 2013 and POODLE in 2014. The deep lesson: encryption without an integrity check lets attackers play with your ciphertexts and learn from your reactions.
What it is
PKCS 7 padding adds N bytes of value N (1 ≤ N ≤ 16) so the message fills whole blocks. A padding oracle is any behaviour that tells an attacker whether a ciphertext decrypted to valid padding.
Why we need it
It shows why "encrypt only" designs fail. The fix is authenticated encryption, which rejects any changed ciphertext before looking at its padding.
How it works
The attacker changes the previous ciphertext block byte by byte. When the server says the padding is valid, the attacker knows that the last decrypted byte equals 01 (then 02 02, and so on) and can solve for the real plaintext byte with XOR.

Lesson 6 of 10

Authenticated encryption: AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305

Encryption hides a message, but it does not stop someone from changing it. In CTR mode, flipping one bit of the ciphertext flips the same bit of the plaintext, so an attacker who knows the message format could turn a payment of 100 into 900 without knowing the key. The answer is authenticated encryption with associated data, AEAD, which encrypts and adds a tag that proves the ciphertext and some extra unencrypted data, like a header, were not changed. Galois Counter Mode, GCM, standardised by NIST in 2007, combines counter mode with a fast checksum computed in the field GF of 2 to the 128. ChaCha20-Poly1305, built by Daniel Bernstein, is the other main choice and is fast on phones without AES hardware. TLS 1.3, the protocol behind secure web pages, allows only AEAD ciphers. There is one strict rule for GCM: never use the same nonce twice with the same key. If you do, the attacker gets the XOR of the two messages, and worse, can recover the secret value used for the tag and then forge messages. This is called the forbidden attack, and researchers found real web servers making exactly this mistake in 2016.
What it is
AEAD (authenticated encryption with associated data) gives confidentiality and integrity together. AES-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 encrypt the message and produce a 16 byte tag over the ciphertext and the associated data.
Why we need it
An attacker who changes even one bit is caught before the data is used. This removes padding oracles and bit flipping attacks at once.
How it works
Encrypt with a key, a unique nonce, the plaintext and optional associated data. Decrypt with the same key, nonce and associated data. If the tag does not match, the library raises an error and returns nothing.

Lesson 7 of 10

Key sizes, brute force and the birthday bound

How long does a key need to be? Each extra bit doubles the work of trying every key. A 56 bit DES key has about 72 thousand million million keys, and special hardware tried them in days in 1998. A 128 bit AES key has 2 to the 128 keys, a number with 39 digits. If every one of the eight billion people on Earth had a computer trying a billion billion keys per second, searching half the space would still take more than six hundred years. Quantum computers change the picture a little. Grover’s algorithm, from 1996, can search an unordered space of N items in about the square root of N steps, so it would cut AES-128 to around 2 to the 64 quantum steps, and AES-256 to 2 to the 128. That is why governments now ask for AES-256 for long term secrets. A second limit matters for the block size. With 64 bit blocks, as in 3DES, two ciphertext blocks become equal by chance after about 2 to the 32 blocks, around 32 gigabytes, because of the birthday paradox. In 2016 the Sweet32 attack used that to recover secret cookies from long running 3DES connections.
What it is
The key size sets brute force cost: 2n keys for an n bit key. The block size sets how much data one key can safely encrypt before random collisions leak information.
Why we need it
Choosing sizes is a real engineering decision: AES-128 is enough today against classical computers, AES-256 adds a margin against quantum search, and 64 bit blocks are no longer acceptable.
How it works
Average brute force time = 2n−1 / speed. Grover reduces this to about 2n/2. Collisions among b bit blocks appear after about 2b/2 blocks.

Lesson 8 of 10

Symmetric choices compared

When you pick a symmetric cipher today, the real decision is rarely the algorithm, because AES and ChaCha20 are both excellent. The decision is the mode and how you handle nonces and keys. Encrypting a disk needs a mode that keeps each sector the same size, so XTS is used there, but it cannot detect tampering, which is acceptable for a stolen laptop but not for a network. Encrypting network messages needs an AEAD such as GCM. Encrypting many messages with random nonces suggests XChaCha20. And when you build a whole protocol, use a vetted library such as libsodium or the Python cryptography package, rather than putting the pieces together yourself, because most real failures happen in that putting together.

Lesson 9 of 10

From beginner to expert

Symmetric cryptography has layers. A beginner knows that one shared key locks and unlocks, and that AES is the standard. A college student can describe the AES round, explain modes and why ECB is unsafe, and use AES-GCM correctly from a library. A masters student studies security definitions: a block cipher should be a pseudorandom permutation, CBC and CTR are secure against chosen plaintext attacks with random IVs, and GCM is secure against chosen ciphertext attacks as long as nonces are unique. They also study side channels, where the key leaks through time, power use or electromagnetic signals. At the research frontier, cryptographers design lightweight ciphers for tiny devices, like Ascon, which NIST selected in 2023 and published as a standard in 2025. They also study how to keep implementations constant time, and how quantum computers might change the security of symmetric schemes beyond Grover’s search.
One idea, four depths

One secret key locks and unlocks. AES is the cipher everyone uses. Never encrypt a picture or file block by block with ECB.

Explain the AES round and the four steps, multiply in GF(28), compare ECB, CBC, CTR and GCM, apply PKCS 7 padding, and use AES-GCM from a library with unique nonces.

Pseudorandom permutations and the PRP/PRF switching lemma, IND-CPA security of CBC and CTR, IND-CCA and authenticated encryption (GCM, encrypt then MAC), padding oracles as chosen ciphertext attacks, and side channels: cache timing attacks on table based AES (Bernstein in 2005, and Osvik, Shamir and Tromer in 2006) and power analysis.

Lightweight cryptography (Ascon, NIST SP 800-232, 2025), nonce misuse resistant AEAD (GCM-SIV, RFC 8452), wide block and tweakable ciphers, formal verification of constant time code, and the effect of quantum algorithms beyond Grover, such as Simon’s algorithm breaking some modes in the quantum query model.

Lesson 10 of 10

Chapter summary

  1. Symmetric encryption uses one shared key. A block cipher encrypts fixed blocks. DES (56 bit) fell in 1998. AES (128 bit block, 128, 192 or 256 bit key) has been the standard since 2001.
  2. AES works in GF(28): add = XOR, multiply mod x8 + x4 + x3 + x + 1 (0x11B). {{57}} + {{83}} = {{d4}}, {{57}} · {{83}} = {{c1}}.
  3. Each AES round: SubBytes (S-box, non linear), ShiftRows, MixColumns, AddRoundKey. 10, 12 or 14 rounds. The last round has no MixColumns.
  4. ECB leaks repeated blocks. CBC: Ci = E(Pi ⊕ Ci−1) with a random IV. CTR: Ci = Pi ⊕ E(nonce ‖ i), never repeat a nonce.
  5. PKCS 7 adds N bytes of value N. A padding oracle decrypts CBC in about 128 tries per byte.
  6. Use AEAD: AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305, 96 bit unique nonce, 16 byte tag. Nonce reuse in GCM is catastrophic.
  7. Brute force: 2n−1 tries on average. Grover gives about 2n/2, so AES-256 for long term secrets. 64 bit blocks collide after about 232 blocks (Sweet32).
