Lesson 1 of 11

Why elliptic curves

In 1985, Neal Koblitz at the University of Washington and Victor Miller at IBM independently suggested a new home for the discrete logarithm problem: the points of an elliptic curve over a finite field. Elliptic curves had been studied by mathematicians for more than a century, and they were at the heart of Andrew Wiles’s proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem in 1995. What made them attractive for cryptography is that the clever sub exponential attacks that work modulo a prime, index calculus and the number field sieve, do not work on well chosen curves. The best known attack is the generic one, Pollard’s rho, which takes about the square root of the group size. So a 256 bit curve gives about 128 bits of security, which needs a 3072 bit RSA key. Smaller keys mean faster handshakes, smaller certificates and less power on phones and smart cards. Adoption was slow at first because of patents and caution, but the NSA endorsed elliptic curves in its Suite B in 2005, Bitcoin chose the curve secp256k1 in 2009, and today X25519 and ECDSA or Ed25519 protect most TLS connections, SSH logins and messaging apps.

Source: Koblitz, Elliptic Curve Cryptosystems (1987), Miller, Use of Elliptic Curves in Cryptography (CRYPTO 1985), Hankerson, Menezes and Vanstone, Guide to Elliptic Curve Cryptography, Washington, Elliptic Curves: Number Theory and Cryptography, SEC 2, FIPS 186-5, RFC 6979, RFC 7748, RFC 8032, Bernstein and Lange, SafeCurves.

What it is
Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) does Diffie-Hellman, signatures and encryption in the group of points of an elliptic curve over a finite field, where the elliptic curve discrete log problem (ECDLP) is hard.
Why we need it
It gives the same security as RSA or finite field Diffie-Hellman with keys about 12 times smaller and much faster private key operations.
How it works
Points are added with simple formulas. A private key is a number d, the public key is the point Q = dG. Finding d from Q is the ECDLP, which takes about √n steps for a group of size n.

Lesson 2 of 11

Elliptic curves over a finite field

An elliptic curve, despite the name, has nothing to do with ellipses. The name comes from elliptic integrals, which mathematicians used to measure the length of an ellipse in the 1800s. For cryptography, a curve is the set of solutions of an equation of the form y squared equals x cubed plus a x plus b, called the short Weierstrass form after Karl Weierstrass. Over the real numbers it is a smooth curve, symmetric about the x axis. The condition that 4 a cubed plus 27 b squared is not zero makes sure it has no sharp point or self crossing. In cryptography we take x and y modulo a prime p, so the curve becomes a finite set of grid points. One extra point, called the point at infinity and written O, is added, and it will play the role of zero. How many points are there? Helmut Hasse proved in 1933 that the count is always close to p plus 1, within two times the square root of p. Counting exactly for a 256 bit prime is not done by trying every x, of course. René Schoof found a polynomial time algorithm in 1985, and that is how the sizes of standard curves were certified.
What it is
An elliptic curve over Fp is the set of (x, y) with y2 ≡ x3 + ax + b (mod p), together with the point at infinity O, where 4a3 + 27b2 ≢ 0 (mod p).
Why we need it
These points, with the addition rule of the next lesson, form a group whose discrete log problem is hard. Standard curves fix p, a, b, a base point G and its order n.
How it works
For each x, compute the right side and check if it is a square mod p (Euler’s criterion). Each square gives two points (x, y) and (x, p − y), and a zero gives one.

Lesson 3 of 11

Adding points: the group law

The magic of elliptic curves is that you can add two points and get a third point on the same curve. Over the real numbers the rule is geometric. Draw the straight line through P and Q. Because the equation is cubic, the line meets the curve in exactly one more point. Reflect that point across the x axis, and the result is P plus Q. To double a point, use the tangent line instead. If the line is vertical, it meets the curve at the point at infinity, so P plus its mirror image is O, which is why O acts as zero. The geometry turns into short formulas for the slope lambda and the new coordinates, and the same formulas work modulo p, where division means multiplying by an inverse. The hardest fact to prove is that this addition is associative, meaning that P plus Q, then plus R, equals P plus the sum of Q and R. A direct proof by algebra fills pages, and a clean proof uses deeper geometry. The program checks it on examples.

Group rules
P + O = P, P + (−P) = O with −(x, y) = (x, −y), P + Q = Q + P, (P + Q) + R = P + (Q + R)
The points form an abelian group. On the toy curve it has 19 elements, so it is cyclic and every point except O generates it.
Worked example: 2G for G = (5, 1) on y2 = x3 + 2x + 2 mod 17
  1. Tangent slope: 3 · 52 + 2 = 77 ≡ 9 (mod 17).
  2. 2y1 = 2, and 2−1 mod 17 = 9 because 2 × 9 = 18 ≡ 1.
  3. λ = 9 × 9 = 81 ≡ 13.
  4. x3 = 132 − 5 − 5 = 159 ≡ 6.
  5. y3 = 13 × (5 − 6) − 1 = −14 ≡ 3. So 2G = (6, 3).
Lesson 4 of 11

Scalar multiplication and the ECDLP

Adding G to itself k times gives the point k G, called scalar multiplication. Done naively, k G for a 256 bit k would need an impossible number of additions. Instead we use double and add, the same idea as square and multiply: read k in binary, double at every bit and add G when the bit is 1. That is about 256 doublings and 128 additions. Going backwards, from the point k G to the number k, is the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem. On a good curve, nobody knows anything better than Pollard’s rho, which needs about the square root of the group order, so about 2 to the 128 steps for a 256 bit curve. The largest prime field ECDLP solved in public was 112 bits, in 2009, using a cluster of 200 PlayStation 3 consoles. The animation shows how the multiples of G jump around the curve in a pattern that looks random. Real code must also be careful about time: a double and add loop that does extra work on 1 bits leaks the secret, so libraries use the Montgomery ladder or other constant time methods.
What it is
Scalar multiplication: kG = G + G + ... + G (k times). The ECDLP: given G and Q = kG, find k.
Why we need it
A private key is a random k from 1 to n − 1 and the public key is Q = kG. ECDH and ECDSA are secure only while the ECDLP is hard.
How it works
Double and add: R = O, then for each bit of k from the top, R = 2R, and if the bit is 1, R = R + G. Constant time code uses the Montgomery ladder, which does one doubling and one addition for every bit.

X25519: Diffie-Hellman done safely

In 2006 Daniel J. Bernstein published Curve25519, designed from the start to be fast and hard to misuse. The prime 2 to the 255 minus 19 makes reduction quick on ordinary processors. The curve is written in Montgomery form, where Peter Montgomery’s ladder from 1987 computes k times a point using only x coordinates, the same number of steps for every key, so timing does not leak. Every 32 byte string is accepted as a public key, and the maths is arranged so that a bad or invalid point cannot leak the secret, which removes a whole class of bugs. Secret keys are clamped: three low bits are cleared so the key is a multiple of the cofactor 8, which neutralises small subgroup points, and the top bit is fixed so the ladder always runs the same length. The scheme is called X25519 and is defined in RFC 7748 from 2016. It is now the default key exchange in TLS 1.3, SSH, Signal and the WireGuard VPN. The program checks the library against the test vectors printed in the RFC.
What it is
X25519 is elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman on Curve25519 using only x coordinates. Keys and shared secrets are 32 bytes.
Why we need it
It is fast, constant time by design, accepts any 32 byte public value safely, and avoids the invalid curve and timing bugs that hit many ECDH implementations.
How it works
Clamp the 32 byte secret, run the Montgomery ladder on the received x coordinate, output the x coordinate of the result. Then feed the shared secret to HKDF.

ECDSA signatures and the nonce rule

ECDSA, the elliptic curve digital signature algorithm, became an ANSI standard in 1999 and a NIST standard in 2000. To sign, the signer picks a fresh secret random number k, called the nonce, computes the point k G and takes its x coordinate as r. Then s is k inverse times the hash plus r times the private key, all modulo the group order n. The signature is the pair r and s. Anyone can verify using the public key Q. The nonce is the weak spot. If the same k is used for two different messages, two lines of algebra recover the private key. In December 2010, the group fail0verflow showed that Sony had signed PlayStation 3 software with the same k every time, so they computed Sony’s master private key. In 2013 a flaw in Android’s random number generator caused repeated nonces in Bitcoin wallets, and coins were stolen. Even a few biased bits of k are enough for lattice attacks, as the Minerva and TPM-Fail studies showed in 2019. The fix, RFC 6979 by Thomas Pornin in 2013, derives k deterministically from the private key and the message hash, so a broken random generator can no longer leak the key.
What it is
ECDSA: sign with nonce k: r = (kG).x mod n, s = k−1(H(m) + r d) mod n. Verify: u1 = H(m) s−1, u2 = r s−1, accept if (u1G + u2Q).x mod n = r.
Why we need it
It is the most widely deployed elliptic curve signature: TLS certificates, passkeys, Bitcoin before Taproot, code signing and smart cards.
How it works
Correctness: u1G + u2Q = s−1(H + r d)G = kG, so its x coordinate is r. The nonce k must be secret, uniform and never reused.

Common mistake
Never write your own nonce generator. Use a library that implements RFC 6979 or the hedged variant, or use Ed25519, which is deterministic by design.

Schnorr signatures and Ed25519

Claus Schnorr designed a simpler and cleaner signature in 1989, with a neat security proof, but he patented it, and ECDSA was designed partly to avoid the patent. After the patent expired in 2008, Schnorr style signatures came back. Ed25519, published in 2011 by Daniel Bernstein, Niels Duif, Tanja Lange, Peter Schwabe and Bo-Yin Yang, is a Schnorr signature on the Edwards form of Curve25519. It fixes the nonce problem by design: the nonce is a hash of a secret prefix and the message, so the same message always gives the same signature and no random generator is needed. Twisted Edwards curves have one formula for all additions, including doubling, which makes constant time code easier. Ed25519 keys are 32 bytes and signatures 64 bytes. It is standardised in RFC 8032 and in FIPS 186-5, and used for SSH keys, Git commit signing, Tor and many other systems. Bitcoin added Schnorr signatures on secp256k1 in its Taproot upgrade in November 2021, partly because Schnorr signatures can be added together for multi signatures.
What it is
A Schnorr signature is (R, s) with R = kG and s = k + H(R, Q, m) d. Ed25519 is a Schnorr signature on Edwards25519 with k = H(prefix, m), so it is deterministic.
Why we need it
It removes the nonce reuse disaster of ECDSA, is faster, has a simpler security proof, and supports aggregation for multi signatures.
How it works
Verify by checking sG = R + H(R, Q, m) Q. If k were reused for two messages, the same algebra as ECDSA would expose d, so the deterministic nonce matters.

Lesson 8 of 11

Attacks on careless ECC and on curve choice

The mathematics of good curves has held up for forty years, but implementations and curve choices have failed many times. The invalid curve attack, described by Ingrid Biehl, Bernd Meyer and Volker Müller in 2000, uses a quiet fact: the addition formulas never use the constant b. If a server multiplies a received point by its secret without checking that the point is on the curve, an attacker can send points from other curves that have small order, learn the secret modulo many small numbers, and join the pieces with the Chinese remainder theorem. In 2015 researchers showed this against real TLS libraries. Some curves are weak by structure: supersingular curves allow the MOV attack, which moves the problem into a finite field, and curves with exactly p points allow an attack in linear time. Curve choice itself raised trust questions. NIST’s curves were generated from seeds whose origin was never explained, and the random number generator Dual EC DRBG, standardised in 2006, turned out to contain what was widely seen as an NSA backdoor, as reports from 2013 suggested. And in January 2020, Windows CryptoAPI was found to accept certificates with a custom curve generator, a bug called CurveBall, reported by the NSA itself.

Real world scenario: Dual EC DRBG

In 2006 NIST published SP 800-90 with four random number generators. One, Dual EC DRBG, used two elliptic curve points P and Q. In 2007 Dan Shumow and Niels Ferguson of Microsoft showed that whoever knew a number e with P = eQ could predict all future output from a small sample. In 2013 documents leaked by Edward Snowden suggested the NSA had pushed the standard, and Reuters reported that RSA Security had been paid 10 million US dollars to make it the default in its BSAFE library. NIST withdrew the generator in 2014. The lesson for designers: constants in a standard should be explainable, and "nothing up my sleeve" numbers like the digits of π are preferred.

Lesson 9 of 11

Elliptic curves compared

With several curves and schemes in use, the choice comes down to the job and the rules you must follow. For key exchange, X25519 is the modern default. For signatures, Ed25519 is the easiest to use safely. P-256 with ECDSA is required where FIPS rules apply, and it is what most TLS certificates, smart cards and passkeys use. secp256k1 is mainly for cryptocurrencies. Ed448 and P-384 give more margin for long term keys. RSA is still needed to talk to older systems. None of these survive a large quantum computer. A 2017 estimate by Martin Roetteler and colleagues found that breaking P-256 with Shor’s algorithm would need about 2,300 logical qubits, fewer than RSA-2048, so the move to post quantum schemes covers elliptic curves too.

Lesson 10 of 11

From beginner to expert

Elliptic curves reach from a picture you can sketch to research mathematics. A beginner learns that an elliptic curve key is a small number, the public key a point, and that the same security as RSA comes from much shorter keys. A college student can add and double points by hand, run double and add, compute ECDH and ECDSA on a toy curve, and recover a key from a reused nonce. A masters student studies the group structure and Hasse’s theorem, point counting with Schoof’s algorithm, the MOV, anomalous and invalid curve attacks, pairings, and coordinate systems such as projective and Edwards coordinates that make code fast and constant time. At the research level, people work on pairing based cryptography, used in some zero knowledge proof systems, lattice attacks on biased nonces, formally verified implementations such as those in the Fiat Cryptography project, and isogenies between curves as a source of post quantum schemes. The 2022 break of SIKE showed that this area still holds surprises.
One idea, four depths

An elliptic curve key pair is a secret number and a public point. Turning the number into the point is fast. Going back is extremely hard, and keys are much shorter than RSA keys for the same safety.

Check points, count points with Euler’s criterion, add and double with the chord and tangent formulas, multiply by double and add, run ECDH, sign and verify ECDSA on a toy curve, and recover a key from a reused nonce.

Hasse’s theorem and Schoof’s algorithm, projective, Jacobian and Edwards coordinates, the Montgomery ladder, cofactors and clamping, MOV and anomalous curve attacks, invalid curve and twist attacks, Schnorr proofs in the random oracle model, and RFC 6979.

Pairings (Weil and Tate) and pairing friendly curves (BLS12-381) for BLS signatures and zk-SNARKs, lattice (hidden number problem) attacks on biased nonces, verified implementations (Fiat Cryptography), Shor resource estimates for ECDLP, and isogeny based cryptography after the SIKE break.

Explain that an ECC key is a number and a point, and why the keys are short.
Intermediate

Expert
Analyse curve security, pairings, lattice nonce attacks and post quantum isogeny research.

Chapter summary

  1. Curve: y2 = x3 + ax + b over Fp plus O, with 4a3 + 27b2 ≠ 0. Hasse: |#E − (p + 1)| ≤ 2√p. Toy curve mod 17 has 19 points.
  2. Addition: chord slope (y2 − y1)/(x2 − x1), tangent slope (3x2 + a)/(2y). x3 = λ2 − x1 − x2, y3 = λ(x1 − x3) − y1.
  3. Scalar multiplication by double and add or the Montgomery ladder. ECDLP: best attack Pollard rho, about √n, so 256 bit curves give 128 bit security.
  4. ECDH: S = aB = bA = abG. X25519: x only ladder, clamping, any input safe, RFC 7748.
  5. ECDSA: r = (kG)x, s = k−1(h + rd). Reused k gives d (PS3, 2010). Use RFC 6979.
  6. Ed25519: Schnorr on Edwards25519 with a deterministic nonce, 32 byte keys, 64 byte signatures.
  7. Attacks: invalid curve points, small subgroups, biased nonces, side channels, weak or unexplained curves (Dual EC DRBG, CurveBall).
  8. All of RSA, DH and ECC fall to Shor’s algorithm. Hybrids with ML-KEM and ML-DSA are the way forward.
