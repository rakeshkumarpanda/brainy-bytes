Lesson 1 of 9

What a vulnerability is, and how the world tracks them

A vulnerability is a weakness that an attacker can use to break a security property: to read what they should not, change what they should not, or stop a service. Most are ordinary bugs, a missing check or a wrong assumption, that happen to have security effects. Others are design flaws or unsafe default settings. Because the same flaw appears in thousands of companies at once, the world needs one name for each. In 1999 MITRE started the CVE list, Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures, which gives each flaw an identifier such as CVE-2021-44228. In 2006 MITRE added CWE, Common Weakness Enumeration, which names the kind of mistake, for example CWE-79 for cross site scripting or CWE-125 for an out of bounds read. The US National Vulnerability Database, run by NIST since 2005, adds scores and affected product lists. The numbers keep growing: about 40000 CVEs were published in 2024. A zero day is a flaw that attackers use before the vendor knows about it or has a fix, so defenders have had zero days to prepare. When a researcher finds a flaw, the usual practice is coordinated disclosure: tell the vendor privately, give them time to fix it, then publish. Google Project Zero gives vendors 90 days. Bug bounty programmes pay researchers for reports, an idea Netscape started in 1995, and platforms such as HackerOne now run thousands of them.

What it is
A vulnerability is a weakness in software, hardware, configuration or process that an attacker can use to violate confidentiality, integrity or availability.
Why we need it
Almost every attack needs at least one. Finding, scoring and fixing them before attackers use them is the daily work of most security teams.
How it works
Each flaw gets a CVE identifier, a CWE class and a CVSS score. Teams then rank them by risk, patch or mitigate them in time, and harden systems so that fewer flaws exist in the first place.

Lesson 2 of 9

Anatomy of a real bug: Heartbleed

Heartbleed is the classic example of how one missing line can expose half the internet. TLS has a small heartbeat feature, added in 2012, that lets one side send some bytes and ask for them back to show the connection is alive. The request carries its own length field. OpenSSL versions 1.0.1 to 1.0.1f copied as many bytes as the length field claimed, without checking that the request really contained that many. An attacker could send one byte and claim 65535, and the server would reply with 65535 bytes of whatever lay next in its memory: passwords, session cookies, and sometimes the server’s private key. The attack left no trace in normal logs and could be repeated. The bug was found independently by Neel Mehta at Google and by the Finnish company Codenomicon, and disclosed on 7 April 2014 with the fixed version 1.0.1g. Netcraft estimated that about 17 percent of secure web servers, around half a million, were affected. Many companies had to revoke and reissue their certificates, since nobody could prove their keys had not leaked. The fix was a bounds check: if the claimed length is bigger than what arrived, drop the request. Heartbleed also showed a social problem. OpenSSL, used by most of the internet, was maintained by a handful of volunteers, which led to the Core Infrastructure Initiative funding critical open source projects.

Going deeper: Why memory safe languages matter here

In C, reading past the end of a buffer is not an error, it just returns whatever bytes are there. In Python, Java, Go or Rust, the same mistake raises an error or is caught when the program is compiled, so it becomes a crash instead of a leak. Google and Microsoft have both reported that about 70 percent of their serious security bugs are memory safety bugs, which is why governments now encourage new code to be written in memory safe languages. Lesson 3 of 9

Scoring severity: CVSS v3.1, formula by formula

With tens of thousands of flaws a year, teams need a common way to say how bad each one is. The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, maintained by FIRST, does this with a short vector string and fixed formulas. Version 1 came in 2005, version 3.1 in 2019, and version 4.0 in November 2023, but most databases still show 3.1 scores, so this lesson uses it. The base metrics split into two groups. Exploitability metrics say how easy the flaw is to reach and use: the attack vector (from the network, an adjacent network, a local login or physical access), attack complexity, privileges required and whether a user must do something such as click a link. Impact metrics say how much confidentiality, integrity and availability are lost: high, low or none. Scope says whether the damage can spread beyond the vulnerable component, for example from a browser into the operating system. Each value has a weight chosen by the CVSS team from expert surveys. The formulas combine them into an impact score and an exploitability score, add them, cap the total at 10 and round up to one decimal place. The result maps to a label: Low below 4, Medium below 7, High below 9 and Critical from 9. A CVSS score measures severity, not risk. It does not know whether anyone is attacking the flaw, or whether your system is even reachable, which is why the next lesson adds likelihood.

Going deeper: What changed in CVSS v4.0

Version 4.0 (November 2023) replaces Scope with separate impacts on the vulnerable system and on subsequent systems, adds Attack Requirements, splits user interaction into passive and active, and drops the formula in favour of a lookup table built from expert rankings of grouped vectors. It also renames the score types: CVSS-B for base only, CVSS-BT with threat metrics, CVSS-BE with environmental metrics and CVSS-BTE with all three, so readers know what a number includes. The ideas stay the same: reachability, complexity and impact.

Lesson 4 of 9

What to fix first: EPSS, KEV and the window of exposure

A typical company scanner reports thousands of open vulnerabilities, and more than half of all CVEs score High or Critical, so sorting by CVSS alone leaves far too much at the top. Research by Cyentia and others found that only a few percent of published CVEs are ever exploited in the wild. Three tools help find those few. EPSS, the Exploit Prediction Scoring System from FIRST, first released in 2021, is a machine learning model that estimates the probability that a CVE will be exploited in the next 30 days, using features such as public exploit code, mentions on social media and the type of flaw. It is updated every day. The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, started in November 2021 with Binding Operational Directive 22-01, lists flaws with reliable evidence of active exploitation, and US federal agencies must fix them within two weeks for recent CVEs. The third input is your own context: is the system on the internet, does it hold sensitive data, are there compensating controls. CERT/CC and CISA turned these questions into a decision tree called SSVC, which ends in Track, Track star, Attend or Act. Bruce Schneier described the window of exposure in 2000: the time between the moment a flaw becomes usable and the moment it is fixed. The 2017 SMB flaw shows why the window matters. Microsoft released patch MS17-010 on 14 March, the EternalBlue exploit leaked on 14 April, and WannaCry struck on 12 May. Every machine it hit had had the patch available for 59 days.

Lesson 5 of 9

Patch management, versions and the software supply chain

Knowing what to fix is half the job. The other half is fixing it without breaking things. NIST SP 800-40 Revision 4, published in 2022, describes patching as preventive maintenance: something planned and routine, not an emergency. A good process starts with an inventory of every device and every piece of software, because you cannot patch what you do not know you have. Patches are tested on a small group first, rolled out in waves, and the result is checked by scanning again. When a patch cannot be applied quickly, teams use mitigations: turn off the vulnerable feature, block the port, or add a web application firewall rule. That is what many did for Log4Shell in December 2021, while waiting for the fixed versions. Log4Shell also showed how hard it is to know what is inside your software. Log4j was buried deep inside other libraries, so many companies spent weeks searching. A software bill of materials, or SBOM, lists every component and version in a product, in standard formats such as SPDX and CycloneDX, and US Executive Order 14028 of May 2021 pushed vendors who sell software to the US government to supply them. Version checks look simple but are easy to get wrong: as text, 2.9.1 sorts after 2.15.0, so versions must be compared as numbers. Finally, the supply chain itself can be attacked. In March 2024 Andres Freund noticed SSH logins taking half a second longer and found a backdoor in xz utils, planted over two years by a contributor who had gained the maintainer’s trust. It was caught just before it reached stable Linux releases.

A patch management cycle (after NIST SP 800-40 Rev. 4)
  1. Inventory: know every asset and every software component, with an SBOM where possible.
  2. Monitor: watch vendor advisories, the NVD, CERT-In and the CISA KEV catalog.
  3. Prioritise: combine CVSS, EPSS, KEV and asset importance, and set deadlines.
  4. Test: apply the patch to a small, representative group first.
  5. Deploy in waves, with a rollback plan.
  6. Verify: rescan, and confirm the vulnerable version is gone.
  7. Mitigate what cannot be patched yet: disable the feature, isolate the system, add a filtering rule.

Lesson 6 of 9

Hardening: fewer doors, safer defaults

Patching fixes flaws after they are found. Hardening reduces how many flaws can matter in the first place, by shrinking the attack surface: every service, open port, account and feature that is not needed is a possible way in, so remove it. The principle is called least functionality, and it pairs with least privilege from the access control chapter. In practice teams follow published baselines. The Center for Internet Security publishes free CIS Benchmarks for operating systems, databases, cloud platforms and more, with each setting explained and checked by tools. The US Defense Information Systems Agency publishes similar guides called STIGs. Typical items are simple: disable root login over SSH, use keys instead of passwords, turn off unused services, enable the host firewall, turn on automatic updates and logging, and encrypt disks. Tools such as Lynis, OpenSCAP and the CIS-CAT assessor compare a machine with a baseline and report failures. Vulnerability scanners such as Nessus and the open source OpenVAS look for known flaws from the outside. Configuration drifts over time as people make quick changes, so modern teams define it as code, with Ansible or Terraform, and rebuild systems from a hardened image rather than fixing them by hand. Misconfiguration is now one of the most common causes of breaches, especially in the cloud, where one storage bucket set to public can expose millions of records.

Lesson 7 of 9

CVE, CWE, CVSS, EPSS and KEV compared

These acronyms are easy to confuse, and exams like to test them. CVE names one specific flaw in one product. CWE names the type of mistake behind it, and one CWE covers thousands of CVEs. CVSS scores how severe a flaw is. EPSS estimates how likely it is to be exploited soon. KEV records that it has been exploited. Related, and also often mixed up, are three kinds of testing. A vulnerability scan is automated and broad. A penetration test is a time boxed human attempt to break in and prove impact. A red team exercise copies a real adversary over weeks, to test detection and response as well as prevention.

Lesson 8 of 9

From beginner to expert

A beginner turns on automatic updates and removes software they do not use. A college student can compute a CVSS score by hand, explain why EPSS and KEV change the order of work, and audit a configuration against a baseline. A professional runs a vulnerability management programme for thousands of assets, with SBOMs and deadlines. Researchers study how to predict exploitation, how to find bugs automatically with fuzzing, and how to secure the open source supply chain.
One idea, four depths

Install updates promptly, remove unused apps, and turn on automatic updates and the firewall.

CVE, CWE and NVD, CVSS v3.1 formulas, EPSS and KEV for priority, the window of exposure, version ranges, and CIS style hardening.

Run scanners and triage results, write patch SLAs, build SBOMs, apply SSVC, handle mitigations when patches are late, and report to management with metrics such as mean time to remediate.

Exploit prediction models, coverage guided fuzzing (AFL, libFuzzer, OSS-Fuzz), automated patch generation, supply chain security frameworks such as SLSA and Sigstore, and formal verification of critical code.

Chapter summary

  1. Names: CVE for one flaw (1999), CWE for the kind of mistake (2006), NVD for scores and products. Zero days, coordinated disclosure, bug bounties.
  2. Heartbleed: a trusted length field leaked up to 64 KB per request. The fix was one bounds check. Memory safe languages remove the class.
  3. CVSS v3.1: ISS = 1 − (1 − C)(1 − I)(1 − A), impact 6.42 × ISS, exploitability 8.22 × AV × AC × PR × UI, Roundup. Severity, not risk.
  4. Priority: EPSS for likelihood, KEV for active exploitation, asset exposure, SSVC. WannaCry hit machines unpatched for 59 days.
  5. Patching: inventory, monitor, prioritise, test, deploy, verify, mitigate. SBOMs, numeric version checks, supply chain attacks such as xz.
  6. Hardening: least functionality, CIS Benchmarks and STIGs, SSH keys and no root login, closed ports, configuration as code.
