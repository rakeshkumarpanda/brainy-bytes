Lesson 1 of 7

Why wireless needs its own security

A wired network has a physical boundary: to listen, you must plug in. Radio has no such boundary. Wi-Fi signals pass through walls and into car parks, and anyone with a laptop can record them or transmit their own. So wireless security must do three jobs that cables gave for free: keep outsiders off the network, keep traffic secret from listeners, and let devices know they are talking to the real access point. The history of Wi-Fi security is a lesson in how hard this is. The first scheme, WEP, shipped with the original 802.11 standard in 1997 and was badly broken by 2001. In 2007 the retailer TJX revealed that attackers who first got in through a store’s weak WEP network had stolen about 45 million card numbers. WPA arrived in 2003 as a stopgap, WPA2 in 2004 with AES, and WPA3 in 2018 to fix the offline password guessing that WPA2-Personal allows. Along the way researchers found protocol level attacks such as KRACK in 2017, and Bluetooth has had its own share, such as KNOB in 2019. This chapter walks through each generation, shows the maths that broke WEP, and explains what to configure today.

Source: IEEE 802.11-1997, Borisov, Goldberg and Wagner, Intercepting Mobile Communications (MobiCom 2001), Fluhrer, Mantin and Shamir (SAC 2001), Tews, Weinmann and Pyshkin (2007), IEEE 802.11i-2004, Viehböck, Brute forcing Wi-Fi Protected Setup (2011), Vanhoef and Piessens, KRACK (CCS 2017), Vanhoef and Ronen, Dragonblood (IEEE S&P 2020), RFC 8110, Antonioli, Tippenhauer and Rasmussen, KNOB (USENIX Security 2019), Vanhoef, FragAttacks (2021).

Practise safely and legally
Only capture or attack wireless networks you own, in a lab or with written permission. Recording other people’s Wi-Fi traffic or cracking their passwords is illegal in most countries. The programs here use made up handshakes and packets.
What it is
Wireless security protects radio networks: access control (who may join), confidentiality and integrity of frames, and authentication of the network itself.
Why we need it
Radio reaches anyone nearby, so listening and fake access points need no physical access. Weak Wi-Fi has been the first step of large breaches.
How it works
Modern Wi-Fi uses WPA3: SAE for home networks, 802.1X with EAP-TLS for enterprises, AES-CCMP or GCMP encryption, and protected management frames.

How WEP broke

WEP encrypted each frame with RC4, using a key made of a 24 bit initialisation vector, sent in the clear, followed by the shared secret key. It added a CRC-32 checksum as an integrity check. Almost every design choice failed. First, 24 bits gives only about 16.8 million IVs, and by the birthday paradox a randomly chosen IV is likely to repeat after about 4800 packets, which a busy access point sends in seconds. When an IV repeats, the RC4 keystream repeats, and XORing two ciphertexts cancels the keystream, leaving the XOR of two plaintexts. Network traffic has a lot of predictable content, so this leaks real data. Second, CRC-32 is linear: flipping bits in the message changes the checksum in a predictable way, so an attacker can change a packet and fix the checksum without knowing the key. Nikita Borisov, Ian Goldberg and David Wagner showed these problems in 2001. Third, and worst, Fluhrer, Mantin and Shamir showed in 2001 that RC4 leaks information about its key when the first key bytes are known, as WEP’s IV is, so collecting enough packets reveals the secret key itself. In 2007 the PTW attack needed only about 40000 packets for a 50% chance, under a minute of captured traffic.

Birthday boundExam favourite
P(repeat) ≈ 1 − e−n²/(2N), 50% at n ≈ √(2N ln 2) ≈ 1.177√N
N = 224: n ≈ 1.177 × 4096 = 4823 packets.
Keystream reuseExam favourite
c1 ⊕ c2 = (p1 ⊕ k) ⊕ (p2 ⊕ k) = p1 ⊕ p2
Knowing or guessing p1 gives p2. The program recovers a PIN this way.
CRC linearityExam favourite
CRC(a ⊕ b) = CRC(a) ⊕ CRC(b) ⊕ CRC(0)
So a bit flip Δ in the message is fixed by XORing CRC(Δ) ⊕ CRC(0) into the checksum. A checksum is not a MAC.
In the exam
Lessons from WEP that apply everywhere: never reuse a nonce with a stream cipher, use a keyed MAC (or an AEAD cipher) for integrity, never put known bytes in front of a key for RC4, and have designs reviewed in public before they ship.
Lesson 3 of 7

WPA2: the four way handshake and offline guessing

WPA2, standardised as 802.11i in 2004, replaced RC4 with AES in CCM mode, called CCMP, with a 48 bit packet number that counts up and a proper message integrity code. Its key management has two modes. In Personal mode, also called PSK, every device knows the same pass phrase. Both sides turn it into a 256 bit pairwise master key with PBKDF2, using the network name as salt and 4096 rounds of HMAC-SHA1. Then the four way handshake runs. The access point sends a random ANonce, the device replies with its own SNonce, and both mix the master key, both MAC addresses and both nonces into a pairwise transient key. Part of that key signs each handshake message with a MIC. The weakness is that everything in the handshake except the pass phrase is sent in the clear. An attacker who records messages 1 and 2 can test pass phrases at home: for each guess compute the master key, the transient key and the MIC, and compare. Since 2018, Jens Steube’s PMKID attack even works from the first message alone. So WPA2-Personal is only as strong as its pass phrase. In 2017 Mathy Vanhoef found KRACK, which tricks a device into reinstalling a key already in use, resetting its packet counter so nonces repeat. Patches fixed it in clients and access points.

What it is
WPA2-Personal: PMK = PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA1(pass phrase, SSID, 4096, 256 bits). The four way handshake derives a fresh PTK per session from the PMK, both MACs and two nonces, then installs keys for CCMP.
Why we need it
It gives strong AES encryption and per session keys, and it is still everywhere, so knowing its limits matters.
How it works
PTK = PRF(PMK, "Pairwise key expansion", min(MACs) ‖ max(MACs) ‖ min(nonces) ‖ max(nonces)). The first 16 bytes (KCK) make the MIC on handshake messages.
Pass phrase to PMKExam favourite
PMK = PBKDF2(HMAC-SHA1, pass phrase, SSID, 4096, 32 bytes)
802.11i test vector: "password" on SSID "IEEE" gives f42c6fc5...a12e, which the program reproduces.
Cost of a guessExam favourite
about 2 × 4096 = 8192 HMAC-SHA1 per PMK (two output blocks)
At 109 HMAC per second: about 122000 guesses per second. 8 lowercase letters (268) fall in about 20 days, a 20 character random pass phrase never.
Strength of a random pass phrase
bits = L × log2(alphabet)
20 characters from 62 letters and digits: 20 × 5.95 ≈ 119 bits.

Lesson 4 of 7

WPS, WPA3 and enterprise Wi-Fi

Wi-Fi Protected Setup was meant to make joining easy with an 8 digit PIN. In 2011 Stefan Viehböck showed the design was fatally flawed: the last digit is only a checksum, and the access point tells you separately whether the first four digits and the next three are right. So instead of a hundred million guesses an attacker needs at most eleven thousand, a few hours. Turning WPS PIN mode off is the fix. WPA3, announced in 2018, replaces the pre shared key handshake in Personal mode with SAE, Simultaneous Authentication of Equals, a password authenticated key exchange based on Dan Harkins’ Dragonfly. Each run uses fresh random secrets, so a recording is useless for offline guessing, every guess requires a live attempt, and the resulting key has forward secrecy. WPA3 also requires protected management frames, which stop forged disconnect messages. Vanhoef and Ronen found side channel flaws in early SAE implementations, called Dragonblood, which led to constant time fixes. For organisations, WPA2 and WPA3 Enterprise use 802.1X: each user or device logs in to a RADIUS server through EAP, ideally EAP-TLS with certificates. The critical setting is that devices must check the server certificate. Without that check, an evil twin access point can run a fake RADIUS server and collect password hashes.

The 8th digit is a checksum (107), and the halves are confirmed separately. At 1.5 s per try: about 4.6 hours.
SAE (simplified)
PE = hash to curve(password, MACs), each side sends commit s = r + m and E = −m·PE, then K = r·(speer·PE + Epeer)
Fresh r and m every run: the transcript reveals nothing that can test a password guess offline.

Common mistake
Bluetooth has its own history: BlueBorne (2017) allowed takeover of unpatched devices over the air, and KNOB (2019) let an attacker force the encryption key of Bluetooth Classic down to one byte of entropy, only 256 possibilities. Keep devices updated and turn Bluetooth off when not needed.
Lesson 5 of 7

Wi-Fi security options compared

When an exam or a manager asks which Wi-Fi security to use, the answer depends on who connects. A home needs WPA3-Personal with a strong pass phrase. A company needs Enterprise mode, so each person has their own credentials, leavers can be removed one by one, and a stolen laptop does not reveal a shared key. Public places need encryption without a password, which is what OWE gives. WEP and TKIP should not appear on any network today, and WPA2-Personal is acceptable only with a long random pass phrase.

Lesson 6 of 7

From beginner to expert

A beginner learns to choose WPA3 or WPA2 with a long pass phrase, to turn off WPS, and to be careful on public Wi-Fi. A college student can explain why WEP broke using the birthday paradox, keystream reuse and CRC linearity, describe the four way handshake and why it allows offline guessing, and compute the cost of guessing pass phrases. A masters student studies 802.1X and EAP methods, protected management frames, the security proofs of the four way handshake and why KRACK slipped past them, and SAE as a password authenticated key exchange. Researchers fuzz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth stacks, find design flaws like FragAttacks, analyse side channels in SAE implementations, and model protocols formally with tools such as Tamarin.
One idea, four depths

Use WPA3 or WPA2 with a long pass phrase, turn off WPS, update your router, and treat public Wi-Fi as untrusted.

Chapter summary

  1. Radio has no walls: wireless security must control access, protect frames and authenticate the network.
  2. WEP: 24 bit IV repeats after about 4823 packets (birthday), keystream reuse leaks p1 ⊕ p2, CRC-32 is linear, FMS and PTW recover the key.
  3. WPA2: AES-CCMP. PMK = PBKDF2(pass phrase, SSID, 4096). Four way handshake gives PTK. A recording allows offline guessing (PMKID too).
  4. Attacks: WPS halves (11000 guesses), KRACK nonce reuse (2017), FragAttacks (2021), evil twins.
  5. WPA3: SAE (Dragonfly) stops offline guessing and gives forward secrecy. PMF required. Dragonblood fixed by constant time code.
  6. Enterprise: 802.1X with EAP-TLS, and always validate the server certificate. OWE for open networks.
  7. Bluetooth: BlueBorne, KNOB (1 byte keys), BIAS. Update and disable when unused.
