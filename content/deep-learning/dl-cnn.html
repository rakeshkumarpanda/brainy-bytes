Convolution: a small filter slid over the image

Source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville (2016) sections 9.1 to 9.3, and the Stanford CS231n notes on convolutional networks.

What it is
A convolutional layer slides a small grid of weights, the filter or kernel, across the image. At each position it multiplies the filter by the patch under it and adds the results, giving one number of the output feature map. A layer has many filters, each learning to detect one pattern such as an edge, a corner or a texture.
Why we need it
An image has thousands of pixels, so a dense layer would need millions of weights and would have to learn the same pattern separately at every position. A convolution reuses the same few weights everywhere (weight sharing) and looks only at nearby pixels (local connections). It needs far fewer weights and finds a pattern wherever it appears.
How it works
Choose the filter size k (3 is the usual choice), the number of filters, the stride s (how far the filter moves) and the padding p (a border of zeros). The output size is ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1. With k = 3, p = 1 and s = 1 the size stays the same.

Channels, pooling and the receptive field

Source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville (2016) section 9.3, Luo and colleagues, Understanding the effective receptive field (NeurIPS 2016), and Yu and Koltun (ICLR 2016).

What it is
A colour image has 3 channels (red, green, blue), and each layer outputs one channel per filter, so a filter really spans k × k × Cin numbers. Max pooling keeps the largest value in each small window, usually 2 × 2, halving the height and width. The receptive field of a unit is the patch of the input image that can affect it.
Why we need it
Pooling (or a convolution with stride 2) shrinks the maps so later layers are cheaper and each unit sees a larger area. It also makes the output change less when the object moves by a pixel or two. A large receptive field lets the top layers recognise whole objects from the small edges found by the bottom layers.
How it works
Stack 3 × 3 convolutions and downsample by 2 every few layers while doubling the number of channels: this is the shape of VGG and ResNet. Check that the receptive field of the last layer covers the objects you need to see.

Training a CNN on handwritten digits

Source: LeCun and colleagues (1998), the scikit-learn digits dataset (UCI optical recognition of handwritten digits), and the CS231n notes on backpropagation through convolution and pooling.

What it is
A complete CNN chains convolution, ReLU and pooling blocks, then flattens the maps and ends with a dense layer and softmax. It is trained with backpropagation like any network. The gradient of a convolution is itself a convolution, and the gradient of max pooling goes only to the position that held the maximum.
Why we need it
Writing one small CNN by hand shows that nothing is magic: the same chain rule from chapter 1.2 handles shared weights by adding up the gradient from every position where the filter was used.
How it works
Here the network is conv 3 × 3 with 8 filters and padding 1, ReLU, 2 × 2 max pooling, then a dense layer to 10 classes. The convolution is done by gathering every 3 × 3 patch into a row and multiplying by the filter matrix, the trick (called im2col) that real libraries use too.

Famous architectures and residual connections

Source: LeCun and colleagues (1998), Krizhevsky and colleagues (2012), Simonyan and Zisserman (2015), Szegedy and colleagues, GoogLeNet (CVPR 2015), He and colleagues (CVPR 2016), Tan and Le (ICML 2019), and Dosovitskiy and colleagues (ICLR 2021).

What it is
A residual block adds its input to its output: hout = h + F(h). The block only has to learn the change F, and if F is zero the block passes its input unchanged. ResNet stacks dozens or hundreds of these blocks.
Why we need it
Before ResNet, adding layers beyond about 20 made accuracy worse, even on the training set, because the gradient faded on its way down. The skip path gives the gradient a direct route to every layer, so networks with 152 layers trained well and won ImageNet 2015.
How it works
Use a pretrained ResNet or EfficientNet as a default image model. For very large datasets, vision transformers (chapter 2.3) now match or beat CNNs. For small datasets and phones, CNNs remain strong and efficient.

With the same weights and residual connections it is about 10.5 times the output gradient, because the skip path carries it straight through. Real ResNets also use batch normalisation to keep the sizes steady.

Transfer learning and data augmentation

Source: Yosinski and colleagues, How transferable are features in deep neural networks? (NeurIPS 2014), Shorten and Khoshgoftaar, A survey on image data augmentation (2019), and the PyTorch torchvision documentation.

What it is
Transfer learning starts from a network already trained on a large dataset such as ImageNet and adapts it to your task. Feature extraction freezes the pretrained layers and trains only a new final layer. Fine tuning also updates some or all pretrained layers with a small learning rate. Data augmentation creates new training images by flipping, cropping, rotating or recolouring the ones you have.
Why we need it
Early layers learn general features such as edges and textures that are useful for almost any image task. Reusing them means you can reach good accuracy with hundreds of images instead of millions, in minutes instead of days. Augmentation teaches the network that a cat flipped left to right is still a cat, which reduces overfitting.
How it works
With little data, freeze the backbone and train the head. With more data, unfreeze the top blocks and fine tune with a learning rate about 10 times smaller. Only use augmentations that keep the label true: flipping a 6 upside down makes a 9.
Your dataSimilar to ImageNetDifferent from ImageNet

Real world scenario: Classifying plant diseases with 800 photos
A farm cooperative has 800 labelled leaf photos in 4 classes. Training a CNN from scratch overfits and reaches about 60% validation accuracy. A ResNet 50 pretrained on ImageNet, with its layers frozen and a new 4 class head, reaches about 90% in a few minutes. Adding random flips, crops and colour changes, then fine tuning the last block with a learning rate of 1e−4, raises it further. The numbers here are illustrative, a typical pattern rather than a measured result.

Worked example: shapes and weights through a small CNN on a 32 × 32 × 3 image
  1. Conv 3 × 3, 16 filters, padding 1, stride 1: the size stays 32.
  2. (32 + 2 − 3)/1 + 1 = 32 ⇒ 32 × 32 × 16,   weights (9 × 3 + 1) × 16 = 448
  3. Max pool 2 × 2 halves the size and has no weights.
  4. 16 × 16 × 16
  5. Conv 3 × 3, 32 filters, padding 1, then pool again.
  6. weights (9 × 16 + 1) × 32 = 4,640,   output 8 × 8 × 32 = 2,048 numbers
  7. A dense layer from 2,048 numbers to 10 classes has 20,490 weights, so the final dense layer holds most of the weights. Global average pooling would cut it to 32 × 10 + 10 = 330.
Going deeper: Equivariance, inductive bias and what CNNs see

A convolution is equivariant to translation: shift the input and the feature map shifts the same way. Pooling then adds a little invariance. These built in assumptions, called an inductive bias, are why CNNs learn from far less data than a dense network. In practice, strided layers and padding break exact equivariance, so small shifts can change predictions (Azulay and Weiss 2019, Zhang 2019, who proposed blurring before downsampling). Visualisation studies (Zeiler and Fergus 2014, Olah and colleagues 2017) show early filters detecting edges and colours, middle layers textures and parts, and late layers whole objects. Group equivariant CNNs (Cohen and Welling 2016) extend weight sharing to rotations and reflections. Vision transformers drop most of this bias and recover it from data, which is why they need more of it.

Chapter summary

  1. A convolution slides a small filter over the image, multiplying and adding. Weight sharing and local connections need far fewer weights than a dense layer.
  2. Output size ⌊(n + 2p − k)/s⌋ + 1. Weights (k²Cin + 1)Cout, whatever the image size.
  3. Pooling and strides shrink the maps and grow the receptive field: r ← r + (k − 1)dj, j ← js.
  4. Residual connections h + F(h) let gradients reach early layers, so very deep networks train. LeNet, AlexNet, VGG, ResNet and EfficientNet mark the history.
  5. With little data, use transfer learning from a pretrained network plus data augmentation.
