Lesson 1 of 10

The energy view: low energy means likely

Source: LeCun and colleagues, A tutorial on energy-based learning (2006), and Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski (1985).

What it is
An energy based model gives every possible state of its units a number called the energy. States the model likes get low energy. The Boltzmann distribution turns energies into probabilities: the lower the energy, the more likely the state. The temperature T controls how strongly it prefers low energy.
Why we need it
Energy is a natural way to say which combinations of features belong together, without having to say which feature causes which. The same idea links Hopfield memories, Boltzmann machines, RBMs and, through the score, the diffusion models of chapter 3.3.
How it works
Write an energy function with weights between units. Learning lowers the energy of training data and raises the energy of everything else. Inference and generation look for low energy states, by settling (Hopfield) or by random sampling (Boltzmann machines).
Boltzmann distributionExam favourite
p(s) = e−E(s)/T / Z
Low energy, high probability. T is the temperature, usually 1.
Partition functionExam favourite
Z = Σall states s e−E(s)/T
Makes the probabilities add up to 1. Summing over 2n states is what makes these models hard.
Energy of binary units
E(s) = −½ Σi,j wij si sj − Σi bi si
Symmetric weights wij = wji, no self connections.

Hopfield networks: memories as valleys

Source: Hopfield (1982), and Amit, Gutfreund and Sompolinsky (1985).

What it is
A Hopfield network is a set of ±1 units, each connected to every other unit with a symmetric weight. Patterns are stored with the Hebb rule: units that agree in a pattern get a positive weight. To recall, start from a damaged pattern and let each unit in turn take the sign of its weighted input.
Why we need it
It is a content addressable memory: give part of a memory and get the whole memory back, the way a few notes bring back a whole song. Hopfield showed that each update can only lower the energy, so the network always settles, and stored patterns sit at the bottoms of energy valleys.
How it works
Build W from the patterns, set the diagonal to 0, then update units one at a time (asynchronously) until no unit changes. Keep the number of random patterns below about 0.14 times the number of units.

In the exam
With 6 of 25 pixels flipped, one sweep of updates rebuilt the letter T exactly and the energy fell from −2.24 to −11.20. In 100 units, all 10 of 10 random patterns came back from 10 flipped pixels, 10 of 14 at the 0.14 N limit, and none of 30.
Hebb ruleExam favourite
wij = (1/N) Σμ piμ pjμ, wii = 0
One pass over the patterns, no gradient descent.
Update ruleExam favourite
si ← sign( Σj wij sj )
One unit at a time.
Energy change of a flipExam favourite
ΔE = 2 siold hi, with hi = Σj wij sj
A unit flips only when si and hi disagree, so ΔE ≤ 0.
Capacity
about 0.138 N random patterns
Beyond it, recall breaks down and spurious memories take over.
Common mistake
Spurious states are valleys that are no stored pattern, such as the negative of a pattern or a mix of three patterns. The animation shows one when too many pixels are flipped.
Lesson 3 of 10

Boltzmann machines: Hopfield with randomness and hidden units

Source: Ackley, Hinton and Sejnowski, A learning algorithm for Boltzmann machines (1985).

What it is
A Boltzmann machine has binary units (0 or 1) with symmetric weights like a Hopfield network, but each unit switches on at random with probability sigmoid of its input. Run long enough, it visits every state with its Boltzmann probability. It can also have hidden units that are not part of the data.
Why we need it
The randomness lets it escape bad valleys and makes it a full probability model, so it can learn the distribution of the data and generate new samples. Hidden units let it capture patterns that no single pixel shows.
How it works
Sample with Gibbs sampling: update one unit at a time from its conditional probability. Learn by making the model’s statistics match the data’s: raise wij when units i and j are on together more often in the data than in the model’s own samples.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 boltzmann_exact.py.
In the exam
With 3 units there are only 8 states, so Z = 10.932 can be summed exactly. The two lowest energy states (energy −1) each get p = 0.249. At T = 0.5 they get 0.339 and at T = 4 only 0.157, so a high temperature flattens the distribution. Gibbs sampling matched every exact probability within 0.005.
Unit conditionalExam favourite
p(si = 1 | rest) = σ( (Σj wij sj + bi) / T )
Each Gibbs step uses this. As T → 0 it becomes the Hopfield sign rule.
Learning ruleExam favourite
Δwij = η ( ⟨si sj⟩data − ⟨si sj⟩model )
The exact gradient of the log likelihood.
Remember
The model term needs samples from the model, and in a fully connected machine every Gibbs chain is slow to mix. That is why general Boltzmann machines are rarely trained, and why the restricted version won.
Lesson 4 of 10

Restricted Boltzmann machines and contrastive divergence

Source: Smolensky (1986), Hinton, Training products of experts by minimizing contrastive divergence (2002), and Hinton, A practical guide to training RBMs (2010).

What it is
A restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) has one visible layer and one hidden layer, with connections only between the layers. Given the visible units, the hidden units are independent of each other, and the other way round, so a whole layer is sampled in one matrix product. Contrastive divergence (CD-k) replaces the slow model statistics with those after only k steps of Gibbs sampling started at the data.
Why we need it
The restriction makes Gibbs sampling fast and lets the hidden units be summed out in closed form (the free energy). CD-1 made RBMs practical to train, and they became the building block of deep belief networks and early recommender systems.
How it works
For each batch: compute p(h | v0) from the data, sample h, reconstruct v1, compute p(h | v1), and move W by the difference of the two outer products. More Gibbs steps (CD-10) or persistent chains (PCD) give a better gradient at more cost.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 rbm_cd.py.
In the exam
On 4 by 4 bars and stripes the best possible average log likelihood is ln(1/30) = −3.401. CD-10 reached −3.886. CD-1 peaked at −4.863 and then fell back to −5.696, because CD-1 does not follow the true likelihood gradient. Still, 93 percent of images generated by the CD-1 model were valid, but only 22 of the 30 kinds appeared.
RBM energyExam favourite
E(v, h) = −a·v − b·h − vT W h
Only visible to hidden weights.
ConditionalsExam favourite
p(hj = 1 | v) = σ(bj + v·W:j), p(vi = 1 | h) = σ(ai + Wi:·h)
A whole layer at once.
Free energyExam favourite
F(v) = −a·v − Σj ln(1 + ebj + v·W:j), p(v) = e−F(v) / Z
The hidden units summed out exactly.
CD-k update
ΔW = η ( v0 p(h | v0)T − vk p(h | vk)T )
Data statistics minus statistics after k Gibbs steps.
Going deeper: Variants of the RBM

Persistent CD (Tieleman 2008) keeps the negative chains running between updates instead of restarting them at the data, which follows the likelihood more closely. Gaussian-Bernoulli RBMs use real valued visible units for data such as audio. RBMs for collaborative filtering (Salakhutdinov, Mnih and Hinton 2007) were part of the winning blend in the Netflix Prize, with one visible unit per rated movie. Evaluating Z for big RBMs needs estimates such as annealed importance sampling.

Deep belief networks and greedy pretraining

Source: Hinton, Osindero and Teh, A fast learning algorithm for deep belief nets (2006), and Salakhutdinov and Hinton, Deep Boltzmann machines (2009).

Greedy layer wise training of a deep belief network
  1. Train an RBM on the data, without labels.
  2. Use its hidden probabilities p(h | v) as the data for a second RBM, and train that one.
  3. Repeat for as many layers as you want. Each new layer models the features of the layer below.
  4. Turn the stack into a deep network, add an output layer, and fine tune all the weights with backpropagation on the labelled data.
What it is
A deep belief network (DBN) is a stack of RBMs trained one layer at a time. Used as a generative model, its top two layers form an RBM and the lower layers generate downwards. Used for classification, the RBM weights initialise a deep network that is then fine tuned.
Why we need it
In 2006 deep networks with sigmoid units were very hard to train from random weights, because gradients faded on the way down. Pretraining placed the weights in a good region first. This result restarted interest in deep learning. A deep Boltzmann machine is different: all its layers are trained together as one undirected model.
How it works
Pretrain on all the data you have, labelled or not, then fine tune on the labelled part. Today ReLU units, good initialisation, normalisation and big labelled datasets usually make pretraining by RBMs unnecessary, which the program shows too.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 dbn_digits.py.
In the exam
With only 100 labels, the deep sigmoid network started from the pretrained RBMs scored 0.876. From tiny random weights it stalled at 0.239, the vanishing gradient problem. Sensibly scaled random weights reached 0.798. Plain logistic regression on the pixels still scored 0.906 on these easy 8 by 8 digits, so pretraining helps deep sigmoid networks but is no magic.

Bottom line: The score in diffusion models is −∇E(x) for an energy E = −ln p(x), so learning the score is learning the slope of an energy landscape without ever computing Z.

Going deeper: Hopfield meets attention

Modern Hopfield networks replace the quadratic energy with one built from a log sum exp of similarities. Their update rule, new state = softmax(β XT ξ) applied to the stored patterns X, is the attention formula of chapter 2.3 with the stored patterns as keys and values. They can store exponentially many patterns. In 2024 John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton received the Nobel Prize in Physics for these foundational discoveries that enable machine learning with neural networks.

The maths, all in one place

Boltzmann distributionExam favourite
p(s) = e−E(s)/T / Z, Z = Σs e−E(s)/T
Ratio of two states: p(a)/p(b) = e(E(b) − E(a))/T.
HopfieldExam favourite
W = (1/N) Σ p pT with zero diagonal, si ← sign(hi), ΔE = 2 siold hi ≤ 0
Capacity about 0.138 N.
Boltzmann machine gradientExam favourite
∂ ln p(v) / ∂wij = ⟨sisj⟩data − ⟨sisj⟩model
Positive phase minus negative phase.
RBMExam favourite
p(hj=1|v) = σ(bj + v·W:j), F(v) = −a·v − Σj ln(1 + ebj + v·W:j)
Gradient: −∂F/∂θ on data minus the same on model samples.
Contrastive divergence
ΔW = η ( v0 p(h|v0)T − vk p(h|vk)T )
k = 1 is cheap but biased.
Link to scores
∇x ln p(x) = −∇x E(x) / T
Z does not depend on x, so it disappears from the gradient.
In the exam
Why Z disappears from the score: ln p(x) = −E(x)/T − ln Z, and ln Z is the same number for every x, so its gradient with respect to x is zero.

Real world scenario: Recommending films with an RBM

A streaming service has millions of users who rated only a few films each. An RBM gets one visible unit per film and a few hundred hidden units. Each user is one training example, with only the rated films clamped. After CD training, the hidden units capture tastes such as “likes slow science fiction”. To recommend, clamp a user’s ratings, compute the hidden probabilities, and read the reconstruction probabilities of the films they have not seen. This is the approach that Salakhutdinov, Mnih and Hinton used in the Netflix Prize.

Energy models work on binary states that change one unit at a time. These problems use the same skills: updating a grid from its neighbours at once, counting and flipping the on units of a state packed into an integer, and walking a path of states that differ in one bit.

289Game of LifeMediumUpdate every cell from its neighbours at the same time, encoding old and new state in one numberA Hopfield update looks at its neighbours and sets a new state. Game of Life updates all cells at once (synchronously), so the new value must not be seen before every cell has read the old one. Store old state in bit 0 and new state in bit 1, then shift. O(mn) time, O(1) extra space.
191Number of 1 BitsEasyClear the lowest set bit with n & (n - 1) until n is 0A binary state vector of units can be packed into one integer. Counting its on units is counting 1 bits. n & (n minus 1) removes exactly one set bit, so the loop runs once per on unit.
338Counting BitsEasyReuse smaller answers: bits(i) = bits(i >> 1) + (i & 1)Enumerating every state of a small Boltzmann machine walks through all integers 0 to 2^n minus 1. The number of on units in i equals that of i with its last bit removed, plus that last bit. O(n) time.
476Number ComplementEasyFlip every bit below the highest set bit with a mask of onesFlipping every unit of a binary state, like the spurious negative of a Hopfield memory. Build a mask with as many ones as n has bits and xor with it.
1611Minimum One Bit Operations to Make Integers ZeroHardThe allowed moves walk the Gray code, so the answer is the inverse Gray code of nEach allowed operation changes one bit, so the states form a path in which neighbours differ in one unit, exactly like a single unit Gibbs or Hopfield update. The order of that path is the Gray code. The number of steps from n to 0 is the position of n in the Gray code, found by xoring all right shifts of n. O(log n).

Chapter summary

  1. An energy based model gives each state an energy, and p(s) = e−E/T/Z. The partition function Z sums over every state, which is what makes these models hard.
  2. A Hopfield network stores patterns with the Hebb rule and recalls them by lowering energy. It rebuilt T from 6 flipped pixels, and holds about 0.14 N random patterns.
  3. A Boltzmann machine adds randomness and hidden units. Gibbs sampling matched the exact probabilities within 0.005, and learning compares data and model statistics.
  4. An RBM links only visible to hidden units and trains with contrastive divergence. CD-10 reached −3.886 against the best −3.401, CD-1 drifted to −5.696.
  5. A deep belief network stacks RBMs greedily. Pretraining lifted a deep sigmoid network from 0.239 to 0.876. Modern Hopfield networks are attention, and diffusion scores are energy slopes.
