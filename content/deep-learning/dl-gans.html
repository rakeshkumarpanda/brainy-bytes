Lesson 1 of 9

The GAN game: a generator against a discriminator

Source: Goodfellow and colleagues, Generative adversarial nets (NeurIPS 2014), and Goodfellow, NIPS 2016 tutorial on generative adversarial networks.

What it is
A generative adversarial network (GAN) trains two networks against each other. The generator G turns random noise z into a fake example G(z). The discriminator D looks at an example and outputs the probability that it is real. D is trained to tell real from fake, and G is trained to fool D.
Why we need it
A GAN learns to generate data without ever writing down a probability formula for it. All it needs is samples of real data and a network that can judge them. This gave the first sharp, realistic generated faces and images, and the same idea of a learned critic is still used in image restoration, super resolution and the decoders of modern image generators.
How it works
Alternate two steps. First, update D with ordinary binary cross entropy: real examples have label 1 and generated examples have label 0. Then, update G so that D gives its fakes higher scores, passing the gradient through D into G while D stays fixed. Repeat for many thousands of steps.

Real world scenario: A forger and an inspector
A new banknote inspector and a forger start on the same day. At first the forger draws crude notes and the inspector rejects them by their colour. The forger fixes the colour, so the inspector starts checking the watermark. Each time one gets better, the other has to improve. In a GAN the forger is the generator and the inspector is the discriminator, and both are networks updated by gradient descent.
Lesson 2 of 9

The best discriminator, the right loss, and mode collapse

Source: Goodfellow and colleagues (2014) section 4, Goodfellow (2016) section 3.2.3, Salimans and colleagues, Improved techniques for training GANs (NeurIPS 2016), and Karras and colleagues, ProGAN (ICLR 2018).

What it is
For a fixed generator, the best discriminator is D*(x) = pdata(x) / (pdata(x) + pg(x)). With that D, the GAN value equals −ln 4 + 2 · JS(pdata, pg), so the generator is really lowering the Jensen Shannon divergence between the real and the generated distribution. The non saturating generator loss −ln D(G(z)) replaces ln(1 − D(G(z))) because it gives strong gradients early in training. Mode collapse is when the generator produces only a few kinds of output.
Why we need it
This explains the two classic failures. Early on, D rejects every fake, D(G(z)) is near 0, and ln(1 − D) is almost flat there, so the original loss gives the generator nearly no signal. Later, the generator can score well by producing one convincing output again and again, because each fake is judged on its own and nothing rewards variety.
How it works
Use the non saturating loss. Give D information about the whole batch, for example the minibatch standard deviation layer of ProGAN and StyleGAN, or minibatch discrimination (Salimans and colleagues 2016). Watch generated samples during training, not only the losses, because a GAN loss does not steadily fall the way a classifier loss does.

Wasserstein GANs and other stable losses

Source: Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, Wasserstein GAN (ICML 2017), Gulrajani and colleagues, Improved training of Wasserstein GANs (NeurIPS 2017), Miyato and colleagues, Spectral normalization (ICLR 2018), and Mao and colleagues, Least squares GANs (ICCV 2017).

What it is
The Wasserstein GAN (WGAN) replaces the discriminator with a critic f that outputs any real number, not a probability. The critic maximises E[f(real)] − E[f(fake)], and the generator maximises E[f(fake)]. If f is kept smooth (1 Lipschitz), this difference estimates the Wasserstein distance, also called the earth mover’s distance: the least total work needed to move one pile of probability onto the other.
Why we need it
Real images lie on thin surfaces in pixel space, so the real and generated distributions often do not overlap at all. Then the JS divergence is stuck at ln 2 and gives no direction to improve, while the Wasserstein distance still shrinks as the fakes move closer. The WGAN critic value also tracks sample quality, which makes training easier to monitor.
How it works
Keep the critic smooth with a gradient penalty (WGAN-GP), which adds λ(||∇f(x̂)|| − 1)² at points x̂ between real and fake samples, or with spectral normalisation of every weight matrix. The original weight clipping works but is crude. Train the critic several times per generator step, often 5.

DCGAN, conditional GANs, StyleGAN, and measuring quality

Source: Radford, Metz and Chintala, DCGAN (ICLR 2016), Mirza and Osindero, Conditional GANs (2014), Isola and colleagues, pix2pix (CVPR 2017), Zhu and colleagues, CycleGAN (ICCV 2017), Karras and colleagues, StyleGAN (CVPR 2019), and Heusel and colleagues, GANs trained by a two time scale update rule converge to a local Nash equilibrium (NeurIPS 2017).

What it is
DCGAN uses transposed convolutions in the generator and strided convolutions in the discriminator, with batch normalisation. A conditional GAN feeds a label or an input image to both networks, so you can ask for a specific class or translate one image into another (pix2pix with paired examples, CycleGAN with unpaired ones). StyleGAN maps z to a style vector that controls each resolution of the image. The Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) measures quality and variety together.
Why we need it
Plain GANs give random samples. Conditioning gives control, which is what applications need: turn a sketch into a photo, a day scene into night, a map into a satellite view. A number like FID is needed because people cannot inspect millions of samples, and the GAN loss itself does not say how good the images are.
How it works
To compute FID, pass real and generated images through a pretrained Inception network, fit a Gaussian (mean and covariance) to each set of features, and compute the Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians. Lower is better. Use at least 10,000 samples, because FID is biased for small sets, and report it with precision and recall when variety matters.

Worked example: why the best discriminator is pdata / (pdata + pg)
  1. Write the value as one integral over x, using pg for the distribution of G(z).
  2. V = ∫ [ pdata(x) ln D(x) + pg(x) ln(1 − D(x)) ] dx
  3. At each x, D(x) is a free number y in (0, 1). Maximise a ln y + b ln(1 − y) with a = pdata(x) and b = pg(x).
  4. d/dy: a / y − b / (1 − y) = 0   so   y = a / (a + b)
  5. Put D* back into V. Each log term becomes a KL divergence to the mixture m = ½(pdata + pg), plus a constant.
  6. V(D*, G) = −ln 4 + KL(pdata || m) + KL(pg || m) = −ln 4 + 2 JS(pdata || pg)
  7. JS is 0 only when the two distributions are equal, so the best generator copies the data exactly and then D* = ½ everywhere.
Going deeper: Game theory, convergence and what GANs really learn

A GAN is a two player game, and gradient descent on both players looks for a Nash equilibrium, not a minimum. Simple games such as minx maxy xy make plain simultaneous gradient steps circle around the equilibrium or spiral away, which is the oscillation seen in practice. Mescheder, Geiger and Nowozin (ICML 2018) showed that unregularised GANs need not converge and that a gradient penalty on real data (R1) makes training locally convergent. Heusel and colleagues (2017) proved convergence for two time scale updates (TTUR). Arora and colleagues (ICML 2017) showed that a discriminator with limited capacity cannot detect a generator that memorised a small set of samples, so a GAN can win the game without learning the full distribution, which the birthday paradox test measures. f-GANs (Nowozin and colleagues 2016) show that changing the loss lets a GAN minimise any f divergence, with the original GAN as the JS case.

Chapter summary

  1. A GAN trains a generator to turn noise into fakes and a discriminator to tell real from fake, alternating their updates in a minimax game.
  2. The best discriminator is pdata / (pdata + pg), and then the generator lowers the JS divergence. The ideal end point is pg = pdata with D = ½.
  3. Use the non saturating loss −ln D(G(z)), because ln(1 − D) gives almost no gradient while the fakes are bad. Fight mode collapse with batch level features and stable losses.
  4. The Wasserstein GAN critic estimates the earth mover’s distance, which keeps giving gradients when the distributions do not overlap. Keep the critic smooth with a gradient penalty or spectral normalisation.
  5. Conditional GANs (pix2pix, CycleGAN) give control, StyleGAN gives photorealistic faces, and FID compares feature means and covariances to score quality and variety.
