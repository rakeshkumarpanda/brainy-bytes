Lesson 1 of 10

Optimisers: SGD, momentum, RMSprop and Adam

Source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville (2016) sections 8.3 and 8.5, Polyak (1964), Hinton’s Coursera lecture 6e, and Kingma and Ba, Adam (ICLR 2015).

What it is
An optimiser is the rule that turns gradients into weight updates. Plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps against the gradient of a mini batch. Momentum keeps a running velocity so steps build up along consistent directions. RMSprop divides each weight’s step by a running size of its own gradients. Adam combines momentum with RMSprop scaling and corrects both averages for their start at zero.
Why we need it
Real loss surfaces have long, narrow valleys: some directions are far more curved than others. Plain SGD must keep its learning rate below the limit set by the steepest direction, so it crawls along the shallow ones. Momentum and adaptive scaling make progress in every direction at once, which often means training in fewer steps and with less tuning.
How it works
Start with Adam (learning rate about 0.001) or AdamW for most networks, because it is forgiving. For large vision models, SGD with momentum 0.9 and a good schedule often generalises a little better. Whatever you pick, the learning rate is still the most important setting to tune.

On the valley f = ½(x² + 100y²), plain SGD is stable only below learning rate 2/100 = 0.02. Mini batches and learning rate schedules

Source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville (2016) section 8.1.3, Loshchilov and Hutter, SGDR (ICLR 2017), Goyal and colleagues, Accurate, large minibatch SGD (2017), and Smith, Cyclical learning rates (WACV 2017).

What it is
A mini batch is the small group of examples used for one gradient step. One pass over all the data is an epoch. A learning rate schedule changes the learning rate during training: step decay divides it by 10 at set points, cosine decay lowers it smoothly to 0, and warm up raises it gently over the first few hundred steps.
Why we need it
A mini batch gradient is a noisy estimate of the full gradient. A large learning rate makes fast early progress but the noise keeps the weights bouncing around the minimum. Lowering the rate later lets them settle. Warm up protects the first steps, when the weights are random and Adam’s averages are still unreliable.
How it works
Choose the largest batch that fits in memory and gives good results, often 32 to 512. When you multiply the batch size by k, multiply the learning rate by about k too (the linear scaling rule), with warm up. Use cosine decay with a short warm up as a strong default.

Batch normalisation and layer normalisation

Source: Ioffe and Szegedy, Batch normalization (ICML 2015), Ba, Kiros and Hinton, Layer normalization (2016), and Santurkar and colleagues, How does batch normalization help optimization? (NeurIPS 2018).

What it is
Batch normalisation (BN) takes each feature of a layer over the current mini batch, subtracts its mean and divides by its standard deviation, then applies a learned scale γ and shift β. At test time it uses running averages collected during training. Layer normalisation (LN) does the same across the features of one example instead of across the batch.
Why we need it
Normalisation keeps the signal in each layer at a steady size whatever the weights do, so deep networks train faster, tolerate larger learning rates and depend less on careful initialisation. BN is standard in convolutional networks. LN is standard in transformers and recurrent networks, because it works with any batch size and with sequences.
How it works
Place the normalisation layer just before or just after the activation, following the architecture you copy. Remember to switch the model to evaluation mode (model.eval() in PyTorch) at test time, otherwise BN uses the statistics of the test batch.

Regularisation: weight decay, dropout and early stopping

Source: Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville (2016) chapter 7, Srivastava and colleagues, Dropout (JMLR 2014), Loshchilov and Hutter, Decoupled weight decay regularization (ICLR 2019), and Prechelt, Early stopping, but when? (1998).

What it is
Regularisation is anything that stops a model fitting the noise in its training data. Weight decay (L2) pulls every weight a little towards zero each step. Dropout switches off each hidden unit at random during training, with probability 1 − keep, so no unit can rely on another. Early stopping watches the validation error and keeps the weights from the epoch where it was lowest.
Why we need it
Neural networks have far more weights than training examples, so they can memorise the training set. Then training error keeps falling while validation error rises: overfitting. Regularisation trades a little training error for a model that does better on new data.
How it works
Use weight decay almost always (AdamW with about 0.01 to 0.1). Add dropout of 0.1 to 0.5 in large fully connected layers or transformers. Always keep a validation set and save the best checkpoint, which gives early stopping for free. More data and data augmentation are the strongest regularisers of all.

In the exam
A network with two layers of 64 tanh units, trained on 16 noisy points of sin(x), reaches a training error of 0.0026 but a validation error of 0.596. Weight decay 0.01 gives a validation error of 0.111, dropout with keep 0.8 gives 0.132, and early stopping halts at epoch 447 with 0.118. The noise alone gives about 0.09, so these are close to the best possible.

A practical recipe for training a network

Source: Karpathy, A recipe for training neural networks (2019), Smith, A disciplined approach to neural network hyper parameters (2018), and Google’s Deep learning tuning playbook (2023).

What it is
Training a network well is mostly a careful process, not one clever trick. The recipe is: look at the data, get a simple baseline working end to end, make the model overfit a small sample, then regularise and tune.
Why we need it
Neural network bugs rarely crash. A wrong label shuffle, a missing model.eval() or a learning rate ten times too large just gives a model that is quietly worse. Checking each stage separately finds these silent failures early.
How it works
Follow the steps in the table in order and change one thing at a time. Plot training and validation loss for every run, and keep a log of each setting you tried.

Real world scenario: Debugging a network that will not learn
A student’s image classifier for 10 classes stays at 10% accuracy. The first loss is 2.30, which matches ln 10, so the start is fine. Trying to overfit 8 images fails too, which points to a bug rather than a hard problem. The student finds that the images were normalised but the labels were shuffled separately. After fixing it, the 8 images reach a loss near 0 within 100 steps, and the full model trains normally.

Worked example: why plain SGD has a speed limit on f = ½(x² + 100y²)
  1. The gradient is (x, 100y), so one SGD step multiplies x by (1 − η) and y by (1 − 100η).
  2. yt = (1 − 100η)t y0
  3. This shrinks only if |1 − 100η| < 1, that is η < 2/100 = 0.02.
  4. η = 0.019:   xt = 0.981t x0,   0.98160 ≈ 0.316
  5. So after 60 steps x is still about a third of its start, and the loss ½(4 × 0.316)² ≈ 0.80 matches the program. The steep direction limits the rate and the shallow direction pays for it.
Going deeper: Sharp and flat minima, and why the noise of small batches can help

Many different weight settings give near zero training loss in a large network. Some sit in narrow, sharp valleys and some in wide, flat ones. Keskar and colleagues (ICLR 2017) found that very large batches tend to end in sharper minima that generalise worse, and that the noise of small batch SGD helps find flatter ones. The ratio of learning rate to batch size acts like a temperature for this noise, which is one reason behind the linear scaling rule. Methods such as sharpness aware minimisation (Foret and colleagues, ICLR 2021) seek flat minima on purpose by minimising the worst loss in a small ball around the weights. Second order methods that use curvature directly, such as K FAC and Shampoo, can cut the number of steps further but cost more per step.

Lesson 10 of 10

Chapter summary

  1. SGD is limited by the steepest direction: η < 2/λmax. Momentum builds speed on steady slopes, RMSprop scales each weight’s step, and Adam does both with bias correction.
  2. Mini batches give noisy gradients. A schedule (cosine or step decay with warm up) starts fast and settles at the end. Scale the learning rate with the batch size.
  3. Batch norm normalises each feature over the batch and uses running averages at test time. Layer norm normalises each example and suits transformers.
  4. Weight decay, dropout and early stopping fight overfitting. Use AdamW for decoupled decay and inverted dropout so test time needs no change.
  5. Follow a recipe: check the first loss, overfit one batch, find the learning rate, then regularise and tune one thing at a time.
