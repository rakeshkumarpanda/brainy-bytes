Lesson 1 of 3

Why we may eliminate again and again

Chapter 5.1 showed that a rational player never uses a strictly dominated strategy. Common knowledge of rationality lets us go further, one layer at a time.

LayerReasoning on the slide 11 game
0Player 2 is rational, so she never plays column C (R beats it)
1Player 1 knows player 2 is rational, so he ignores C. In the smaller game M and D become dominated
2Player 2 knows player 1 knows she is rational, so she expects only row U. Now R is dominated
kEach round of elimination uses one more layer of "I know that you know that…"
IESDS, round kExam favourite
DSik = {{ si ∈ Sik : some s′i ∈ Sik beats it against every s−i ∈ S−ik }}
Compare only against strategies that still survive.
UpdateExam favourite
Sik+1 = Sik ∖ DSik
Remove the elements. Stop when no player has a dominated strategy. Every profile of survivors is a prediction.
Remember
Two small slip ups on slide 9: the dominator must range over the current set Sᵢᵏ, and the update is Sᵢᵏ minus DSᵢᵏ, not minus a set that contains DSᵢᵏ. Slide 6 also says "strictly" where it means "weakly" in two places. The formulas are the correct part.
Real world scenario: the blue eyed islanders

Islanders must leave on the night they work out that their own eyes are blue. A visitor announces "at least one of you has blue eyes". Everyone already knew it, yet the announcement makes it common knowledge, and with k blue eyed people all leave on night k. Each night peels off one layer of "knows that knows", just like each round of IESDS. In "guess two thirds of the average" experiments, real people stop after one to three layers, which is why predictions needing many rounds are less reliable in practice.

Worked examples: the Marketing War and slide 11

Marketing War (slide 10)
  1. Round 1: M ▷₁ L (8 > 6, 4 > 2, 1 > 0) and by symmetry M ▷₂ L. Delete row L and column L together.
  2. Round 2 on {{M, H}}: against M, 4 > 3, but against H, 1 < 2. No domination for either player.
  3. Survivors {{M, H}} × {{M, H}}: four profiles. IESDS says "nobody goes low" but no more
  4. Among the four, only (M, M) and (H, H) are self enforcing. Finding them needs Nash equilibrium.

Slide 11, cell by cell
  1. Round 1, player 1: U is best against L (4) and D is best against C (9), so neither can be dominated. M is beaten by U against L but wins against C, so nothing goes.
  2. Round 1, player 2: R = (2, 6, 8) against C = (1, 4, 6): 2 > 1, 6 > 4, 8 > 6, so R ▷₂ C. Delete C. One dominator is enough.
  3. Round 2, player 1 against L and R only: U = (4, 6) beats M = (2, 3) and D = (3, 2). Delete M and D.
  4. Round 3, player 2 with only row U: L gives 3, R gives 2. Delete R.
  5. IESDS equilibrium (U, L) with payoffs (4, 3). Order: C, then M and D, then R

Neither player has a dominant strategy here, so the dominant strategy equilibrium says nothing, yet IESDS pins down one answer. Is it self enforcing? Yes: player 1 moving down column L gets 2 or 3, less than 4, and player 2 moving along row U gets 1 or 2, less than 3. Is it the best? No. (D, C) = (9, 6) Pareto dominates it, and the Pareto optimal profiles are (9, 6) and (2, 8). Solving each player's problem separately, holding the others fixed, need not maximize anything for the group.

Theorems about IESDS

TheoremStatement
5If a strict dominant strategy equilibrium exists, IESDS leaves exactly it after one round
6Every Nash equilibrium survives IESDS
7In a finite game, if IESDS leaves one profile, it is the unique Nash equilibrium
8For strict dominance in finite games, the order of deletion does not change the survivors (Gilboa, Kalai and Zemel 1990)
Nash equilibrium (preview)Exam favourite
vi(si*, s*−i) ≥ vi(si, s*−i) for every i and every si
No player gains by a unilateral deviation. The course defines it fully in later lectures.
Proof of theorem 6: Nash equilibria are never deleted
  1. Suppose some part of a Nash equilibrium s* is deleted. Let round k be the first time, and sᵢ* the strategy deleted.
  2. Before round k nothing of s* was deleted, so s*₋ᵢ is still available in round k.
  3. Deletion means some s′ᵢ beats sᵢ* against every surviving s₋ᵢ, in particular against s*₋ᵢ.
  4. So vᵢ(s′ᵢ, s*₋ᵢ) > vᵢ(sᵢ*, s*₋ᵢ), and player i gains by deviating from s*.
  5. That contradicts s* being a Nash equilibrium.

  2. Delete T first: on rows M and B, R = (1, 1) weakly beats L = (1, 0). Delete L. Payoff (2, 1).
  3. Delete B first: on rows T and M, L = (1, 1) weakly beats R = (0, 1). Delete R. Payoff (1, 1).

Going deeper: Proof idea of theorem 8, order independence

Removing the opponents' strategies only shrinks the list of s₋ᵢ to check, so a strategy strictly dominated in a game stays strictly dominated in every smaller game that keeps its dominator. If the dominator was deleted first, its own dominator takes over, by transitivity, and that chain ends at a survivor because the game is finite. So a deletion can be postponed but never lost, and every maximal sequence of deletions ends at the same sets. With weak dominance a tie can make the replacement fail, which is exactly what the order game shows.

One idea, four depths

Cross out the choices nobody sensible would make, then look again: with fewer options left, more choices become silly. Keep going until nothing more can be crossed out.

IESDS deletes all strictly dominated strategies, compared only within the current game, round after round. Slide 11 ends at (U, L) = (4, 3). Strict IESDS is order independent, always leaves something, finds any DSE, and keeps every Nash equilibrium. Weak IESDS depends on order.

With mixed strategy domination, IESDS gives the rationalizable strategies in two player games (Bernheim 1984, Pearce 1984). Rationalizability is exactly what common knowledge of rationality implies, a weaker prediction than Nash equilibrium.

Level k and cognitive hierarchy models (Stahl and Wilson, Camerer, Ho and Chong 2004) replace common knowledge with a small number of reasoning steps and fit laboratory data better. In AI, iterated best response and double oracle methods for large games are computational relatives of iterated elimination.
