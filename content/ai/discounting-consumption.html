Lesson 1 of 3

Why money later is worth less

Put 100 rupees in a bank paying interest r. After a year you have 100(1 + r). Now turn the question round. Someone promises you 100 a year from now. What is that promise worth today? It is the amount X that would grow into 100, so X(1 + r) = 100.

Discount factorExam favourite
δ = 11 + r,   0 < δ < 1
A rupee next period is worth δ rupees today.
n periods aheadExam favourite
value today of A received after n periods = A δn = A(1 + r)n
Two periods: 100 grows to 100(1 + r)², so it is worth 100δ² today.
Present value of a stream
PV = ∑t xt δt
Discount each payment by its own delay, then add.

δ is not something you choose. It describes how patient you are. The professor's example: if you need money for an operation today, a payment tomorrow is worth very little to you, so your δ is close to 0. Someone with no urgent needs has δ close to 1.

Practice problem B: a scholarship pays 200, 150 and 300 at the ends of years 1, 2 and 3, with δ = 0.9
  1. 200 × 0.9 = 180.
  2. 150 × 0.81 = 121.5.
  3. 300 × 0.729 = 218.7.
  4. PV = 180 + 121.5 + 218.7 = 520.2
  5. The implied interest rate is r = 1/δ − 1 = 1/0.9 − 1 ≈ 11.1% a year.

Discounting inside a decision tree

In the discounted R&D tree, research costs 1 today and pays 10 next period if it works. The branch value is 10δ − 1, not (10 − 1)δ. The cost is paid now, so it is not discounted. Only the later gain is.

Lesson 2 of 3

Spreading consumption over time

You have a total income K to spend over several periods. Each period you enjoy u(x) from spending x, with u′ > 0 (more is better) and u″ < 0 (each extra rupee adds a little less). Later enjoyment is discounted by δ. How should you split K?

Two periods, u = ln xExam favourite
max ln x₁ + δ ln(K − x₁)   ⟹   x₁* = K1 + δ,   x₂* = δK1 + δ
From 1/x₁ = δ/(K − x₁).
Three periods, u = ln xExam favourite
x₁* = K1 + δ + δ²,   x₂* = δK1 + δ + δ²,   x₃* = δ²K1 + δ + δ²
The board solution from lecture 4.
Equal discounted marginal utility
1/x₁* = δ/x₂* = δ²/x₃*
No small move of money between two periods can raise the total.
The whiteboard derivation for three periods
  1. Maximize ln x₁ + δ ln x₂ + δ² ln(K − x₁ − x₂).
  2. ∂/∂x₁: 1/x₁ − δ²/(K − x₁ − x₂) = 0. ∂/∂x₂: δ/x₂ − δ²/(K − x₁ − x₂) = 0.
  3. Both equal the same δ²/x₃, so 1/x₁ = δ/x₂, giving x₁ = x₂/δ. Likewise x₃ = δx₂.
  4. ln is increasing, so spend everything: x₂/δ + x₂ + δx₂ = K.
  5. x₂*(1 + δ + δ²) = δK, so x₂* = δK/(1 + δ + δ²)
Remember
Diminishing marginal utility means u″ < 0, not u′ falling to zero. Going from 10 to 11 rupees adds more than going from 110 to 111, but both add something. The first order condition sets the derivative of the whole objective to zero, never u′ itself.
Practice problem D: u = √x, two periods
  1. Maximize √x₁ + δ√(K − x₁).
  2. Derivative: 1/(2√x₁) − δ/(2√(K − x₁)) = 0, so √(K − x₁) = δ√x₁.
  3. Square: K − x₁ = δ²x₁, so x₁* = K/(1 + δ²).
  4. K = 181, δ = 0.9: x₁* = 181/1.81 = 100 and x₂* = 81
Lesson 3 of 3

Corners, homework and time consistency

Homework 1: linear utility

With u(x) = x the objective is x₁ + δx₂ + δ²x₃ with x₁ + x₂ + x₃ = K. The coefficient on x₁ is the largest because 1 > δ > δ². There is no curvature to reward spreading, so all the money goes to the earliest period.

Linear utility
x* = (K, 0, 0)
A linear objective over a budget is maximized at a corner.

Convex utility: the derivative finds the worst point

With u(x) = x² in two periods, v(x) = x² + δ(K − x)² is convex in x. Setting v′ = 0 finds its minimum, the worst split. The best plan is a corner: compare v(K) = K² with v(0) = δK². Since δ < 1, consume everything now. With δ = 1 the two corners tie, but splitting is still worse.

Lecture example: K = 10, δ = 0.8, u = x²
  1. v(x) = x² + 0.8(10 − x)².
  2. v′(x) = 2x − 1.6(10 − x) = 0 gives x = 16/3.6 = 4.44, where v = 44.4. That is the minimum.
  3. Corners: v(10) = 100 and v(0) = 80.
  4. Best: x₁ = 10, x₂ = 0, payoff 100

Homework 2: is the plan time consistent?

Re-plan in period 2 with what is left
  1. After x₁* = K/(1 + δ + δ²), the remaining money is R = Kδ(1 + δ)/(1 + δ + δ²).
  2. From period 2 the problem is the two period one with R: x₂ = R/(1 + δ), x₃ = δR/(1 + δ).
  3. Substitute R: x₂ = Kδ/(1 + δ + δ²) and x₃ = Kδ²/(1 + δ + δ²).
  4. These are the original x₂* and x₃*, so the plan is time consistent

Going deeper: Why exponential discounting is time consistent

The trade off between periods t and t + 1 has relative weight δt+1/δt = δ, the same no matter when you look at it. So today's view of tomorrow against the day after is the same as tomorrow's view. With hyperbolic weights 1/(1 + kt), the ratio between t and t + 1 seen from today is (1 + kt)/(1 + k(t + 1)), close to 1 for large t, but seen from period t it becomes 1/(1 + k), much smaller. Plans made today for the far future look too patient once that future arrives, which is the economics of procrastination.

One idea, four depths

A rupee today is worth more than a rupee next year, because today's rupee could earn interest. If more money makes you a little less happy each time, you should spread spending out instead of spending it all at once.

δ = 1/(1 + r), and A received in n periods is worth Aδⁿ. With concave u the optimal plan equalizes discounted marginal utilities, so for ln x the shares are 1 : δ : δ². Linear or convex u sends everything to a corner.

The intertemporal Euler equation u′(xt) = δ(1 + r) u′(xt+1) generalizes the first order conditions. Exponential discounting is the only time consistent stationary scheme. Quasi hyperbolic β δ discounting (Laibson 1997) models present bias.

In reinforcement learning the discount γ in Σ γt rt plays the role of δ, keeping returns finite and trading off near against far rewards. Work on hyperbolic discounting in RL (Fedus et al. 2019) learns values for many γ at once and combines them into a hyperbolic weighting.
