Lesson 1 of 5

How to write any game down

Lecture 6 opens with a recipe. Before you solve a game you must state it precisely, and almost every mistake in class came from skipping a step.

The modelling recipe
  1. Players: who makes a decision?
  2. Strategies: what exactly does each player choose, a quantity, a price, contribute or not? What is the smallest and largest allowed value?
  3. Payoffs: split into cases. In each case write what the player receives minus what it costs.
  4. Game = ⟨N, {{Sᵢ}}, {{vᵢ}}⟩

This chapter applies the recipe to three games used throughout the course: Cournot competition in quantities, Bertrand competition in prices, and the streetlamp public good.

Lesson 2 of 5

Cournot: competing in quantities

Two airlines, say IndiGo and SpiceJet, decide how many seats to put on the same route. They do not name a price. The market sets the price from the total supply through the inverse demand function. A potato farmer is in the same position: he can grow as much as he likes, but he cannot also fix the price.

Inverse demand
P(q) = a − bq if this is ≥ 0, and 0 otherwise
q is the total quantity of both firms. a and b are common knowledge.
Cournot payoffExam favourite
vi(si, sj) = (a − b(si + sj)) si − Ci(si),   or −Ci(si) if the price would be negative
Strategy set Sᵢ = [0, ∞), so the game is infinite.
Best response, linear cost cᵢExam favourite
si = a − ci − b sj2b
From ∂vᵢ/∂sᵢ = a − 2bsᵢ − bsⱼ − cᵢ = 0. Your best output falls by half of the rival's.
Symmetric equilibriumExam favourite
s* = a − c3b
Put sⱼ = s* in the best response: 3bs* = a − c.
Lecture numbers: a = 100, b = 1, c = 10
  1. Each firm: s* = (100 − 10) ÷ 3 = 30.
  2. Total Q = 60, so the price is 100 − 60 = 40.
  3. Profit of each firm = (40 − 10) × 30.
  4. = 900 each
Practice problem F: different costs, a = 60, b = 1, c₁ = 12, c₂ = 18
  1. Best responses: s₁ = (48 − s₂) ÷ 2 and s₂ = (42 − s₁) ÷ 2.
  2. Substitute: s₁ = 13.5 + s₁ ÷ 4, so s₁* = 18 and s₂* = 12.
  3. General formula: s₁* = (a − 2c₁ + c₂) ÷ 3b = 54 ÷ 3 = 18.
  4. Price 30, profits 18 × 18 = 324 and 12 × 12 = 144
  5. The cheaper firm makes more and earns more. Constant costs do not mean equal costs.
Lesson 3 of 5

Bertrand: competing in prices

Now the firms choose prices and the market decides how much each sells. Buyers go only to the cheaper firm, and split evenly when prices are equal. In class, students first said the strategy was a quantity, then "0 to infinity", then "MRP". The professor corrected each in turn.

Step 2 of the recipe: the strategy set
  1. A firm chooses a price pᵢ, not a quantity.
  2. A price cannot be negative, so pᵢ ≥ 0.
  3. The highest price the market can produce is at q = 0, which gives p = a.
  4. Sᵢ = [0, a]
  5. Prices above a sell nothing, exactly like a, so they add nothing. MRP, a legal price cap, plays no part in this model.
Quantity from price
q = a − pb
Turn the demand rule around: p = a − bq gives bq = a − p.
Bertrand payoffExam favourite
vi = pi a − pib − Ci(a − pib) if cheaper,   half the market (divide by 2b) if tied,   −Ci(0) if dearer
Three cases. The −Cᵢ(0) term is a fixed cost paid even when nothing sells.

The payoff jumps. Just below 40 the firm earns about 1,800, exactly at 40 it earns 900, and just above it earns 0. Undercutting by one paisa roughly doubles profit. That is why price competition is so fierce.

In the exam
The Bertrand paradox: with equal unit cost c, the only stable prices are p₁ = p₂ = c. Two firms are enough to drive profit to zero, while Cournot firms keep the price at 40, well above cost 10.

The streetlamp: a public good

Three neighbours each decide whether to pay 1 towards a streetlamp. It is built only if at least two pay, and once built it is worth 3 to everyone, including anyone who did not pay. There is no refund if it is not built.

Streetlamp payoffExam favourite
vi(s) = 3 · 1[ ∑j sj ≥ 2 ] − si,   si ∈ {{0, 1}}
The indicator 1[·] is 1 when the statement is true and 0 otherwise. Split on the sum, not on the eight profiles one by one.
Others who payYou payYou do not payBetter for you
0−1 (not built)0Do not pay
12 (you complete it)0Pay, you are pivotal
223 (free ride)Do not pay

Neither choice is always best, so the game has no dominant strategy. From (1, 1, 1) each neighbour gains by stopping alone, 3 against 2, so everyone paying is not self enforcing. The self enforcing profiles are the three with exactly two payers, and the unhappy (0, 0, 0), where a lone payer would just lose 1.

Real world scenario: all or nothing crowdfunding

A crowdfunding campaign that only charges backers if a target is reached is a threshold public good. Everyone wants the project, but each backer hopes others will cover the target. Open source maintenance and herd immunity from vaccination have the same structure: a good that nobody can be excluded from and that needs enough contributors.

Going deeper: Comparing the three models

Cournot payoffs are smooth and concave in a firm's own quantity, so calculus finds the best response, and the price stays above cost. Bertrand payoffs jump at pᵢ = pⱼ, so there is no smooth maximum and price falls to cost. The streetlamp is finite, with two strategies each, and its interest lies in coordination and free riding rather than in calculus. Choosing the right strategy variable changes the prediction completely.

One idea, four depths

To study a game, first write down who plays, what each can choose, and what each gets in every case. Firms that choose amounts behave differently from firms that choose prices, and shared goods tempt people to let others pay.

Cournot: sᵢ = (a − cᵢ − bsⱼ)/2b and s* = (a − c)/3b, so 30 each, price 40, profit 900. Bertrand: Sᵢ = [0, a] and a three case payoff, and price falls to cost. Streetlamp: vᵢ = 3·1[Σ ≥ 2] − sᵢ.

With n Cournot firms s* = (a − c)/((n + 1)b), and the price tends to c as n grows, so Cournot converges to perfect competition. Bertrand with capacity limits (Edgeworth) or differentiated products restores positive margins.

Learning agents in repeated pricing games can learn to keep prices above the Bertrand level without communicating (Calvano et al. 2020, algorithmic collusion), which is now a question for competition law. Mechanism design asks how to set rules so that public goods are funded despite free riding.

Lesson 5 of 5

From intuition to research

Writing a real situation as a game is a skill. Here it is practised on two tea stalls, with the numbers worked out and a modern twist with pricing algorithms.

Real world scenario: two tea stalls choosing how many cups to make

Two stalls sell tea on the same street. The more cups both make, the lower the price customers will pay. Each owner decides how many cups to prepare, knowing the other is doing the same. This is a Cournot game.

Work it out: Cournot with P = 100 − q₁ − q₂ and cost 10 per cup
  1. Profit of stall 1: (100 − q₁ − q₂ − 10) q₁. Best response q₁ = (90 − q₂) ÷ 2.
  2. Symmetric equilibrium: q = (90 − q) ÷ 2, so q = 30 each, price 40.
  3. Profit each: (40 − 10) × 30 = 900.
  4. A single owner would make 45 cups at price 55 for 2025, or 1012.5 each if shared, more than 900
Going deeper: the analysis

Cournot competition gives more output and lower prices than a monopoly but less than perfect competition. Bertrand competition in prices with identical goods drives the price down to cost with just two firms, the Bertrand paradox, because undercutting by a tiny amount captures the whole market. The streetlamp shows the public good problem: each person’s contribution helps everyone, so each contributes too little.

Going deeper: the research view

Calvano et al. (2020) showed that simple Q-learning pricing algorithms, playing a repeated Bertrand game, learn to keep prices above the competitive level without any communication. This has become a question for competition law as more prices are set by AI.

Common mistake
The Cournot equilibrium is not the joint best outcome for the firms. Each firm ignores that its extra output lowers the other’s price.
