IESDS solves the Cournot duopoly

Cournot firms have no dominant quantity. Yet some quantities are strictly dominated, and IESDS solves the whole game. This was the professor's homework at the end of lecture 6.

The rounds of elimination
  1. Round 1: the peak is largest when the rival makes 0, at the monopoly quantity 45. Any q above 45 is beaten by 45 whatever the rival does. Survivors [0, 45].
  2. Round 2: the rival makes at most 45, so every peak is at least BR(45) = 22.5. Any q below 22.5 is beaten by 22.5. Survivors [22.5, 45].
  3. Round 3: the rival makes at least 22.5, so every peak is at most BR(22.5) = 33.75. Survivors [22.5, 33.75].
  4. Round 4: BR(33.75) = 28.125. Survivors [28.125, 33.75]. The width halves each round.
  5. Limit q* = (a − c)/3b = 30, the only survivor

Auctions: when truth telling is dominant

In a sealed bid auction each bidder privately values the object at v and submits a bid b. Let B be the highest rival bid.

Second price (Vickrey)Exam favourite
payoff = v − B if b > B, otherwise 0
The winner pays the second highest bid. Bidding b = v weakly dominates every other bid.
First priceExam favourite
payoff = v − b if b > B, otherwise 0
The winner pays her own bid. Overbidding is weakly dominated, but bidding v is not dominant.

Why underbidding is never better in a second price auction
  1. If B is below the low bid, both bids win and pay B: same payoff.
  2. If B is between the low bid and v, the truthful bid wins v − B > 0 while the low bid loses.
  3. If B is above v, both lose.
  4. Never worse, sometimes strictly better: b = v is weakly dominant

In a first price auction, winning with b = v gives v − v = 0. Shading the bid below v can win with a positive surplus, so truthful bidding is not dominant and bidders shade. No dominant strategy exists there, which is why first price auctions need the Nash equilibrium analysis later in the course.

Real world scenario: online ad auctions

Search engines sell ad slots in automated auctions millions of times a day. Early systems used a first price rule and bidders kept adjusting their bids against each other. The move to second price style rules was meant to make simple truthful bidding safe. Display advertising later moved back to first price with automated bid shading, which shows the trade offs are still debated.

Voting, prices and puzzles

Practice D5: where should a candidate stand?

Two candidates choose a position 1 to 5 on a line. One voter sits at each position and votes for the nearer candidate, splitting ties. The payoff is the number of votes.

IESDS in the location game
  1. Round 1: position 2 beats 1 against every rival position (4 > 2.5, 2.5 > 1, 2 > 1.5, 2.5 > 2, 3 > 2.5). By symmetry 4 beats 5. Delete 1 and 5.
  2. Round 2: against rivals in {{2, 3, 4}}, position 3 earns (3, 2.5, 3), beating both 2 and 4, which earn (2.5, 2, 2.5).
  3. Both candidates stand at 3, the median voter

Practice D6: Bertrand with whole number prices

Prices 1 to 5, unit cost 1, 10 buyers
  1. Against a rival price of 1 every price earns 0, so no price beats another strictly in every column. Strict IESDS deletes nothing.
  2. Weak elimination: 2 weakly beats 1 and 4 weakly beats 5. Then 3 beats 4, then 2 beats 3.
  3. Weak IESDS ends at (2, 2), one step above cost
  4. But (1, 1), (2, 2) and (3, 3) are all self enforcing. Weak elimination threw two of them away.

Practice E4: below cost Bertrand prices

Pricing at cost c always earns 0. Pricing below cost earns a loss when you are cheapest or tied, and 0 when the rival is cheaper still. So pᵢ = c weakly but not strictly dominates pᵢ < c, and strict IESDS cannot start. The Bertrand prediction needs Nash equilibrium.

Going deeper: Mechanism design in one line

The Vickrey auction and the subsidy in the Marketing War (chapter 5.2) share an idea: instead of predicting play in a given game, change the rules so that the outcome you want becomes dominant. That reverse question, choosing a game to get a desired equilibrium, is mechanism design, and the Gibbard Satterthwaite theorem marks where it runs out for voting.

One idea, four depths

Even when no single choice is always best, some choices are always bad. Removing them again and again can lead to one clear answer, as with two shops on a street both moving to the middle.

Cournot IESDS halves the surviving interval each round to (a − c)/3b. In a second price auction bidding your value is weakly dominant. The location game ends at the median. Weak elimination can delete self enforcing outcomes, as in discrete Bertrand.

For Cournot with more than two firms, IESDS no longer pins down a single quantity, while the Nash equilibrium is still unique. The revenue equivalence theorem shows first and second price auctions give the same expected revenue with independent private values.

Generalized second price auctions for search ads are not truthful, which led to work on their equilibria (Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz 2007, Varian 2007). Learning agents bidding repeatedly in first price auctions are now studied as online learning problems with regret guarantees.
