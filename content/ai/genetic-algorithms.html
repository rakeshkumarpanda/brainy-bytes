Lesson 1 of 8

Search by evolution

Source: slide Set 2, slides 84 to 87, and the class of 1 February 2026.

What it is
A genetic algorithm (GA) keeps a population of candidate solutions, each written as a string (a chromosome) of genes. Better solutions are more likely to be chosen as parents, and children are made by mixing parents (crossover) and making small random changes (mutation).
Why we need it
It searches many regions at once, so it is less likely to get stuck than one hill climber. Crossover can join good parts found by different parents into one better solution.
How it works
Each generation: measure the fitness of every individual, select parents in proportion to fitness, cross them over at a random point, mutate a few genes, and replace the old population with the children. Repeat for many generations or until a good enough solution appears.

Genetic algorithm
function GENETIC-ALGORITHM(population, FITNESS):
    repeat:
        new_population = empty
        for i = 1 to size(population):
            x = RANDOM-SELECTION(population, FITNESS)
            y = RANDOM-SELECTION(population, FITNESS)
            child = REPRODUCE(x, y)          # crossover at a random cut point
            with a small probability: child = MUTATE(child)
            add child to new_population
        population = new_population
    until some individual is fit enough, or enough time has passed
    return the best individual

Selection, crossover and mutation in detail

Selection methodHow it worksStrengthWeakness
Roulette wheel (fitness proportionate)P(i) = fi ⁄ ∑f. Spin a wheel with slices sized by fitnessSimple, matches the theoryOne very fit individual can take over too early. Needs positive fitness
TournamentPick k individuals at random, the fittest of them winsEasy to tune with k, works with any fitness scaleLarge k is very greedy
RankSort by fitness, pick by rank, not by raw valueNot fooled by huge fitness gapsIgnores how much better the best is
ElitismCopy the best few unchanged into the next generationNever loses the best found so farCan reduce diversity

Common mistake
For problems like the travelling salesperson, plain one point crossover makes invalid tours (a city twice). Use order crossover or swap mutation, which always give valid permutations.
Lesson 4 of 8

A GA for 8 queens

AIMA writes an 8 queens state as 8 digits, the row of the queen in each column, for example 24748552. The fitness is the number of non attacking pairs, at most 28 for a solution.

Selection in the AIMA example
  1. Four boards have fitness 24, 23, 20 and 11. The total is 78.
  2. Selection chances: 24 ⁄ 78 = 31 percent, 23 ⁄ 78 = 29 percent, 20 ⁄ 78 = 26 percent, 11 ⁄ 78 = 14 percent.
  3. Crossover joins the left columns of one parent with the right columns of the other.
  4. if each parent has its queens placed well on one side, the child can be good on both sides
Fitness for 8 queens
fitness = 28 − (number of attacking pairs)
28 is the number of pairs among 8 queens, 8 × 7 ⁄ 2.
Search space for the digit string
88 ≈ 16.8 million
The same as for hill climbing on 8 queens.
Remember
Crossover helps when nearby genes form meaningful blocks, like the left half of the board. If the encoding scatters related decisions, crossover mostly breaks good solutions, and a GA is no better than random restarts.

The maths: selection pressure and schemata

Roulette wheel probabilityExam favourite
P(i) = fi ⁄ ∑j fj
Expected copies of i in a population of n: n P(i) = fi ⁄ f̄.
Schema theorem (Holland)
E[m(H, t + 1)] ≥ m(H, t) · (f(H) ⁄ f̄) · [1 − pc δ(H) ⁄ (l − 1) − o(H) pm]
m(H, t) individuals match schema H (like 1***1). f(H) is their average fitness, δ(H) the defining length, o(H) the order (fixed positions), l the string length, pc and pm the crossover and mutation rates.
Chance a string escapes mutation
(1 − pm)l
With pm = 0.01 and l = 5: 0.99⁵ ≈ 0.951.
Worked example: the schema 1**** in the slide example
  1. Strings matching 1****: 11000 (576) and 10011 (361). m = 2, average f(H) = (576 + 361) ⁄ 2 = 468.5.
  2. Population average f̄ = 292.5.
  3. δ(H) = 0 (one fixed position) and o(H) = 1, so crossover cannot break it. With no mutation the factor in brackets is 1.
  4. E[m] ≥ 2 × 468.5 ⁄ 292.5 ≈ 3.2 copies next generation (all 4 children start with 1 except 01100)
Remember
Short, low order schemata with above average fitness grow exponentially. These are the "building blocks" that crossover combines.

Bottom line: a GA is worth its cost when good solutions are built from parts that can be found separately and combined. Otherwise annealing or restarts are simpler.

Real world scenario: designing an antenna

NASA used an evolutionary algorithm to design the antenna of the ST5 spacecraft (2006). Each individual was a sequence of bending and branching instructions. The fitness came from a simulation of the radio pattern. The evolved shape looked like a bent paper clip that no engineer would draw, yet it met the mission needs, which is the classic strength of evolutionary search: it does not need intuition about what a good design looks like.

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

A genetic algorithm copies nature: the fitter animals have more children, children mix their parents, and small random changes sometimes help. Over many generations the population gets better.

Encode solutions as strings, choose parents by fitness (roulette, tournament, rank, elitism), cross over at a random point, mutate with a small probability. In the slide example the average fitness rose from 292.5 to 438.5 in one generation.

The schema theorem explains why short, fit building blocks spread. Diversity matters: too much selection pressure leads to premature convergence on one local optimum. Encodings must keep children valid (order crossover for permutations).

Research descendants include evolution strategies with self adapted mutation (CMA-ES), genetic programming that evolves programs as trees, neuroevolution of network weights and shapes (NEAT), and multi objective GAs (NSGA-II) that return a Pareto front of trade offs instead of one answer.

Chapter summary

  1. A genetic algorithm evolves a population of chromosomes (strings of genes) using a fitness function.
  2. Each generation: selection by fitness, crossover at a cut point, mutation with a small probability.
  3. Roulette wheel: P(i) = fi ⁄ ∑f, expected copies fi ⁄ f̄. Slide example: 169, 576, 64, 361, total 1170, chances 0.14, 0.49, 0.06, 0.31.
  4. After one generation the children are 01100, 11001, 11011, 10000 with best fitness 729 (x = 27).
  5. Crossover helps when good solutions are built from blocks. GAs search broadly but use more memory and need more tuning than hill climbing or annealing.
