Lesson 1 of 8

Inference rules and proofs

Source: Russell and Norvig, AIMA 4th ed., section 7.5.

What it is
An inference rule takes sentences of a certain shape and lets you write a new sentence. A proof is a chain of rule uses that ends with the sentence you want.
Why we need it
Model checking looks at all 2n models. A proof can ignore every symbol that does not matter, so it can be far shorter when the KB is large.
How it works
Start from the KB. Apply a sound rule to sentences you already have and add the result. Stop when the goal appears. This is a search problem: states are sets of sentences, actions are rule uses.
RuleFromConclude
Modus Ponensα ⇒ β and αβ
And-Eliminationα ∧ βα (and β)
Biconditional eliminationα ⇔ β(α ⇒ β) ∧ (β ⇒ α)
Contrapositionα ⇒ β¬β ⇒ ¬α

Proof that there is no pit in [1,2] or [2,1] (from R2 and R4 of chapter 7.1)
  1. R2: B1,1 ⇔ (P1,2 ∨ P2,1). Biconditional elimination gives R6: (B1,1 ⇒ (P1,2 ∨ P2,1)) ∧ ((P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ⇒ B1,1).
  2. And-Elimination on R6 gives R7: (P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ⇒ B1,1.
  3. Contraposition gives R8: ¬B1,1 ⇒ ¬(P1,2 ∨ P2,1).
  4. Modus Ponens with R4 (¬B1,1) gives R9: ¬(P1,2 ∨ P2,1).
  5. De Morgan gives R10: ¬P1,2 ∧ ¬P2,1.
  6. neither [1,2] nor [2,1] has a pit, proved in 5 steps without listing 128 models
Remember
Logic is monotonic: adding sentences to a KB never removes something it already entails. If KB ⊨ α then KB ∧ β ⊨ α. So a rule can be used whenever its premises are in the KB, whatever else is there.
Lesson 2 of 8

Resolution and conjunctive normal form

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 7.5.2.

The resolution ruleExam favourite
from ℓ1 ∨ ... ∨ ℓk and m1 ∨ ... ∨ mn, where ℓi and mj are opposite literals, conclude the clause of all the other literals
Example: from P ∨ Q and ¬Q ∨ R, conclude P ∨ R. Copies of the same literal are kept once (factoring).
Why it is sound
if ℓi is true then mj is false, so the rest of the second clause must be true. If ℓi is false, the rest of the first clause must be true
Either way, the joined rest is true.

Resolution works on clauses: ORs of literals. A sentence that is an AND of clauses is in conjunctive normal form (CNF). Every sentence can be turned into an equivalent CNF sentence.

Converting R2: B1,1 ⇔ (P1,2 ∨ P2,1) to CNF
  1. Remove ⇔: (B1,1 ⇒ (P1,2 ∨ P2,1)) ∧ ((P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ⇒ B1,1).
  2. Remove ⇒ (α ⇒ β becomes ¬α ∨ β): (¬B1,1 ∨ P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ∧ (¬(P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ∨ B1,1).
  3. Move ¬ inwards with De Morgan: (¬B1,1 ∨ P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ∧ ((¬P1,2 ∧ ¬P2,1) ∨ B1,1).
  4. Distribute ∨ over ∧: (¬B1,1 ∨ P1,2 ∨ P2,1) ∧ (¬P1,2 ∨ B1,1) ∧ (¬P2,1 ∨ B1,1).
  5. three clauses
PL-RESOLUTION
function PL-RESOLUTION(KB, α) returns true or false
    clauses = the clauses of the CNF form of KB ∧ ¬α
    new = { }
    loop:
        for each pair of clauses Ci, Cj in clauses:
            resolvents = PL-RESOLVE(Ci, Cj)
            if resolvents contains the empty clause: return true
            new = new ∪ resolvents
        if new ⊆ clauses: return false       (nothing new: no contradiction)
        clauses = clauses ∪ new
In the exam
The ground resolution theorem: if a set of clauses is unsatisfiable, resolution will reach the empty clause. So resolution is a complete refutation procedure for propositional logic. It cannot list every entailed sentence directly, but it can always confirm or refute a given one.

Horn clauses, forward and backward chaining

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 7.5.4.

Clause typeAt most / exactlyExampleAs an implication
Definite clauseexactly one positive literal¬L ∨ ¬M ∨ PL ∧ M ⇒ P
Factone positive literal and nothing elseAA
Horn clauseat most one positive literal¬A ∨ ¬BA ∧ B ⇒ False (a goal clause)
What it is
Forward chaining starts with the known facts, fires every rule whose premises are all known, and adds its conclusion, until the query appears or nothing new comes. Backward chaining starts with the query and looks for rules that conclude it, then tries to prove their premises the same way.
Why we need it
Most rule based systems use rules of the form "if these hold then that holds". For these definite clauses, chaining decides entailment in time linear in the size of the KB, far faster than general methods.
How it works
Forward chaining keeps a count of unknown premises for each rule and an agenda of new facts. Backward chaining is a depth first AND OR search: OR over the rules for a goal, AND over the premises of a rule, with a check for goals already on the path.
Forward chainingBackward chaining
Driven byData (the facts)The goal
Good forWatching a stream of new facts, such as sensorsAnswering one question, such as "what should I do now?"
Wasted workMay derive many facts unrelated to the queryOnly touches relevant rules
CostO(size of KB), each rule counted down at most once per premiseOften much less than the KB size
Sound and complete for definite clausesYesYes, with the loop check
Lesson 4 of 8

Fast satisfiability: DPLL and WalkSAT

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 7.6.

What it is
DPLL is a depth first search over truth values for a CNF formula, with three tricks that cut the search. WalkSAT is a local search that flips one symbol at a time.
Why we need it
Proving KB ⊨ α is the same as showing KB ∧ ¬α is unsatisfiable. A good SAT solver therefore gives a good prover, and SAT solvers also find plans, schedules and circuit errors.
How it works
DPLL assigns symbols one at a time and simplifies. Early termination: stop a branch as soon as a clause is false, or accept once every clause is true. Pure symbol: a symbol with the same sign in every remaining clause can be set to make them true. Unit clause: a clause with one literal left forces its value. WalkSAT picks a false clause and flips one of its symbols, either the one that makes the most clauses true or, with probability p, a random one.
MethodCompleteTypical use
Truth table (TT-ENTAILS)YesTeaching, tiny problems
ResolutionYes (refutation complete)Theorem provers, the basis of first order provers
Forward or backward chainingYes, for definite clauses onlyRule based systems, Prolog
DPLL and its modern form CDCLYesIndustrial SAT solving with millions of clauses
WalkSATNo: it can find a model, but it cannot prove there is noneLarge satisfiable problems
Going deeper: Modern SAT solvers

Modern solvers use conflict driven clause learning (CDCL): when a branch fails, they work out which choices caused the conflict, add a new clause that rules out that combination, and jump back several levels at once. With watched literals for fast unit propagation, restarts and smart variable order, they solve industrial problems with millions of variables.

The maths: how many clauses, and how fast

Possible clauses over n symbolsExam favourite
3n
Each symbol is positive, negative or absent in a clause. So resolution always stops: there are at most 3n distinct clauses to add.
Clauses from distributing
(a ∧ b) ∨ (c ∧ d) gives (a ∨ c) ∧ (a ∨ d) ∧ (b ∨ c) ∧ (b ∨ d)
An OR of k ANDs with 2 parts each gives 2k clauses: CNF can blow up. The Tseitin trick adds new symbols to keep it linear.
Forward chaining cost
O(n) for a KB of total size n
Each premise of each rule is counted down at most once.
Random 3-SAT hardness
m ⁄ n ≈ 4.26
With m clauses over n symbols, problems are hardest near this ratio. Below it almost all are satisfiable, above it almost none.
Worked example: resolve (A ∨ B ∨ ¬C) with (¬B ∨ D)
  1. The opposite pair is B and ¬B.
  2. Keep the rest of the first clause: A ∨ ¬C.
  3. Keep the rest of the second: D.
  4. A ∨ ¬C ∨ D
Worked example: why resolving on two pairs at once is wrong
  1. Clauses: (P ∨ Q) and (¬P ∨ ¬Q).
  2. Wrong: cancel both pairs and get the empty clause, claiming a contradiction.
  3. But P = T, Q = F makes both clauses true, so they are satisfiable.
  4. resolve on one pair only: P ∨ ¬P or Q ∨ ¬Q, both always true and useless

Bottom line: use chaining whenever the knowledge fits definite clauses, a SAT solver (DPLL or CDCL) for general problems, and resolution when you need a proof that can be checked and lifted to first order logic.

Real world scenario: Rule based systems

Business rule engines (such as Drools) and the expert systems of the 1980s run forward chaining over large sets of if then rules. The Rete algorithm (Forgy, 1982) speeds this up by remembering partial matches, so a new fact only touches the rules it can affect. Prolog runs backward chaining.

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Instead of checking every possible world, follow safe rules step by step, like "if A gives B, and A is true, then B is true", until you reach what you want.

Sound rules: Modus Ponens, And-Elimination, equivalences, resolution. Resolution on CNF is refutation complete. Definite clauses allow linear time forward and backward chaining. DPLL adds early termination, pure symbols and unit clauses, and WalkSAT searches locally.

Resolution proofs can need exponential length: Haken (1985) showed the pigeonhole formulas need exponentially long resolution proofs. That is a limit of the proof system, not just of a search strategy. CDCL solvers are, in a precise sense, as strong as general resolution (Pipatsrisawat and Darwiche, 2011).

Research topics: proof systems stronger than resolution (cutting planes, extended resolution), certified SAT solving where the solver outputs a checkable proof of unsatisfiability, and learning heuristics for SAT with neural networks.

Lesson 8 of 8

Chapter summary

  1. Inference rules such as Modus Ponens and And-Elimination give proofs that skip irrelevant symbols. Logic is monotonic.
  2. Resolution: from ℓ ∨ α and ¬ℓ ∨ β, get α ∨ β. Turn sentences into CNF in four steps: remove ⇔, remove ⇒, move ¬ in, distribute ∨ over ∧.
  3. Proof by resolution adds ¬α and searches for the empty clause. It is refutation complete.
  4. Definite clauses allow forward chaining (from facts) and backward chaining (from the goal) in linear time.
  5. DPLL (early termination, pure symbols, unit clauses) and WalkSAT solve SAT. Modern CDCL solvers handle millions of clauses.
