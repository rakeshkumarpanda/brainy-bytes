Lesson 1 of 9

From first order inference to propositional inference

Source: Russell and Norvig, AIMA 4th ed., section 9.1. The uploaded slides do not cover logic, so this step follows the textbook.

RuleWhat it doesExample
Universal Instantiation (UI)From ∀v α, infer α with v replaced by any ground term gFrom ∀x King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x), infer King(John) ∧ Greedy(John) ⇒ Evil(John)
Existential Instantiation (EI)From ∃v α, infer α with v replaced by a brand new constant k (a Skolem constant)From ∃x Crown(x) ∧ OnHead(x, John), infer Crown(C1) ∧ OnHead(C1, John)

UI can be used many times, with every ground term. EI is used once, and then the ∃ sentence is dropped. Using both on every quantified sentence, a first order KB becomes a propositional KB: this is propositionalisation.

Why that is not enough
  1. With k constants, a rule with v variables becomes kv ground rules. Most of them are useless.
  2. With a function symbol such as Father(·), there are infinitely many ground terms: John, Father(John), Father(Father(John)), and so on.
  3. Herbrand's theorem saves completeness: if α is entailed, it is entailed by a finite part of the ground KB. So try terms of depth 0, then depth 1, then depth 2, until a proof is found.
  4. But if α is not entailed, this can go on for ever.
  5. first order entailment is semidecidable: a yes is always found, a no may never come
Lesson 2 of 9

Unification and lifting

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 9.2.

What it is
Unification finds a substitution θ that makes two atoms look the same. Generalised Modus Ponens uses it to apply a rule to facts directly, without making every ground copy of the rule first. This is called lifting an inference rule to first order logic.
Why we need it
Propositionalisation makes kv copies of each rule. Unification makes only the substitutions that the facts at hand need, so most useless work disappears.
How it works
Compare the two atoms from the outside in. Same predicate and argument count: unify the arguments pair by pair. A variable meets a term: bind it, unless the variable occurs inside the term (the occurs check). Two different constants, or two different functions: fail. Keep the bindings in θ and apply them as you go.
Generalised Modus Ponens (GMP)Exam favourite
from p1′, ..., pn′ and (p1 ∧ ... ∧ pn ⇒ q), infer SUBST(θ, q), where SUBST(θ, pi′) = SUBST(θ, pi) for every i
King(John), Greedy(y) and King(x) ∧ Greedy(x) ⇒ Evil(x) with θ = {x/John, y/John} give Evil(John).
UNIFY
UNIFY(p, q) = θ where SUBST(θ, p) = SUBST(θ, q)
θ is written as a set of bindings variable/term.
pqθ = UNIFY(p, q)
Knows(John, x)Knows(John, Jane){x/Jane}
Knows(John, x)Knows(y, Bill){x/Bill, y/John}
Knows(John, x)Knows(y, Mother(y)){y/John, x/Mother(John)}
Knows(John, x)Knows(x, Elizabeth)fail: x cannot be John and Elizabeth
Knows(John, x)Knows(x17, Elizabeth){x17/John, x/Elizabeth}, after standardising apart

Standardising apart: rename the variables of one sentence so that the two sentences share none. The x in "John knows x" and the x in "x knows Elizabeth" are different variables that happen to share a name.

Most general unifier (MGU): Knows(John, x) and Knows(y, z) unify with {y/John, x/z} or with {y/John, x/John, z/John}. The first is more general, since it commits to less. Up to renaming of variables, every unifiable pair has a single MGU.

Common mistake
The occurs check (does the variable appear inside the term?) makes unification cost quadratic in the size of the terms. Prolog leaves it out for speed, so Prolog can unify x with F(x) and build an endless term.
Lesson 3 of 9

Forward chaining: the crime example

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 9.3.

"The law says that it is a crime for an American to sell weapons to hostile nations. The country Nono, an enemy of America, has some missiles, and all of its missiles were sold to it by Colonel West, who is American. Prove that West is a criminal."

Sentence in definite clausesMeaning
American(x) ∧ Weapon(y) ∧ Sells(x, y, z) ∧ Hostile(z) ⇒ Criminal(x)the law
Owns(Nono, M1), Missile(M1)Nono has a missile, M1 (EI on "Nono has some missiles")
Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono, x) ⇒ Sells(West, x, Nono)all of Nono's missiles were sold by West
Missile(x) ⇒ Weapon(x)missiles are weapons
Enemy(x, America) ⇒ Hostile(x)an enemy of America counts as hostile
American(West), Enemy(Nono, America)facts
Forward chaining
  1. Round 1: Missile(x) ⇒ Weapon(x) with {x/M1} gives Weapon(M1).
  2. Round 1: Missile(x) ∧ Owns(Nono, x) ⇒ Sells(West, x, Nono) with {x/M1} gives Sells(West, M1, Nono).
  3. Round 1: Enemy(x, America) ⇒ Hostile(x) with {x/Nono} gives Hostile(Nono).
  4. Round 2: the law with {x/West, y/M1, z/Nono} gives Criminal(West).
  5. proved in 2 rounds, and a fixed point: nothing more can be added
Fact about first order forward chainingDetail
Sound and complete for first order definite clausesEvery entailed atom is eventually derived
DatalogDefinite clauses with no function symbols. Forward chaining then stops after polynomially many steps
Pattern matching is hardFinding all θ that match a rule's premises to the facts is like solving a CSP, and is NP-hard in general. Order the premises, most constrained first
Incremental forward chainingIn round t, only try rules with a premise that matches a fact new in round t − 1
ReteStore partial matches in a network so new facts update them cheaply (used in rule engines)
Lesson 4 of 9

Backward chaining and Prolog

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 9.4.

Backward chaining starts with the goal Criminal(West). It finds a rule whose head unifies with the goal, then makes each premise a new goal, carrying the bindings found so far from left to right. It is a depth first AND OR search over proofs.

Prolog featureWhat it means
Syntaxcriminal(X) :- american(X), weapon(Y), sells(X, Y, Z), hostile(Z). Upper case letters are variables
SearchDepth first, rules in the order written, premises left to right
Negation as failurenot P (written with a special operator in Prolog) succeeds if P cannot be proved. This is the closed world assumption, not real negation
No occurs checkFaster unification, but unsound in rare cases
IncompleteA left recursive rule such as path(X, Z) :- path(X, Y), link(Y, Z) can loop for ever. Writing link first fixes it
TablingRemembering solved subgoals (memoisation) avoids repeated work and many loops
Remember
Backward chaining is the reason Prolog programs can be read as logic and run as programs. But the order of rules and premises changes what happens, which pure logic does not care about.
Lesson 5 of 9

Resolution in first order logic

Source: AIMA 4th ed., section 9.5.

Conversion to CNF: "everyone who loves all animals is loved by someone"
  1. Start: ∀x [∀y Animal(y) ⇒ Loves(x, y)] ⇒ [∃y Loves(y, x)].
  2. Remove ⇒: ∀x ¬[∀y ¬Animal(y) ∨ Loves(x, y)] ∨ [∃y Loves(y, x)].
  3. Move ¬ inwards (¬∀ becomes ∃¬): ∀x [∃y Animal(y) ∧ ¬Loves(x, y)] ∨ [∃y Loves(y, x)].
  4. Standardise variables: ∀x [∃y Animal(y) ∧ ¬Loves(x, y)] ∨ [∃z Loves(z, x)].
  5. Skolemise: each ∃ variable becomes a Skolem function of the ∀ variables around it: y becomes F(x), z becomes G(x). ∀x [Animal(F(x)) ∧ ¬Loves(x, F(x))] ∨ Loves(G(x), x).
  6. Drop ∀ and distribute ∨ over ∧:
  7. [Animal(F(x)) ∨ Loves(G(x), x)] ∧ [¬Loves(x, F(x)) ∨ Loves(G(x), x)]
Binary resolutionExam favourite
from ℓ1 ∨ ... ∨ ℓk and m1 ∨ ... ∨ mn with UNIFY(ℓi, ¬mj) = θ, infer SUBST(θ, all other literals)
Example: [Animal(F(x)) ∨ Loves(G(x), x)] and [¬Loves(u, v) ∨ ¬Kills(u, v)] with θ = {u/G(x), v/x} give Animal(F(x)) ∨ ¬Kills(G(x), x).
Resolution proof that West is a criminal (add ¬Criminal(West))
  1. ¬Criminal(West) with the law gives ¬American(West) ∨ ¬Weapon(y) ∨ ¬Sells(West, y, z) ∨ ¬Hostile(z).
  2. With American(West): ¬Weapon(y) ∨ ¬Sells(West, y, z) ∨ ¬Hostile(z). With ¬Missile(x) ∨ Weapon(x): ¬Missile(y) ∨ ¬Sells(West, y, z) ∨ ¬Hostile(z).
  3. With Missile(M1): ¬Sells(West, M1, z) ∨ ¬Hostile(z). With the Sells rule: ¬Missile(M1) ∨ ¬Owns(Nono, M1) ∨ ¬Hostile(Nono).
  4. With Missile(M1), then Owns(Nono, M1): ¬Hostile(Nono). With the Hostile rule: ¬Enemy(Nono, America). With Enemy(Nono, America): the empty clause.
  5. 9 resolution steps, and they follow exactly the steps of backward chaining
Fact about first order resolutionDetail
Refutation complete (Robinson, 1965)If KB ⊨ α, resolution with factoring derives { } from KB ∧ ¬α
FactoringMerge two literals of one clause that unify, for example P(x) ∨ P(A) becomes P(A)
EqualityNeeds extra rules: demodulation (rewrite with x = y) and paramodulation
StrategiesUnit preference (resolve with short clauses), set of support (always use a clause from ¬α or derived from it), input resolution, subsumption (delete clauses implied by shorter ones)
Used inTheorem provers such as Vampire and E, and software verification
Lesson 6 of 9

The maths: instances, ground terms and proof size

Ground instances of one ruleExam favourite
kv
k constants, v variables. The crime law has v = 3 and the KB has k = 4 constants (West, M1, Nono, America): 64 copies, of which forward chaining needs 1.
Ground terms with one unary function F and one constant A, up to depth d
d + 1
A, F(A), F(F(A)), and so on.
Ground terms with c constants and one binary function, depth at most 1
c + c²
Each argument is a constant. Depth 2 grows much faster.
Worked example: unify P(x, F(y)) with P(G(z), F(A))
  1. Same predicate P with 2 arguments: unify pair by pair.
  2. x with G(z): x is a variable, so bind x/G(z).
  3. F(y) with F(A): same function, so unify y with A: bind y/A.
  4. θ = {x/G(z), y/A}, and both become P(G(z), F(A))
Worked example: why P(x, x) does not unify with P(A, B)
  1. First pair: x with A, bind x/A.
  2. Second pair: x with B. x is already bound to A, so compare A with B.
  3. A and B are different constants.
  4. fail

Which first order inference method to use

Inference in first order logic
PropositionalisationForward chainingBackward chaining (Prolog)Resolution
Works onAny FOL KBDefinite clausesDefinite clausesAny FOL KB in CNF
CompleteYes, with growing term depthYesNo with plain depth first search (loops), yes with tablingYes, refutation complete
Main costkv copies of every ruleMatching premises to factsRepeated subgoalsMany useless resolvents
Best forSmall finite domainsDatabases, rule engines, DatalogAnswering queries, logic programmingMathematics, software verification

Bottom line: use forward chaining when facts stream in and many conclusions are wanted, backward chaining for one query at a time, and resolution when the knowledge is not in definite clause form.

Real world scenario: Proving theorems with computers

In 1996 the resolution style prover EQP proved the Robbins conjecture, an open question in algebra for over 60 years. Amazon uses automated reasoning to check access policies in its cloud, and chip companies prove properties of floating point units with first order and higher order provers.

Lesson 8 of 9

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

A rule with variables is a pattern. Unification is the matching step: it finds which values for the variables make the rule fit the facts you have.

UI and EI reduce FOL to propositional logic but blow up. Unification and GMP lift inference instead. Forward chaining, backward chaining (Prolog) and resolution (with CNF and Skolemisation) are the three main methods. First order entailment is semidecidable.

Unification can be done in linear time (Paterson and Wegman, 1978). Herbrand's theorem underlies propositionalisation. Resolution with a fair strategy is refutation complete. Datalog with stratified negation is the basis of deductive databases.

Research continues on superposition calculi for equality, SMT solving that mixes theories, higher order unification (undecidable in general), and machine learned premise selection for large libraries of mathematics such as the Lean mathlib.

Lesson 9 of 9

Chapter summary

  1. Universal and existential instantiation turn FOL into propositional logic, but rules get kv copies and functions make it infinite. FOL entailment is semidecidable.
  2. Unification finds the most general θ that makes two atoms equal, with the occurs check. Standardise variables apart first.
  3. Generalised Modus Ponens applies a rule through unification. Forward chaining proves Criminal(West) in 2 rounds.
  4. Backward chaining (Prolog) works from the goal, depth first and left to right. It is fast but can loop.
  5. Resolution needs CNF with Skolem functions, and is refutation complete. The crime proof takes 9 resolution steps.
