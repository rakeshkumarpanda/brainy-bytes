Lesson 1 of 4

What a solution concept is for

Writing down a game does not tell us what will happen. A solution concept is a method that narrows all possible profiles down to the reasonable ones, either to advise players or to predict them. Whatever profile it selects is called an equilibrium of that concept.

The professor warned against the physics picture of equilibrium, a state a system returns to after a push. In game theory an equilibrium is simply what a chosen concept selects. There is no implied motion towards it.

Play a strategy that is best whatever others do(C, C)Sharp and useful, but not always available

The four assumptions of equilibrium analysis

AssumptionMeaning in this course
RationalEach player maximizes her own (expected) payoff
IntelligentEach player knows every action, the outcome map and everyone's preferences, as well as the analyst does
Common knowledgeThat everyone is rational and intelligent is itself common knowledge
Self enforcementThe equilibrium must hold without any outside enforcer
Real world scenario: the gang lawyer

Suppose each suspect in the Prisoner's Dilemma gets a lawyer from the same gang who threatens his family if he confesses. Both stay mum and (M, M) results. That outcome is not self enforcing. It holds only because of a third party. Remove the threat and each suspect gains by confessing alone.

What we want from a good concept

PropertyQuestion it asks
ExistenceDoes it give an answer for every game?
UniquenessDoes it pick a small set, ideally one profile? "Anything can happen" fails badly here.
InvarianceDoes a small change in payoffs that keeps every ranking leave the answer the same?

Pareto optimality

Pareto optimality is not a solution concept. It does not predict anything. It is a yardstick for judging the answer a solution concept gives: does the equilibrium waste payoff that everyone could have shared?

Pareto dominanceExam favourite
s Pareto dominates s′ ⟺ vi(s) ≥ vi(s′) for all i, and > for at least one j
Nobody is worse off and someone is strictly better off.
Pareto optimalExam favourite
s′ is not Pareto dominated by any profile
Also called Pareto efficient.
TestHow to apply it
Direct testSearch for a profile that is at least as good for everyone and strictly better for someone. If one exists, the candidate is not optimal.
Improvement testTry to make one player strictly better off. If every such move hurts someone, the candidate is optimal.
Prisoner's Dilemma
  1. (M, M) = (−2, −2) beats (C, C) = (−4, −4) for both players, so (C, C) is not Pareto optimal.
  2. Nothing beats (M, M): (M, C) is worse for player 1, (C, M) worse for player 2, (C, C) worse for both.
  3. (M, C) and (C, M) hold the best payoff for one player, so nothing can dominate them.
  4. Pareto optimal: (M, M), (M, C), (C, M). Not optimal: (C, C), the dominant strategy outcome
Marketing Campaign War (rows and columns L, M, H)
  1. Payoffs: (L,L) 6,6. (L,M) 2,8. (L,H) 0,4. (M,L) 8,2. (M,M) 4,4. (M,H) 1,3. (H,L) 4,0. (H,M) 3,1. (H,H) 2,2.
  2. (L, L) = (6, 6) beats (M, M) and every profile with H, strictly for both players.
  3. (L, M) and (M, L) contain 8, the largest single payoff, so nothing dominates them. Nothing dominates (6, 6) either.
  4. Pareto optimal: (L, L), (L, M), (M, L)
Common mistake
Pareto optimality says nothing about fairness. (2, 8) and (8, 2) are Pareto optimal next to the balanced (6, 6). It only rules out waste.
Lesson 3 of 4

Zero sum games, altruism and the practice problems

Rock, Paper, Scissors and a general proof

In Rock, Paper, Scissors the winner gets 1 and the loser −1, and a tie gives 0 to both. The two payoffs always add to 0, so the game is zero sum. Checking nine cells one by one caused confusion in class. A short proof settles every cell at once.

Claim: in a two player zero sum game every profile is Pareto optimal
  1. Suppose s Pareto dominates s′. Then v₁(s) ≥ v₁(s′) and v₂(s) ≥ v₂(s′), with one strict.
  2. Zero sum means v₂ = −v₁, so the second inequality says −v₁(s) ≥ −v₁(s′), that is v₁(s) ≤ v₁(s′).
  3. Together with v₁(s) ≥ v₁(s′) this forces v₁(s) = v₁(s′), and then v₂(s) = v₂(s′) too.
  4. No inequality is strict, a contradiction. Every profile is Pareto optimal.

With weight ½, staying mum becomes the better reply to both choices: −3 beats −3.5 and −5.5 beats −6. Caring about the other person turns the dilemma into a game whose sensible outcome is also Pareto optimal. Chapter 5.1 finds the exact weight, ⅓, where this switch happens.

Practice problem A: two farmers and one aquifer, pump Heavily (H) or Moderately (Mod)
  1. Payoffs: (H,H) 2,2. (H,Mod) 5,0. (Mod,H) 0,5. (Mod,Mod) 3,3.
  2. H beats Mod for player 1 against H (2 > 0) and against Mod (5 > 3). The table is symmetric.
  3. So both pump heavily and get (2, 2). But (Mod, Mod) = (3, 3) Pareto dominates it.
  4. Pareto optimal: (Mod, Mod), (H, Mod), (Mod, H). The predicted outcome is the wasteful one.
Practice problem C: two ride hailing apps choose driver incentives Small, Medium, Large
  1. Payoffs: (S,S) 5,5. (S,Md) 9,2. (S,Lg) 3,3. (Md,S) 2,9. (Md,Md) 6,6. (Md,Lg) 1,4. (Lg,S) 3,3. (Lg,Md) 4,1. (Lg,Lg) 2,2.
  2. (Md, Md) = (6, 6) dominates (S, S) and all profiles with Lg.
  3. (S, Md) and (Md, S) contain 9, the largest payoff.
  4. Pareto optimal: (S, Md), (Md, S), (Md, Md)
Going deeper: The tragedy of the commons

The farmers game is a two player preview of the tragedy of the commons, studied with n players and continuous effort next in the course. When each user of a shared resource gets the full benefit of extra use but bears only a share of the damage, self interest pushes total use above the level that would be best for the group. Elinor Ostrom's field work showed communities avoiding this with monitoring and graduated sanctions, which change the game rather than the players.

One idea, four depths

A rule for predicting games should always give an answer, a clear one, and not change wildly over tiny details. Separately, we can ask whether the result wastes anything that would make someone better off for free.

Equilibrium analysis assumes rational, intelligent players, common knowledge of both, and self enforcement. Pareto dominance is ≥ for all and > for one. In the Prisoner's Dilemma the dominant outcome (C, C) is the only profile that is not Pareto optimal. In zero sum games all profiles are optimal.

The Pareto frontier of a game is the set of undominated payoff vectors. Folk theorems for repeated games show that patient players can sustain Pareto superior outcomes such as (M, M) as equilibria, which links efficiency to the shadow of the future.

Measuring the price of anarchy (Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 1999) quantifies how far equilibria can fall below the social optimum, for example in routing networks and congestion games. Multi agent learning research studies how to steer self interested learners towards Pareto efficient outcomes, through reward shaping, communication or opponent shaping.

Lesson 4 of 4

From intuition to research

Pareto optimality asks whether an outcome wastes anything. Equilibria often do, and the size of that waste has a name in research: the price of anarchy.

Real world scenario: splitting a pizza

Two friends split a pizza 5 slices to 3. Moving one slice from one to the other helps one and hurts the other, so the split is Pareto optimal. Throwing a slice away hurts someone and helps no one, so any split that wastes slices is not. Pareto optimal does not mean fair: 8 slices to 0 is Pareto optimal too.

Work it out: the prisoner’s dilemma
  1. Payoffs: both cooperate (−1, −1), one defects (0, −3) or (−3, 0), both defect (−2, −2).
  2. Defect is better for each player whatever the other does, so the equilibrium is (defect, defect).
  3. Both cooperating gives (−1, −1), better for both.
  4. The equilibrium is Pareto dominated: total cost 4 against the optimum 2
Going deeper: the analysis

A good solution concept should exist for a wide class of games, give a small set of predictions, and not change under irrelevant details. Pareto optimality is a separate test of efficiency. The two can conflict, as the prisoner’s dilemma shows, which is the formal reason why contracts, repeated play and regulation exist.

Going deeper: the research view

The price of anarchy (Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou 1999) is the ratio between the cost of the worst equilibrium and the optimal cost. In the dilemma above it is 4 ÷ 2 = 2. For selfish routing with linear delays it is at most 4/3 (Roughgarden and Tardos 2002), and Braess’s paradox shows adding a road can make every driver slower.

Common mistake
Pareto optimal and fair are different ideas. Pareto says nothing about how the total is shared.
