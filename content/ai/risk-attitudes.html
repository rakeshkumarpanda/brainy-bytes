Lesson 1 of 4

Three attitudes to risk

Would you rather have 50 rupees for sure, or a coin toss that pays 100 or nothing? Both have the same expected money, 50. Most people take the sure 50. Expected utility explains this through the shape of u.

Risk neutralExam favourite
E[ u(X) ] = u( E[X] )
u is linear, u(x) = ax + b. Only expected money matters.
Risk averseExam favourite
E[ u(X) ] ≤ u( E[X] )
u is concave (u″ < 0), for example ln x or √x. The sure amount is preferred.
Risk lovingExam favourite
E[ u(X) ] ≥ u( E[X] )
u is convex (u″ > 0), for example x². The gamble is preferred.

The picture behind this is Jensen's inequality. Draw u and join the two outcomes with a straight chord. The chord's height at the mean is E[u(X)], and the curve's height there is u(E[X]). For a concave curve the chord is below, so the gamble is worth less than its mean.

The fair gamble from lecture 3
  1. Pay 1 to enter. Win 2 with probability 0.5, or nothing.
  2. Net outcomes: +1 with 0.5 and −1 with 0.5, so expected money is 0.
  3. Not playing gives 0 for sure, also expected money 0.
  4. Play when 0.5 u(1) + 0.5 u(−1) > u(0)
  5. A risk averse agent refuses, a risk neutral one is indifferent, and a risk lover plays.
Practice problem 5: u = x², 0 or 10 with 0.5 each
  1. E[X] = 5, so u(E[X]) = 25.
  2. E[u(X)] = 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 100 = 50.
  3. 50 > 25, so this agent is risk loving
Lesson 2 of 4

The certainty equivalent

How much is a gamble worth to you in sure money? That amount is its certainty equivalent. It turns any lottery into one number you can compare with cash.

Certainty equivalentExam favourite
u(CE) = E[ u(X) ]   ⟹   CE = u−1( E[u(X)] )
The sure amount you value exactly as much as the lottery.
Risk premium
RP = E[X] − CE
What you would give up in expectation to remove the risk. Positive for a risk averse agent.
Ordering
averse: CE ≤ E[X],   neutral: CE = E[X],   loving: CE ≥ E[X]
Practice problem 4: √x, 100 with 0.2 against 30 for sure
  1. EU(A) = 0.2 × √100 + 0.8 × √0 = 0.2 × 10 = 2.
  2. EU(B) = √30 = 5.477. Choose B.
  3. Certainty equivalent of A: √CE = 2, so CE = 4.
  4. E[A] = 20 but CE(A) = 4, a risk premium of 16

Real world scenario: insurance

A home worth 100 lakh has a 1% chance of being destroyed. Expected loss is 1 lakh. A risk averse owner with u = ln(wealth) would pay a premium above 1 lakh to remove the risk, and the insurer, being close to risk neutral across many homes, is happy to sell at that price. The gap between the two is the risk premium, and it is why insurance exists.

Lesson 3 of 4

The St Petersburg paradox

A fair coin is tossed until the first tail. If the first tail is on toss n you win 2n−1. How much would you pay to play?

Expected moneyExam favourite
E[X] = ∑n=1∞ 12n · 2n−1 = ∑ 12 = ∞
Every toss adds exactly one half to the sum, forever.
Expected log utilityExam favourite
E[ln X] = ∑ (n − 1) ln 22n = ln 2
Since ∑ (n − 1) ÷ 2ⁿ = 1. Finite.
Its certainty equivalent
CE = eln 2 = 2
A log utility agent would pay only about 2.

The paradox is that expected money says pay any amount, yet nobody pays much. Daniel Bernoulli resolved it in 1738 with a concave, logarithmic utility. Extra money matters less the more you already have.

Why Σ (n − 1) ÷ 2ⁿ = 1
  1. Write S = Σn≥1 (n − 1) ÷ 2ⁿ = 0/2 + 1/4 + 2/8 + 3/16 + …
  2. Put m = n − 1: S = Σm≥0 m ÷ 2m+1 = ½ Σ m ÷ 2m.
  3. The known sum Σ m xᵐ = x ÷ (1 − x)² gives Σ m ÷ 2ᵐ = ½ ÷ ¼ = 2.
  4. S = ½ × 2 = 1, so E[ln X] = ln 2
In the exam
Concavity is what tames the paradox. With the convex u = x² the terms are 2ⁿ⁻² and the sum still diverges. Even √x converges, to about 1.707, which gives a certainty equivalent near 2.91.
Going deeper: Bounded utility and the real resolution

Menger showed in 1934 that for any unbounded utility you can design a super St Petersburg game whose payoffs grow fast enough to make expected utility infinite again. With log utility, pay e2ⁿ instead of 2ⁿ. A fully safe theory needs bounded utility. In practice the bank cannot pay more than it owns: with a cap of about 10¹² the expected money is only about 20.

One idea, four depths

Most people would take a sure 50 over a coin toss for 100 or nothing. Extra money feels less important the more you have, and that makes people careful with risk.

Concave u means risk averse (E[u] ≤ u(E[X]), CE ≤ E[X]), linear means neutral, convex means loving. CE = u⁻¹(E[u]). St Petersburg has infinite expected money, but log utility gives E = ln 2 and CE = 2.

The Arrow Pratt coefficient −u″(x) ÷ u′(x) measures absolute risk aversion. For a small gamble with variance σ², the risk premium is about ½ σ² times this coefficient. CRRA utilities x1−γ ÷ (1 − γ) give constant relative risk aversion γ, with ln x at γ = 1.

Rabin's calibration theorem (2000) shows that turning down small fair gambles at all wealth levels implies absurd aversion to large ones under expected utility, which supports reference dependent models. In reinforcement learning, exponential utility gives risk sensitive control and entropic risk measures.

Lesson 4 of 4

From intuition to research

Risk attitude is the shape of the utility curve. Concave means careful, convex means adventurous, straight means indifferent to risk.

Real world scenario: a farmer choosing a crop

A safe crop always earns 25. A risky crop earns 100 in a good year and 0 in a bad one, each with probability 0.5. Expected money favours the risky crop (50), but a farmer whose family must eat every year may prefer the safe one. How much safety is worth is the risk premium.

Work it out: certainty equivalent with u(x) = √x
  1. Expected utility of the risky crop: 0.5 × 10 + 0.5 × 0 = 5.
  2. Certainty equivalent: the sure amount with √CE = 5, so CE = 25.
  3. Risk premium = expected money − CE = 50 − 25 = 25.
  4. This farmer is just indifferent between the safe crop (25) and the risky one
Going deeper: the analysis

Jensen’s inequality says that for a concave u, E[u(X)] ≤ u(E[X]), so a risk averse person always values a gamble below its expected money. The Arrow and Pratt measure r(x) = −u″(x) ÷ u′(x) gives the strength of risk aversion at wealth x. For u = √x, r(x) = 1 ÷ (2x): risk aversion falls as wealth grows, matching everyday experience.

Going deeper: the research view

Risk sensitive reinforcement learning replaces expected return with measures such as conditional value at risk, the average of the worst α percent of outcomes, to build safer agents for finance, robotics and medicine. The Kelly criterion, which bets to maximise expected log wealth, is the log utility case.

Common mistake
A risk averse person may still gamble. They need the gamble to pay enough more than the sure amount, the risk premium.
