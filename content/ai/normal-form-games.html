Many agents: static games of complete information

Up to now one agent faced nature. From lecture 5 on, several rational agents face each other, and each one's payoff depends on what everyone does. The course places such interactions on two axes.

AxisOne endOther end
What do players know about each other?Complete informationIncomplete information
When do players act?Static: choose without seeing others' choicesDynamic: some players see earlier moves

This step studies the corner that is static with complete information. "Static" does not mean everyone acts at the same clock time. It means no player sees what any other player actually chose before choosing. Thinking about what the other might do is allowed. Seeing what they did is not.

Practice problem D: static or dynamic?
  1. Sealed tenders opened together after both are submitted: static. Neither bidder sees the other bid.
  2. Chess: dynamic. Every move is seen before the next one.
  3. Two candidates reveal platforms at the same moment, drafted without seeing each other: static.
  4. Poker betting, where each player sees earlier bets: dynamic

Complete information and common knowledge

Complete information means four things are common knowledge: every player's possible actions, the possible outcomes, how each combination of actions leads to an outcome, and every player's preferences. Common knowledge is stronger than everyone knowing. Everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end.

Common knowledgeExam favourite
E is common knowledge ⟺ (everyone knows)n E for every n = 1, 2, 3, …
Knowing a fact privately is mutual knowledge. Common knowledge needs the whole infinite chain.
Real world scenario: the class boycott

The professor used the course itself. Grades are relative. Suppose the whole class secretly agrees that nobody writes anything in the exam. For that to hold, each student must be sure every other student is committed, and sure that everyone else is sure, and so on. The moment anyone suspects one classmate might study, writing a little becomes very attractive under relative grading. Without common knowledge of commitment, the boycott unravels.

The normal form of a game

A static game of complete information is written as a normal form game, also called the strategic form. It has three ingredients.

Normal form gameExam favourite
⟨ N, {{Si}}, {{vi}} ⟩
N = {1, …, n} players. Sᵢ = pure strategies of player i. vᵢ = payoff of player i.
Strategy profile
s = (s₁, …, sn) ∈ S₁ × … × Sn
One choice per player. In a static game a strategy is simply an action.
Payoff functionExam favourite
vi : S₁ × … × Sn → ℝ
Your payoff depends on the whole profile, not only on your own choice.
Everyone else
s−i = (s₁, …, si−1, si+1, …, sn)
Read "s minus i". We write s = (sᵢ, s₋ᵢ).

A game is finite when there are finitely many players and each has finitely many strategies. If even one player has infinitely many, such as any quantity in [0, ∞), the game is infinite.

The bimatrix convention

For two players with finite strategies, draw a table. Rows are player 1's strategies and columns player 2's. Each cell holds two numbers: the row player's payoff first, then the column player's.

The Prisoner's Dilemma

Two suspects are held in separate rooms. Each can stay mum (M) or confess (C). Both mum: 2 years each. Both confess: 4 years each. One confesses: the confessor gets 1 year and the other 5. A year in prison is worth −1.

Player 1 (rows), Player 2 (columns)MC
M(−2, −2)(−5, −1)
C(−1, −5)(−4, −4)

Best responses and unilateral deviation

A best response is your best choice when you take the others' choices as fixed.

Best responseExam favourite
si ∈ BRi(s−i) ⟺ vi(si, s−i) ≥ vi(s′i, s−i) for all s′i
Hold everyone else fixed and maximize your own payoff.
Unilateral deviation
from s to (s′i, s−i)
Only player i changes. A profile where no unilateral deviation helps anyone is self enforcing.
Best responses in the Prisoner's Dilemma
  1. If player 2 plays M, player 1 compares −2 (M) with −1 (C). C is better.
  2. If player 2 plays C, player 1 compares −5 (M) with −4 (C). C is better again.
  3. So C is player 1's best reply whatever player 2 does. The game is symmetric, so the same holds for player 2.
  4. Both confess and get (−4, −4), although (−2, −2) was better for both
Remember
A best response is computed against fixed choices of the others. It is not a guess about what they will do. Whether the others actually play those choices is what a solution concept, in chapter 4.3, decides.
Going deeper: Static games and extensive form

A static game is a normal form game. A dynamic game is drawn as a tree, the extensive form, with information sets that group nodes a player cannot tell apart. A static game is the special case where each player's whole move sits in one information set, because she has seen nothing. Every extensive form game also has a normal form, whose strategies are full contingent plans, as in the R&D tree of chapter 3.1.

One idea, four depths

In a game, what you get depends on what others choose too. A table lists every combination of choices and what each player gets from it.

A normal form game has players N, strategy sets Sᵢ and payoffs vᵢ on profiles. Static means unobserved choices, not same time. Complete information makes actions, outcomes, the map and preferences common knowledge. Best responses hold s₋ᵢ fixed.

Common knowledge is the fixed point of the everyone knows operator. Aumann's agreement theorem and the muddy children puzzle show how public announcements create it. Normal forms of extensive games can be reduced by merging equivalent strategies.

Multi agent reinforcement learning builds empirical normal form games from trained policies (PSRO, Lanctot et al. 2017) and computes best responses with RL. Epistemic game theory studies which solution concepts follow from which assumptions about knowledge and rationality.
