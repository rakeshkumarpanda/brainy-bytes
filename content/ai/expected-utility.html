Lesson 1 of 4

Expected utility

Once each action is a lottery, how do we compare two lotteries? The course uses the most common answer: weight each outcome's utility by its probability and add. The result is the expected utility of the action, and the rational agent picks the action with the largest.

Expected utility, discreteExam favourite
EU(a) = ∑i P( xi | a ) u( xi )
Probability times utility, summed over outcomes.
Expected utility, continuous
EU(a) = ∫ u(x) dFa(x) = ∫ u(x) fa(x) dx
The second form needs a density.
Rational choice under riskExam favourite
a* ∈ arg maxa E[ u(X) | a ]
Maximize expected utility, not expected money.

Expected payoff is not expected outcome

The professor stressed this. Multiply probability by the utility of the outcome, not by the outcome itself. The two agree only when u(x) = x.

Practice problems 5 and 6: 100 with 0.3, 40 with 0.7
  1. With u(x) = x: EU = 0.3 × 100 + 0.7 × 40 = 30 + 28 = 58.
  2. With u(x) = √x: u(100) = 10 and u(40) = 6.325.
  3. EU = 0.3 × 10 + 0.7 × 6.325 = 3 + 4.427 = 7.43.
  4. u(E[X]) = √58 = 7.616, which is not E[u(X)] = 7.43
Practice problem 9: compare two risky actions
  1. Action A: 20 with 0.4, 0 with 0.6, so EU(A) = 8.
  2. Action B: 12 with 0.8, 0 with 0.2, so EU(B) = 9.6.
  3. 9.6 > 8, choose B
  4. B has the smaller prize but a much better chance of getting it.
Lesson 2 of 4

Compound lotteries: the R&D example

A firm decides whether to invest in research (action G) or not (action S). Research succeeds with probability 0.625. After success the market is good with probability 0.9. After failure it is good with probability 0.5. A good market pays 10 and a bad one 0. Without research the market is good with probability 0.5.

Action G is a compound lottery: a lottery whose prizes are themselves lotteries. To compare it with S, reduce it to a simple lottery over final payoffs.

Reduction of compound lotteriesExam favourite
P(final outcome) = ∑paths ∏branches on the path P(branch)
Multiply along each path, then add the paths that end at the same outcome.
Reduce action G
  1. Success and good: 0.625 × 0.9 = 0.5625.
  2. Success and bad: 0.625 × 0.1 = 0.0625.
  3. Failure and good: 0.375 × 0.5 = 0.1875. Failure and bad: 0.1875.
  4. Check: 0.5625 + 0.0625 + 0.1875 + 0.1875 = 1.
  5. P(10 | G) = 0.5625 + 0.1875 = 0.75, so EU(G) = 7.5 against EU(S) = 5

Other ways to compare lotteries

Here every rule agrees. They need not. The course uses expected utility because it has a clean mathematical justification, which is the von Neumann and Morgenstern theorem in the deep box below.

Lesson 3 of 4

Why ranking is not enough under risk

Chapter 1.2 showed that any increasing transformation of a utility function keeps the ranking. Under risk that is no longer true. Lecture 2 gives a sharp example.

Same ranking, opposite choice
  1. Outcomes A, B, C, D with u = 9, −1, 10, 0. The ranking is C ≻ A ≻ D ≻ B.
  2. G gives A with 0.75 and B with 0.25. S gives C with 0.5 and D with 0.5.
  3. EU(G) = 0.75 × 9 + 0.25 × (−1) = 6.5 and EU(S) = 5. Choose G.
  4. Now change only u(B) to −8. The ranking C ≻ A ≻ D ≻ B is unchanged.
  5. EU(G) = 6.75 − 2 = 4.75 < EU(S) = 5. Now choose S.
Positive affine transformationExam favourite
v(x) = a u(x) + b,   a > 0
The only transformations that keep every expected utility comparison.
Why it works
E[ a u(X) + b ] = a E[ u(X) ] + b
Expectation is linear. Scaling by a positive a and shifting by b keeps the order of the expected values.
In the exam
Moving u(B) from −1 to −8 is not affine: it stretches one gap and leaves the others alone. Under risk the sizes of the gaps matter, so expected utility needs cardinal utility, unique up to a u + b with a > 0.
Going deeper: The von Neumann and Morgenstern theorem

Suppose a preference over lotteries is complete and transitive, continuous (if L ≻ M ≻ N there is a mix of L and N indifferent to M) and independent (mixing both sides with the same third lottery does not change the comparison). Then there is a utility u over outcomes such that L ⪰ M exactly when EL[u] ≥ EM[u], and u is unique up to positive affine transformations. Independence is the axiom that the Allais paradox shows people often break.

A look ahead: money later is worth less

If research pays off in t years, its payoff should be discounted. With interest rate r, a future value FV is worth PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)t today. Chapter 3.2 develops this fully.

Present value
PV = FV(1 + r)t
For a risky payoff: E[u(Xt)] ÷ (1 + r)t.
One idea, four depths

To compare two gambles, multiply what each result is worth to you by how likely it is, add up, and pick the bigger total.

EU(a) = Σ P(xᵢ | a) u(xᵢ). Reduce compound lotteries by multiplying along paths. Expected utility choices survive a u + b with a > 0 but not other increasing transforms, so utility under risk is cardinal.

The von Neumann and Morgenstern axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) are exactly what is needed for an expected utility representation. Reduction of compound lotteries is an extra assumption that experiments on ambiguity question.

The Allais paradox breaks independence, and Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory replaces probabilities with decision weights and utility with a value function around a reference point. Rank dependent utility and Choquet expected utility are formal models that relax independence.

Lesson 4 of 4

From intuition to research

Expected utility weights the value of each outcome by its probability. Here it is followed from a phone insurance decision to the axioms behind it and the paradoxes that test it.

Real world scenario: insuring a phone

A phone worth 100 has a 50 percent chance of breaking this year. Expected money without insurance is 50. Many people still pay more than 50 to be covered for sure, because losing everything hurts more than its money value suggests. Expected utility, not expected money, explains this.

Work it out: sure 40 or a coin toss for 100 or 0, with u(x) = √x
  1. Expected money of the toss: 0.5 × 100 = 50, more than 40.
  2. Expected utility of the toss: 0.5 × √100 + 0.5 × 0 = 5.
  3. Utility of the sure amount: √40 = 6.32.
  4. 6.32 > 5, so this person takes the sure 40
Going deeper: the analysis

The von Neumann and Morgenstern theorem: if preferences over lotteries are complete, transitive, continuous and satisfy independence (mixing two lotteries with a third in the same proportion keeps their order), then there is a utility u such that lottery L is preferred to M exactly when EL[u] ≥ EM[u]. The utility is unique up to a positive affine change a·u + b with a > 0, which is why cardinal differences now matter.

Going deeper: the research view

The Allais paradox shows real people break the independence axiom, and prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979) models this with reference points and distorted probability weights. In AI, an agent that maximises expected reward is an expected utility maximiser, so choosing the reward function is choosing its vN-M utility.

Common mistake
Expected value and expected utility differ whenever u is not a straight line. Write which one a question asks for.
