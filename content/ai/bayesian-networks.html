Bayesian networks: a graph plus small tables

Source: Pearl (1988), Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems, and AIMA 3rd ed., sections 14.1 and 14.2 (4th ed., 13.1 and 13.2). The burglary network is AIMA figure 14.2.

What it is
A Bayesian network is a directed graph with no cycles. Each node is a random variable. An arrow X → Y means X directly influences Y. Each node has a conditional probability table (CPT) giving P(node | its parents).
Why we need it
The full joint has 2ⁿ entries. Most variables only depend directly on a few others. The network stores only those local tables, so a model of hundreds of variables fits in a few thousand numbers, and experts can read and check it.
How it works
The joint probability of any world is the product of one entry from each table: P(x₁, ..., xₙ) = Π P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ)). This is the chain rule, with each condition cut down to the parents.
Network semanticsExam favourite
P(x₁, ..., xₙ) = Π over i of P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ))
One table entry per variable.
Size of a CPT
a true or false node with k true or false parents needs 2ᵏ numbers
One number per row, since P(false) = 1 − P(true).
Size of the network
Σ over nodes of 2^(number of parents)
n nodes with at most k parents: at most n 2ᵏ, against 2ⁿ − 1.

Worked example: P(j, m, a, ¬b, ¬e)
  1. Take one entry from each table: P(j | a) = 0.90, P(m | a) = 0.70, P(a | ¬b, ¬e) = 0.001.
  2. P(¬b) = 0.999, P(¬e) = 0.998.
  3. Multiply: 0.90 × 0.70 × 0.001 × 0.999 × 0.998.
  4. ≈ 0.000628
Remember
Build a network in causal order: causes first, then effects. The diagnostic order (MaryCalls, JohnCalls, Alarm, Burglary, Earthquake) is still correct but needs more arrows and harder numbers, such as P(Earthquake | Burglary, Alarm).

Reading independence from the graph: d-separation

Source: Pearl (1988), Geiger, Verma and Pearl (1990), and AIMA 3rd ed., section 14.2.2.

What it is
The graph says which variables are conditionally independent. Each node is independent of its non descendants given its parents. More generally, X and Y are independent given Z when Z d-separates them: every path between them is blocked.
Why we need it
With d-separation we can see from the drawing alone which evidence matters for a query. Inference programs use it to skip whole parts of the network.
How it works
Check each path three nodes at a time. A chain A → M → B or a fork A ← M → B is blocked when M is observed. A collider A → M ← B is blocked unless M or one of its descendants is observed.

Given its blanket, a node is independent of every other node.
Remember
Explaining away: if the alarm rang, a burglary is likely. If we then learn an earthquake happened, the burglary becomes less likely, because the earthquake already explains the alarm. The two causes were independent before we saw the alarm.
Lesson 3 of 8

Compact tables: deterministic nodes, noisy OR and continuous variables

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 14.3, and Pearl (1988) for noisy OR.

What it is
Some CPTs follow a simple rule, so they need far fewer than 2ᵏ numbers. A noisy OR node has a separate chance qᵢ that cause i fails to produce the effect, and the failures are independent.
Why we need it
A disease with 10 possible causes needs 1024 rows in a full CPT. Noisy OR needs 10 numbers, which an expert can actually give.
How it works
The effect is false only if every present cause fails: P(¬effect | present causes) = product of their qᵢ. Deterministic nodes are fixed functions of their parents. Continuous nodes use formulas, such as a linear Gaussian whose mean is a linear function of the parents.
Noisy ORExam favourite
P(¬x | parents) = Π over true parents j of q_j
q_j is the chance that cause j alone fails.
Linear Gaussian
P(x | h) = N(x | mean a h + b, variance σ²)
A continuous child of a continuous parent h.
Worked example: fever with q_cold = 0.6, q_flu = 0.2, q_malaria = 0.1 (AIMA)
  1. Cold and flu present, no malaria.
  2. P(¬fever) = 0.6 × 0.2 = 0.12.
  3. P(fever | cold, flu, ¬malaria) = 1 − 0.12 = 0.88
Lesson 4 of 8

Undirected models: Markov networks

Source: Kindermann and Snell (1980), Koller and Friedman (2009), Probabilistic Graphical Models, chapter 4 (the misconception example), and AIMA 4th ed., section 13.2.

What it is
A Markov network (Markov random field) is an undirected graph. Each clique (a set of nodes all joined to each other) has a factor φ, a table of non negative numbers that scores how well the values fit together.
Why we need it
Some relations have no direction: neighbouring pixels in an image tend to share a colour, friends tend to share opinions. Forcing arrows on them gives the wrong independencies. Undirected models state them directly.
How it works
Multiply the factors of a world to get its score. Divide by the partition function Z, the sum of the scores of all worlds, to get a probability. Two sets of nodes are independent given S when every path between them passes through S.
Gibbs distributionExam favourite
P(x) = (1 ÷ Z) Π over cliques c of φ_c(x_c)
Factors need not add up to 1.
Partition function
Z = Σ over all worlds x of Π φ_c(x_c)
A sum with 2ⁿ terms: computing it is the hard part.
Hammersley Clifford theorem
a positive distribution has the independencies of the graph exactly when it factorises over the cliques
Links the graph to the formula.
Log linear (energy) form
P(x) = (1 ÷ Z) exp(Σ wᵢ fᵢ(x))
Used by conditional random fields and Boltzmann machines.

Remember
To turn a Bayesian network into a Markov network, moralise it: join the parents of each child (they "marry"), then drop the arrow heads. The result may lose some independencies, such as Burglary ⫫ Earthquake.

The maths: counting parameters and checking independence

Parameters with at most k parentsExam favourite
n 2ᵏ, against 2ⁿ − 1 for the full joint
n = 30, k = 5: 960 against about 1.07 × 10⁹.
Local Markov property
X ⫫ NonDescendants(X) | Parents(X)
Equivalent to the product formula for a Bayesian network.
Markov blanket property
P(X | everything else) = P(X | MB(X)) ∝ P(X | parents) × Π over children Y of P(y | parents(Y))
Used by Gibbs sampling in chapter 9.3.
Moral ancestral graph test
X ⫫ Y | Z exactly when X and Y are not joined in the moralised graph of the ancestors of X, Y and Z, after removing Z
Lauritzen and others, 1990. This is the algorithm in the code above.
Worked example: P(Cloudy, ¬Sprinkler, Rain, WetGrass) in the sprinkler network
  1. P(Cloudy) = 0.5.
  2. P(¬Sprinkler | Cloudy) = 1 − 0.1 = 0.9. P(Rain | Cloudy) = 0.8.
  3. P(WetGrass | ¬Sprinkler, Rain) = 0.9.
  4. 0.5 × 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.324
Worked example: Z for the chain A - B - C where each factor gives 4 to equal values and 1 to different values
  1. Worlds 000 and 111 score 4 × 4 = 16 each: 32.
  2. Worlds with one disagreement (001, 011, 100, 110) score 4 × 1 = 4 each: 16.
  3. Worlds 010 and 101 disagree twice and score 1 each: 2.
  4. Z = 32 + 16 + 2 = 50

Bottom line: use a Bayesian network when causes and effects are clear, a Markov network for symmetric relations, and a factor graph inside inference code.

Real world scenario: Bayesian networks in real use

Microsoft’s Windows printer troubleshooter (1990s) used a Bayesian network to pick the next question to ask. The Pathfinder system (Heckerman, 1991) diagnosed lymph node diseases at expert level. The CTBT monitoring system NET-VISA (Arora, Russell and Sudderth, 2013), which detects nuclear tests from seismic signals, is a large probabilistic model of this family. Image segmentation and part of speech tagging used Markov random fields and conditional random fields (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira, 2001).

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Draw arrows from causes to effects, and for each effect write how likely it is for each mix of its causes. The drawing tells you what can affect what.

A Bayesian network is a DAG with CPTs, and the joint is the product of the CPT entries. d-separation (chain, fork, collider) reads independence from the graph. Markov networks use clique factors and Z.

I-maps and perfect maps: some distributions have no perfect directed map and some no perfect undirected map. Markov equivalence: graphs with the same skeleton and the same colliders (v-structures) encode the same independencies, so data alone cannot always fix arrow directions.

Research: learning the structure of a network from data (score based search, the PC algorithm), deep generative models seen as graphical models (variational autoencoders), and relational models (Markov logic networks) that mix first order logic with Markov networks.

Lesson 8 of 8

Chapter summary

  1. A Bayesian network is a directed graph with no cycles plus a CPT per node. P(x₁, ..., xₙ) = Π P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ)).
  2. It needs Σ 2^(parents) numbers instead of 2ⁿ − 1. The burglary network needs 10 instead of 31.
  3. d-separation: chains and forks are blocked by observing the middle, colliders are opened by observing the middle or a descendant.
  4. Noisy OR, deterministic and linear Gaussian nodes make tables even smaller.
  5. Markov networks use clique factors: P(x) = (1 ÷ Z) Π φ_c(x_c). Z is a sum over all worlds, which is what makes them hard.
