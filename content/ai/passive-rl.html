Lesson 1 of 7

Passive learning: judge a fixed policy without a model

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., sections 21.1 and 21.2 (4th ed., 22.1 and 22.2), Sutton and Barto (2018), Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, chapters 5 and 6. The uploaded slides and transcripts do not cover this step.

What it is
Reinforcement learning (RL) learns to act well from rewards alone, without being told the transition model P or the reward function R. In passive RL the policy π is fixed, and the agent only learns how good it is: the utilities U^π(s).
Why we need it
In most real problems nobody can write down P and R: a robot does not know how its wheels slip on a new floor, a game program does not know how its opponents play. The agent must learn from trials.
How it works
Run trials from a start state to an end state, following π, and record the states and rewards seen. Then use them in one of three ways: average what followed each state (direct estimation), learn P and R and solve the Bellman equations (adaptive dynamic programming), or nudge each U(s) towards what happened next (temporal difference learning).
MethodWhat it learnsUpdateSpeed
Direct utility estimationU(s) as an averageaverage of all rewards to go seen from sslow: ignores links between states
Adaptive dynamic programming (ADP)P̂(s′ | s, π(s)) and R(s)solve U(s) = R(s) + γ Σ P̂ U(s′) after each stepfastest per trial, costly per step
Temporal difference (TD)U(s) directlyU(s) ← U(s) + α (R(s) + γ U(s′) − U(s))cheap per step, a bit slower than ADP
Worked example: direct estimation of U(1,1) from the three textbook trials
  1. Trial 1 visits (1,1) once, then 6 more squares before reaching +1: reward to go = 7 × (−0.04) + 1 = 0.72.
  2. Trial 2 also gives 0.72.
  3. Trial 3 goes (1,1), (2,1), (3,1), (3,2) and ends at −1: 4 × (−0.04) − 1 = −1.16.
  4. U(1,1) ≈ (0.72 + 0.72 − 1.16) ÷ 3 ≈ 0.0933, far from the true 0.705 after only 3 trials
Lesson 2 of 7

Temporal difference learning

Source: Sutton (1988), Learning to predict by the methods of temporal differences, and AIMA 3rd ed., section 21.2.3.

What it is
TD learning updates the guess U(s) after every single step, using the guess for the next state: the target is R(s) + γ U(s′). The difference between the target and the old guess is the TD error.
Why we need it
It needs no model and no waiting until the end of a trial. Because the target uses U(s′), information about rewards flows backwards from state to state, much faster than averaging whole trials.
How it works
After the move s → s′, compute δ = R(s) + γ U(s′) − U(s), then set U(s) ← U(s) + α δ. With a learning rate α that shrinks the right way, U converges to U^π.
TD(0) updateExam favourite
U(s) ← U(s) + α (R(s) + γ U(s′) − U(s))
α is the learning rate.
TD error
δ = R(s) + γ U(s′) − U(s)
Positive: things went better than expected.
Learning rate schedule
Σ αₙ = ∞ and Σ αₙ² < ∞, for example αₙ = 1 ÷ n or 60 ÷ (59 + n)
Robbins and Monro (1951). Then TD converges.
TD(λ)
U(x) ← U(x) + α δ e(x), with traces e(x) that decay by γ λ each step
λ = 0 is TD(0), λ = 1 is like averaging whole trials.
Worked example: one TD update
  1. U(s) = 0.5, the agent moves to s′ with U(s′) = 0.8, R(s) = −0.04, γ = 1, α = 0.1.
  2. TD error δ = −0.04 + 0.8 − 0.5 = 0.26.
  3. U(s) ← 0.5 + 0.1 × 0.26.
  4. U(s) = 0.526
Remember
TD learning from experience is the same idea as the Bellman update, but it uses the one next state that actually happened instead of an average over all next states. Over many steps, states are reached in proportion to P(s′ | s, a), so the averages come out right.
Lesson 3 of 7

Adaptive dynamic programming

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 21.2.2, and Moore and Atkeson (1993) for prioritised sweeping.

What it is
An ADP agent learns the transition model from the counts of what happened, then solves the Bellman equations of the learned MDP for the fixed policy.
Why we need it
It uses every bit of the data: each observed move updates the model and, through the equations, all utilities at once. It learns from the fewest trials.
How it works
Keep counts N(s, a, s′). Set P̂(s′ | s, a) = N(s, a, s′) ÷ N(s, a). After each step, run policy evaluation on (P̂, R). Prioritised sweeping updates first the states whose values are most likely to change.
Learned modelExam favourite
P̂(s′ | s, a) = N(s, a, s′) ÷ N(s, a)
Maximum likelihood from counts.
Worked example: the model from the three trials
  1. (1,3) was left 3 times with the action Right: twice to (2,3), once to (1,2).
  2. P̂((2,3) | (1,3), Right) = 2 ÷ 3.
  3. (3,3) was left 3 times with Right: twice to (4,3), once to (3,2).
  4. P̂((4,3) | (3,3), Right) = 2 ÷ 3, close to the true 0.8 only after more trials

The maths: bias, variance and fixed points

Monte Carlo target
Gₜ = Rₜ + γ Rₜ₊₁ + γ² Rₜ₊₂ + ...
Unbiased, but high variance: it depends on every later random step.
TD targetExam favourite
Rₜ + γ U(Sₜ₊₁)
Biased while U is wrong, but low variance: it depends on one step.
Eligibility trace
e(s) ← γ λ e(s), then e(Sₜ) ← e(Sₜ) + 1
Credit fades by γλ per step.
Batch TD answer
the values of the maximum likelihood Markov model of the data
Batch Monte Carlo gives the averages of the observed returns instead.
Worked example: batch TD versus Monte Carlo (Sutton and Barto, example 6.4)
  1. Eight episodes: once A, 0, B, 0. Six times B, 1. Once B, 0. (γ = 1)
  2. Both methods: U(B) = 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75.
  3. Monte Carlo: the only episode through A returned 0, so U(A) = 0.
  4. Batch TD: A always went to B with reward 0, so U(A) = 0 + U(B).
  5. U(A) = 0.75 by TD, 0 by Monte Carlo. TD uses the Markov structure.

Bottom line: TD learning is the usual choice: almost as data efficient as ADP, and as cheap as direct estimation.

Real world scenario: TD-Gammon

Gerald Tesauro’s TD-Gammon (1992 to 1995) learned backgammon by playing about 1.5 million games against itself, with a neural network trained by TD(λ) to predict the chance of winning. It reached the level of the best human players and changed how experts play some opening moves.

Lesson 6 of 7

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

You learn how good a place is by going there many times. TD learning updates your opinion of a place as soon as you see where it led, not only at the end of the trip.

Passive RL: fixed π, learn U^π. Direct estimation averages rewards to go. ADP learns P̂ and solves the equations. TD(0): U(s) ← U(s) + α(R + γU(s′) − U(s)).

Robbins Monro conditions for convergence. TD as stochastic approximation of the Bellman operator. Bias and variance of TD and Monte Carlo targets. TD(λ) and n step returns as a family between them.

Research: off policy evaluation (estimating a new policy from old logs), TD with neural networks and the deadly triad (function approximation, bootstrapping and off policy data can diverge), and distributional RL that learns the whole return distribution.

Lesson 7 of 7

Chapter summary

  1. Passive RL: the policy is fixed, the agent learns U^π(s) from trials without knowing P or R.
  2. Direct estimation averages rewards to go. Three textbook trials give U(1,1) ≈ 0.093.
  3. ADP learns P̂(s′ | s, a) = N(s, a, s′) ÷ N(s, a) and solves the Bellman equations. Most data efficient.
  4. TD(0): U(s) ← U(s) + α (R(s) + γ U(s′) − U(s)). It converges with a learning rate that satisfies Robbins Monro.
  5. TD targets have lower variance than Monte Carlo returns but are biased while U is wrong.
