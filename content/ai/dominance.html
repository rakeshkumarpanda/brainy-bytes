Lesson 1 of 4

Strict domination

Sometimes one of your strategies is better than another whatever the others do. Then you never need to guess what they will do to know you should not play the worse one.

Strict dominationExam favourite
si ▷i s′i ⟺ vi(si, s−i) > vi(s′i, s−i) for every s−i
Only player i's own payoffs are compared, once for each situation the others can create.
Weak domination
si ⊵i s′i ⟺ ≥ for every s−i
Textbooks add "and > for at least one s₋ᵢ". State which version you use in an exam.
Common mistake
The mistake made twice in class: checking player 1 by mixing in the second number of each cell, or by moving down a column. Rule: for the row player compare first numbers, moving across columns. For the column player compare second numbers, moving down rows. Moving along the other player's strategies is exactly "for every s₋ᵢ".
Lecture examples
  1. Prisoner's Dilemma: against M, C gives −1 > −2. Against C, −4 > −5. So C ▷₁ M, and by symmetry C ▷₂ M.
  2. With altruism ½ the table is (−3, −3), (−5.5, −3.5), (−3.5, −5.5), (−6, −6). Now −3 > −3.5 and −5.5 > −6, so M ▷₁ C. The direction reversed.
  3. Rock, Paper, Scissors: each strategy is the only best reply to something, so nothing dominates anything, not even weakly.
  4. Marketing War: M ▷₁ L (8 > 6, 4 > 2, 1 > 0), but M and H are not comparable (1 < 2 against H)

The difference matters. A dominant strategy, needed by the first solution concept, is rare. A dominated strategy, which is what elimination deletes, is common. A student thought a column could only be deleted if every other column beat it. One dominator is enough, because a rational player would switch to it. With only two strategies dominating equals dominant, which is why the Prisoner's Dilemma hides the difference.

Theorem 1: strict domination is a strict partial order
  1. Irreflexive: sᵢ ▷ sᵢ would need a number strictly bigger than itself.
  2. Asymmetric: sᵢ ▷ s′ᵢ and s′ᵢ ▷ sᵢ would give x > y and y > x at the same s₋ᵢ.
  3. Transitive: for every s₋ᵢ, a > b and b > c give a > c.
  4. Not complete: M and H in the Marketing War, or any pair in RPS, are incomparable
Going deeper: Domination by a mixed strategy

Player 1 has U = (3, 0), M = (0, 3) and D = (1, 1) against (L, R). Neither U nor M dominates D. But the 50/50 mix of U and M earns 1.5 against L and 1.5 against R, more than D's 1 in both. D is strictly dominated by a mixture. This course uses pure strategies for now, and a fuller elimination later removes such strategies too.

Real world scenario: the voting correction

In class, voting for your favourite candidate was offered as a weakly dominant strategy in plurality voting with alphabetical tie breaks. It is not. Voter 3 ranks c ≻ b ≻ a. Voter 1 votes a and voter 2 votes b. Voting sincerely for c makes a three way tie that a wins, her worst outcome. Voting for b makes b win. What is true is that voting for your least favourite is weakly dominated. The Gibbard Satterthwaite theorem says every non dictatorial rule with three or more candidates sometimes rewards insincere voting.

The dominant strategy equilibrium

Strict dominant strategy equilibriumExam favourite
sD with siD strictly dominant for every player i
Everyone plays the strategy that is best against everything. No one needs to predict anyone.
Rational players and dominated strategiesExam favourite
Eμ[vi(si)] − Eμ[vi(s′i)] = ∑ μ(s−i)[vi(si, s−i) − vi(s′i, s−i)] > 0
Theorem 4: whatever the belief μ, a strictly dominated strategy has lower expected payoff, so a rational player never uses it.

Examples: the Prisoner's Dilemma gives (C, C) with (−4, −4). With altruism it gives (M, M) with (−3, −3). Rock, Paper, Scissors and the Marketing War have none.

Theorem 3: a strict dominant strategy equilibrium is unique (the proof many students missed the first time)
  1. Assume two different ones, s and s′. Then some player j has sⱼ ≠ s′ⱼ.
  2. In s, sⱼ is strictly dominant, so it beats s′ⱼ: vⱼ(sⱼ, s₋ⱼ) > vⱼ(s′ⱼ, s₋ⱼ) for all s₋ⱼ.
  3. In s′, s′ⱼ is strictly dominant, so vⱼ(s′ⱼ, s₋ⱼ) > vⱼ(sⱼ, s₋ⱼ) for all s₋ⱼ.
  4. Fix one s₋ⱼ. The first line says x > y and the second y > x. Impossible.
  5. So there is at most one. With ≥ instead of > the argument breaks, and weak ones can be many.

Going deeper: Which payoff changes cannot affect dominance

Dominance compares player i's own payoffs with s₋ᵢ held fixed. So it is unchanged by any strictly increasing f(vᵢ), and by adding any term h(s₋ᵢ) that depends only on the others: f(vᵢ(sᵢ, s₋ᵢ)) + h(s₋ᵢ) > f(vᵢ(s′ᵢ, s₋ᵢ)) + h(s₋ᵢ) exactly when vᵢ(sᵢ, s₋ᵢ) > vᵢ(s′ᵢ, s₋ᵢ). Altruism is not of this form because vⱼ depends on player i's own choice too, which is why it could flip the prediction.

One idea, four depths

If one choice is better than another no matter what anyone else does, never make the worse choice. If one choice beats all the others, make it.

sᵢ ▷ s′ᵢ needs vᵢ larger for every s₋ᵢ. Dominant beats all, dominated is beaten by one. A strict DSE is unique when it exists, but often does not exist, is not robust, and can be Pareto dominated.

Strict domination by pure or mixed strategies is equivalent to never being a best response to any belief (for two players, via linear programming duality). Dominant strategy implementation is the strongest form of incentive compatibility in mechanism design.

Strategy proof mechanisms, where truth telling is dominant, include the Vickrey auction and its generalization VCG. The Gibbard Satterthwaite theorem limits them for voting, and work on large markets and approximate strategy proofness studies how close practical systems such as school choice can get.

Lesson 4 of 4

From intuition to research

A dominated strategy is never the right choice. This lesson adds a case the pure check misses and shows where dominance drives real systems.

Real world scenario: the free umbrella

If carrying an umbrella costs nothing and keeps you dry when it rains, carrying it is at least as good as leaving it in every kind of weather, and strictly better when it rains. Leaving it is weakly dominated.

Work it out: domination by a mixture
  1. Row payoffs against two columns: A (3, 0), B (0, 3), C (1, 1).
  2. No pure strategy beats C in both columns: A loses to C in column 2, B loses in column 1.
  3. Mix A and B with probability 0.5 each: (1.5, 1.5).
  4. 1.5 > 1 in both columns, so C is strictly dominated by the mixture
Going deeper: the analysis

A strategy can survive every pure dominance check and still be dominated by a mixed strategy. A rational player never uses a strictly dominated strategy, and in two player games a strategy is never a best response to any belief exactly when it is strictly dominated, possibly by a mixture. This equivalence is the link between dominance and rationalisability.

Going deeper: the research view

Mechanism design builds rules where telling the truth is a dominant strategy, called dominant strategy incentive compatibility. The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves mechanism achieves it for efficient allocation, and variants run in online advertising and spectrum auctions.

Common mistake
Strict and weak dominance behave differently. Removing weakly dominated strategies can remove equilibria and depends on the order of removal.
