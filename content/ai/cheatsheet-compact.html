Step 1Advanced AI · Rational choice

1.1Decision problems and preferences

  • Decision problem (A, X, c, ⪰): actions, outcomes, consequence map c : A → X, preference. Utility goes on outcomes, v(a) = u(c(a)).
  • Three relations Weak x ⪰ y. Strict x ≻ y means x ⪰ y and not y ⪰ x. Indifference x ∼ y means both directions.
  • Rationality Complete (every pair compared) plus transitive (rankings chain). Cycles have no best element and allow a money pump.
  • What carries over Transitivity of ⪰ passes to ≻ and ∼. Completeness does not pass to either.
  • Indifference is not incompleteness Indifferent: equally good. Incomplete: cannot compare. Equality: the same object.
  • A decision problem( A, X, c, ⪰ )
  • Weak preferencex ⪰ y
  • Strict preferencex ≻ y ⟺ x ⪰ y and not y ⪰ x
  • Indifferencex ∼ y ⟺ x ⪰ y and y ⪰ x
  • Completenessfor all x, y ∈ X:   x ⪰ y or y ⪰ x
  • Transitivityx ⪰ y and y ⪰ z ⟹ x ⪰ z
  • Trap Equality and indifference are different ideas. Equality means two things are the same object. Indifference means two different outcomes are judged equally good. Tea and coffee are not equal, but you may be indifferent between them.
  • Trap Completeness does not mean you like every option. It means you can compare any two, possibly by saying you are indifferent.

1.2Utility functions

  • Representation x ⪰ y ⟺ u(x) ≥ u(y). Strict gives >, indifference gives =. Not "=" for weak preference.
  • Ordinal Only order matters. Any strictly increasing f gives f(u), another valid utility. Gaps and ratios mean nothing.
  • Existence Finite X + complete + transitive ⟹ a utility exists. Build it by peeling off top classes: A ≻ B ∼ C ≻ D gives 3, 2, 2, 1.
  • Best exists In finite X an endless chain of improvements must cycle, which transitivity forbids. Ties at the top are allowed.
  • Counting n strict outcomes give n! rankings (5! = 120). With ties: 1, 3, 13, 75 weak orders.
  • Representationx ⪰ y ⟺ u(x) ≥ u(y)
  • Ordinal invarianceif f is strictly increasing,   v(x) = f( u(x) ) represents the same ⪰
  • Existence theoremX finite and ⪰ complete and transitive ⟹ some u : X → ℝ represents ⪰
  • Trap The slide transcript once read "u(x) = u(y) ⟺ x ⪰ y". That is a transcription slip. The correct condition for weak preference uses ≥. Equality belongs to indifference only.
  • Trap With ordinal utility, differences mean nothing. u(A) − u(B) = 2 does not say A is liked twice as much as some other pair with difference 1.

1.3Choosing the best action

  • Rational choice v(a) = u(c(a)). a* ∈ arg max v, i.e. v(a*) ≥ v(a) for all a. Ties are all rational.
  • Movie tree Utilities 6 to 1, minus 1 per mile. INOX 1 mile, PVR 3. Best: INOX Aliens, net 5.
  • Fruit trees 1.6 Plain: AA at X − 60. Second same tree saves half: AA, OO become X + 5, PP X + 22.5, best AP at X − 55.
  • Parks 1.7 v = √(400c) − c/80 gives √c = 800, c* = 640,000 under the 1,000,000 cap. With √(1600c) the peak 2,560,000 is infeasible, so c* = 1,000,000.
  • Other ideas Satisficing picks the first v ≥ T. Minimizing loss = maximizing −loss. Check feasibility and boundaries.
  • Value of an actionv(a) = u( c(a) )
  • Rational choicea* ∈ arg maxa ∈ A v(a)   ⟺   v(a*) ≥ v(a) for every a
  • Spending after a planu(plan) = X + Σ maintenance − Σ reduction
  • Benefitv(c) = √400c − c80 = 20√c − c80
  • First order conditionv′(c) = 10√c − 180 = 0
  • Trap The transcript sometimes wrote v(a*) > v(a) for every other a. That strict version only fits when the best action is unique. With ties the right condition is ≥.
  • Trap Setting the derivative to zero is not enough. Check the second derivative and the end points of the allowed range.

Step 2Advanced AI · Choice under risk

2.1Lotteries and distributions

  • Lottery Action a gives P(xᵢ | a) = pᵢ with pᵢ in [0, 1] and Σ pᵢ = 1. Nature picks the outcome.
  • CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x | a). Non decreasing, 0 at −∞, 1 at +∞, right continuous. F(50) = 0.7 is "at most 50".
  • Intervals P(r < X ≤ s) = F(s) − F(r). Notes example 0.8 − 0.3 = 0.5.
  • Density F = ∫ f, f = F′. P(X = x) = 0 for continuous X. Densities can exceed 1.
  • Compound paths Multiply along each path, add the paths that reach the same outcome: 0.6 × 0.8 + 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.6.
  • Lottery of an actionP( xi | a ) = pi,   0 ≤ pi ≤ 1,   ∑ pi = 1
  • CDF of an actionFa(x) = P( X ≤ x | a )
  • Interval probabilityP( r < X ≤ s | a ) = Fa(s) − Fa(r)
  • From density to CDFFa(x) = ∫−∞x fa(t) dt
  • Trap F(50) = 0.7 means a 70% chance the outcome is at most 50. It does not mean a 70% chance the outcome is exactly 50. This misreading is the most common exam error on CDFs.
  • Trap A density can be larger than 1. The uniform density on [0, 0.5] equals 2. Only the area under it must be 1.

2.2Expected utility and compound lotteries

  • Expected utility EU(a) = Σ P(xᵢ | a) u(xᵢ), or ∫ u dF. Choose arg max EU, not arg max E[X].
  • R&D compound lottery P(10 | G) = 0.625 × 0.9 + 0.375 × 0.5 = 0.75, EU(G) = 7.5 > EU(S) = 5. Multiply along paths, add paths.
  • Reversal u = 9, −1, 10, 0: EU(G) 6.5 > 5. u(B) = −8: 4.75 < 5. Same ranking, opposite choice.
  • Affine invariance Only v = a u + b with a > 0 keeps every EU comparison. Utility under risk is cardinal.
  • Other rules and PV Optimistic (P best), pessimistic (P worst). PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ. vNM axioms give EU.
  • Expected utility, discreteEU(a) = ∑i P( xi | a ) u( xi )
  • Rational choice under riska* ∈ arg maxa E[ u(X) | a ]
  • Reduction of compound lotteriesP(final outcome) = ∑paths ∏branches on the path P(branch)
  • Positive affine transformationv(x) = a u(x) + b,   a > 0
  • Exam Moving u(B) from −1 to −8 is not affine: it stretches one gap and leaves the others alone. Under risk the sizes of the gaps matter, so expected utility needs cardinal utility, unique up to a u + b with a > 0.
  • Trap Expected value and expected utility differ whenever u is not a straight line. Write which one a question asks for.

2.3Risk attitudes and the St Petersburg paradox

  • Three attitudes Concave u (ln, √): averse, E[u] ≤ u(E[X]). Linear: neutral. Convex (x²): loving. Jensen with the chord picture.
  • Certainty equivalent u(CE) = E[u(X)]. Risk premium = E[X] − CE. √x with 100 at 0.2: EU = 2, CE = 4, sure 30 wins.
  • Fair gamble +1 or −1 at 50/50 has mean 0. Play when 0.5u(1) + 0.5u(−1) > u(0). Averse agents refuse.
  • St Petersburg Pays 2ⁿ⁻¹ w.p. 1/2ⁿ. E[X] = Σ ½ = ∞. Log utility: E = ln 2, CE = 2. x² still diverges.
  • Practice 5 u = x², 0 or 10: E[X] = 5, u(E[X]) = 25, E[u] = 50, so risk loving.
  • Risk neutralE[ u(X) ] = u( E[X] )
  • Risk averseE[ u(X) ] ≤ u( E[X] )
  • Risk lovingE[ u(X) ] ≥ u( E[X] )
  • Certainty equivalentu(CE) = E[ u(X) ]   ⟹   CE = u−1( E[u(X)] )
  • Expected moneyE[X] = ∑n=1∞ 12n · 2n−1 = ∑ 12 = ∞
  • Expected log utilityE[ln X] = ∑ (n − 1) ln 22n = ln 2
  • Exam Concavity is what tames the paradox. With the convex u = x² the terms are 2ⁿ⁻² and the sum still diverges. Even √x converges, to about 1.707, which gives a certainty equivalent near 2.91.
  • Trap A risk averse person may still gamble. They need the gamble to pay enough more than the sure amount, the risk premium.

Step 3Advanced AI · Decisions over time

3.1Backward induction

  • Method From the last decision back: chance node = Σ p × value, decision node = max. Never compare raw leaves at a chance node.
  • R&D tree Success: m 11 > d 8. Failure: m 3 < d 4. Go = 0.625 × 11 + 0.375 × 4 = 8.375 > skip 5.
  • Full strategies MM 8.0, MD 8.375, DM 6.125, DD 6.5. Same answer as folding, but 2ᵏ plans for k binary nodes.
  • Practice A Good buzz push 14.4, weak buzz hold 3.7, big budget 7.98 > small 3.5.
  • Why it works Bellman's principle of optimality: each tail of an optimal plan is optimal. Folding is linear in the tree size.
  • Backward inductionstart at the last decision, replace each chance node by its expected value, keep the best action at each decision node, and move one level back
  • Trap Never compare raw leaf payoffs where nature moves. 13 is not "better than" −7 in any useful sense at a chance node, because the firm does not choose between them. Convert every chance node to its expected value first, and compare only at nodes where the firm chooses.
  • Trap At chance nodes average with the probabilities. Taking the maximum there assumes luck always goes your way.

3.2Discounting and consumption over time

  • Discount factor δ = 1/(1 + r), 0 < δ < 1, patience not a choice. A after n periods is worth Aδⁿ. Practice B: PV = 520.2.
  • Timing Discount each piece by when it happens: R&D branch 10δ − 1, not (10 − 1)δ.
  • ln consumption 2 periods K/(1 + δ), δK/(1 + δ). 3 periods K, δK, δ²K over 1 + δ + δ². Equal discounted marginal utility.
  • Corners Linear u: (K, 0, 0). Convex u = x²: FOC finds the minimum, best is a corner. √x: x₁ = K/(1 + δ²), K = 181 gives 100, 81.
  • Time consistency Exponential: re-planning keeps x₂*, x₃*. Hyperbolic 1/(1 + kt) is time inconsistent.
  • Discount factorδ = 11 + r,   0 < δ < 1
  • n periods aheadvalue today of A received after n periods = A δn = A(1 + r)n
  • Two periods, u = ln xmax ln x₁ + δ ln(K − x₁)   ⟹   x₁* = K1 + δ,   x₂* = δK1 + δ
  • Three periods, u = ln xx₁* = K1 + δ + δ²,   x₂* = δK1 + δ + δ²,   x₃* = δ²K1 + δ + δ²
  • Trap The discount rate r and the discount factor δ = 1 ÷ (1 + r) are different numbers. A higher rate means a lower factor.

3.3The value of information

  • VOI formula VOI = E[max_a u(a, s)] − max_a E[u(a, s)]. Choose per state then average, minus average per action then choose.
  • MBA Cost 10, states 0.25/0.5/0.25. No info: 9 (MBA) vs 8. Oracle: 22, 8, 4 gives 10.5. VOI = 1.5.
  • Never negative You can ignore the oracle, so VOI ≥ 0. It is 0 when one action is best in every state.
  • Venue practice C Outdoor 50/−20, indoor 30/25 at 0.5 each. 27.5 without, 37.5 with, VOI = 10. 90% forecast: 6.75.
  • Imperfect signals Update by Bayes, pick the best per signal, average. Worth 0 if it cannot change the decision (q = 0.5).
  • Value of perfect informationVOI = E[ maxa u(a, s) ] − maxa E[ u(a, s) ]
  • MBA exampleVOI = 10.5 − 9 = 1.5
  • Bayes updateP(s | signal) = P(signal | s) P(s)P(signal)
  • Trap A student in class computed 0.25 × 22 + 0.5 × 8 + 0.25 × 4 as the no information value. That mixes actions across states, which you can only do once you know the state. Without information you must use one action in every state.
  • Trap If every forecast would lead to the same choice, the forecast is worth 0 to you, however accurate it is.

Step 4Advanced AI · Games between rational agents

4.1Static games and the normal form

  • Two axes Complete vs incomplete information. Static (no one sees others' choices) vs dynamic. Static is about observation, not clock time.
  • Common knowledge Everyone knows, everyone knows everyone knows, forever. Complete info: actions, outcomes, map, preferences are common knowledge.
  • Normal form ⟨N, Sᵢ, vᵢ⟩ with vᵢ on full profiles. Finite if all Sᵢ finite. Bimatrix cell = (row payoff, column payoff).
  • Prisoner's Dilemma (M,M) = (−2,−2), (M,C) = (−5,−1), (C,M) = (−1,−5), (C,C) = (−4,−4). C is the best reply to both.
  • Best response BRᵢ(s₋ᵢ) maximizes vᵢ(·, s₋ᵢ). A profile with no profitable unilateral deviation is self enforcing.
  • Common knowledgeE is common knowledge ⟺ (everyone knows)n E for every n = 1, 2, 3, …
  • Normal form game⟨ N, {{Si}}, {{vi}} ⟩
  • Payoff functionvi : S₁ × … × Sn → ℝ
  • Best responsesi ∈ BRi(s−i) ⟺ vi(si, s−i) ≥ vi(s′i, s−i) for all s′i
  • Trap Payoff pairs are written (row player, column player). Swapping them is the most common slip in exam tables.

4.2Modelling games: Cournot, Bertrand and the streetlamp

  • Recipe Players, strategies (with their range), payoffs split into cases. Game = ⟨N, Sᵢ, vᵢ⟩.
  • Cournot P = a − bq. sᵢ = (a − cᵢ − bsⱼ)/2b, s* = (a − c)/3b. a = 100, c = 10: 30 each, P = 40, profit 900.
  • Asymmetric Cournot s₁* = (a − 2c₁ + c₂)/3b. Practice F: 18 and 12, P = 30, profits 324 and 144.
  • Bertrand Sᵢ = [0, a], q = (a − p)/b. Cheaper takes all, tie splits (divide by 2b), dearer gets −Cᵢ(0). Paradox: p = c.
  • Streetlamp vᵢ = 3·1[Σ ≥ 2] − sᵢ, 8 profiles. Pay only when pivotal. Stable: exactly two payers, or nobody.
  • Cournot payoffvi(si, sj) = (a − b(si + sj)) si − Ci(si),   or −Ci(si) if the price would be negative
  • Best response, linear cost cᵢsi = a − ci − b sj2b
  • Symmetric equilibriums* = a − c3b
  • Bertrand payoffvi = pi a − pib − Ci(a − pib) if cheaper,   half the market (divide by 2b) if tied,   −Ci(0) if dearer
  • Streetlamp payoffvi(s) = 3 · 1[ ∑j sj ≥ 2 ] − si,   si ∈ {{0, 1}}
  • Exam The Bertrand paradox: with equal unit cost c, the only stable prices are p₁ = p₂ = c. Two firms are enough to drive profit to zero, while Cournot firms keep the price at 40, well above cost 10.
  • Trap The Cournot equilibrium is not the joint best outcome for the firms. Each firm ignores that its extra output lowers the other’s price.

4.3Solution concepts and Pareto optimality

  • Solution concept Narrows profiles to reasonable ones. Its pick is an equilibrium. Not a physics equilibrium.
  • Four assumptions Rational (maximize), intelligent (know the game), common knowledge of both, self enforcement (no outside enforcer).
  • Good concept Existence, uniqueness, invariance. "Anything can happen" exists but is not unique.
  • Pareto s dominates s′: ≥ for all, > for one. PD: only (C, C) fails. Marketing War: (L,L), (L,M), (M,L). Zero sum: all optimal.
  • Altruism vᵢ + ½vⱼ gives (−3,−3), (−5.5,−3.5), (−3.5,−5.5), (−6,−6). Mum becomes best. Pareto is not fairness.
  • Pareto dominances Pareto dominates s′ ⟺ vi(s) ≥ vi(s′) for all i, and > for at least one j
  • Pareto optimals′ is not Pareto dominated by any profile
  • Altruistic payoffvialt(s) = vi(s) + ½ vj(s)
  • Trap Pareto optimality says nothing about fairness. (2, 8) and (8, 2) are Pareto optimal next to the balanced (6, 6). It only rules out waste.
  • Trap Pareto optimal and fair are different ideas. Pareto says nothing about how the total is shared.

Step 5Advanced AI · Dominance and iterated elimination

5.1Dominance and the dominant strategy equilibrium

  • Strict domination sᵢ ▷ s′ᵢ: vᵢ(sᵢ, s₋ᵢ) > vᵢ(s′ᵢ, s₋ᵢ) for every s₋ᵢ. Row player: first numbers across columns. Column player: second numbers down rows.
  • Vocabulary Dominating/dominated: beats/is beaten by at least one. Dominant/submissive: beats/is beaten by all. One dominator is enough to delete.
  • Partial order ▷ is irreflexive, asymmetric, transitive, not complete (RPS, M and H in the Marketing War). Weak: ≥ (textbook adds > once).
  • DSE Everyone plays a strictly dominant strategy. Unique if it exists. PD (C, C), altruistic PD (M, M), RPS and Marketing War none.
  • Rationality A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy under any belief. Increasing transforms and h(s₋ᵢ) do not change dominance.
  • Strict dominationsi ▷i s′i ⟺ vi(si, s−i) > vi(s′i, s−i) for every s−i
  • Strict dominant strategy equilibriumsD with siD strictly dominant for every player i
  • Rational players and dominated strategiesEμ[vi(si)] − Eμ[vi(s′i)] = ∑ μ(s−i)[vi(si, s−i) − vi(s′i, s−i)] > 0
  • Trap The mistake made twice in class: checking player 1 by mixing in the second number of each cell, or by moving down a column. Rule: for the row player compare first numbers, moving across columns. For the column player compare second numbers, moving down rows. Moving along the other player's strategies is exactly "for every s₋ᵢ".
  • Trap Strict and weak dominance behave differently. Removing weakly dominated strategies can remove equilibria and depends on the order of removal.

5.2Iterated elimination of dominated strategies

  • Algorithm Each round delete every strictly dominated strategy of every player, comparing only survivors. Stop when nothing is dominated.
  • Common knowledge Round k uses k layers of "I know that you know". Real people manage about 1 to 3 layers.
  • Slide 11 R ▷ C, then U ▷ M and D, then L ▷ R. Outcome (U, L) = (4, 3), self enforcing but Pareto dominated by (9, 6).
  • Theorems DSE found in one round. Every NE survives. Unique survivor = unique NE. Strict IESDS is order independent.
  • Limits Marketing War leaves {M, H}², RPS deletes nothing. Weak IESDS depends on order: (2, 1) or (1, 1).
  • IESDS, round kDSik = {{ si ∈ Sik : some s′i ∈ Sik beats it against every s−i ∈ S−ik }}
  • UpdateSik+1 = Sik ∖ DSik
  • Nash equilibrium (preview)vi(si*, s*−i) ≥ vi(si, s*−i) for every i and every si
  • Trap IESDS does not always leave one outcome. In many games, such as the restaurant game, nothing is dominated and every strategy survives.

5.3Dominance at work: Cournot, auctions and voting

  • Cournot IESDS BR = max{0, (a − c − bq)/2b}. Rounds: [0, 45], [22.5, 45], [22.5, 33.75], … halving to (a − c)/3b = 30. E1: 40, price 50, profit 1,600.
  • Second price auction Payoff v − B if b > B. Bidding v weakly dominates every bid (never worse, sometimes better). Strategy proof.
  • First price auction Payoff v − b if you win. Overbidding weakly dominated, but bidding v not dominant: bidders shade.
  • Location and prices Hotelling 1 to 5: delete 1, 5 then 2, 4, both at 3 (median). Discrete Bertrand: strict deletes nothing, weak ends at (2, 2) losing (1, 1), (3, 3).
  • Weak only Below cost Bertrand prices and the guess game: weakly but not strictly dominated, so strict IESDS cannot start.
  • Best responseBRi(qj) = max{{ 0, a − c − bqj2b }}
  • Second price (Vickrey)payoff = v − B if b > B, otherwise 0
  • First pricepayoff = v − b if b > B, otherwise 0
  • Trap In a second price auction you do not pay your own bid. Students who forget this conclude that shading the bid is wise.
