Tier 1: school and first year

Questions at this level check that you can read a preference, compute an average over chances and spot a choice that is always better.

Expected valueExam favourite
E[X] = Σ pi xi
Weight each prize by its chance and add.
Present valueExam favourite
PV = Σ δt xt
A payment t periods away is worth δt of its face value today.
Strict orderings
n!
The number of ways to rank n outcomes with no ties.
Question 1. Riya prefers tea to coffee and coffee to juice, but juice to tea. Is she rational in the sense of this course?
  1. Rational preferences must be complete and transitive.
  2. Transitivity needs tea preferred to juice, but she says juice is preferred to tea.
  3. So she is not rational. A trader could sell her each swap for a small fee and take her money round the cycle, the money pump of chapter 1.1.
Question 2. A raffle ticket wins 100 rupees with probability 0.3 and nothing otherwise. What is its expected value?
  1. E = 0.3 × 100 + 0.7 × 0.
  2. = 30 rupees.
  3. A risk neutral buyer would pay up to 30 for it. A risk averse buyer would pay less.
Question 3. In how many ways can you rank 4 films with no ties?
  1. The first place has 4 choices, then 3, then 2, then 1.
  2. 4 × 3 × 2 × 1.
  3. = 24 strict orderings.
Question 4. Two players each pick Quiet or Confess. Confessing is better for you whatever the other does. Why do both confess?
  1. Confess strictly dominates Quiet for each player.
  2. A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy.
  3. So (Confess, Confess) is played, even though (Quiet, Quiet) is better for both. That gap is the lesson of the Prisoner's Dilemma.
Remember
Try the presets stag and pennies in the game above. Stag has two equilibria and pennies has none in pure strategies.
Lesson 3 of 6

Tier 2: university semester and IIT course exams

Here the examiner gives a tree, a lottery or a game and asks you to work it by hand. State the formula, then show each step.

Question 5. With u(w) = √w, find the certainty equivalent and risk premium of a lottery paying 100 or 0 with equal chance.
  1. EU = 0.5 × √100 + 0.5 × √0 = 5.
  2. CE solves √CE = 5, so CE = 25.
  3. EV = 50, so the risk premium is 50 − 25 = 25.
  4. The person would swap the lottery for a sure 25. Concave utility means risk aversion.
Question 6. Two firms face p = 100 − (q₁ + q₂) with unit cost 10. Find the Cournot equilibrium.
  1. Firm 1 maximises (100 − q₁ − q₂ − 10) q₁. The first order condition is 90 − 2q₁ − q₂ = 0.
  2. Best response q₁ = (90 − q₂) ÷ 2, and the same for firm 2.
  3. By symmetry q = (90 − q) ÷ 2, so q* = 30 each.
  4. Price 100 − 60 = 40, profit (40 − 10) × 30 = 900 each.
Question 7. Action A pays 10 if the market is good and 0 if bad. Action B pays 4 for sure. The market is good with probability 0.6. What is the value of perfect information?
  1. Without information: E(A) = 6, E(B) = 4, so pick A for 6.
  2. With information: if good pick A for 10, if bad pick B for 4.
  3. Expected value with information = 0.6 × 10 + 0.4 × 4 = 7.6.
  4. VOI = 7.6 − 6 = 1.6. Never pay more than 1.6 for the forecast.
Question 8. Split K = 100 over two periods to maximise ln x₁ + δ ln x₂ with δ = 0.9.
  1. The first order conditions give 1 ÷ x₁ = δ ÷ x₂, so x₂ = δ x₁.
  2. Budget x₁ + δ x₁ = 100, so x₁ = 100 ÷ 1.9.
  3. x₁ = 52.63, x₂ = 47.37
  4. A more patient person (δ closer to 1) spreads consumption more evenly.

Question 9 (numerical answer). A payment of 100 arrives in 2 years with δ = 0.9. Its present value is
  1. PV = δ² × 100.
  2. = 0.81 × 100.
  3. = 81.
Question 10 (multiple select). Which are true for u(w) = w²? (a) risk loving (b) CE > EV (c) risk premium < 0 (d) concave
  1. u is convex, so the person is risk loving. (a) is true.
  2. For a convex u, Jensen gives u(EV) ≤ EU, so CE ≥ EV. (b) is true for a lottery with real risk.
  3. Risk premium = EV − CE < 0. (c) is true.
  4. (d) is false. Answer: a, b, c.
Question 11 (numerical answer). A player has 3 decision nodes with 2 actions at each. How many pure strategies?
  1. A strategy names one action at every node, even nodes it never reaches.
  2. 2 × 2 × 2.
  3. = 8. The R&D tree of chapter 3.1 had 2 nodes and 4 strategies, MM, MD, DM and DD.
Question 12 (numerical answer). A lottery pays 2k with probability 2−k for k = 1 to 10. Its expected value is
  1. Each term is 2−k × 2k = 1.
  2. Ten terms.
  3. = 10. Without the cap the sum grows without bound, the St Petersburg paradox.
In the exam
Round only at the very end. A numerical answer type key often has a narrow range such as 52.6 to 52.7.
Lesson 5 of 6

Tier 4: proofs, derivations and critique

Exams in the style of MIT and Cambridge reward a complete argument more than a final number. State the assumptions, prove the claim, then say when it fails.

Question 13. Show that every complete and transitive preference on a finite set has a utility function.

Model answer
  1. For each outcome x, let u(x) be the number of outcomes y with x ⪰ y.
  2. If x ⪰ z then, by transitivity, every y below z is also below x, so u(x) ≥ u(z).
  3. If x ≻ z then z ⪰ x fails, so x is counted for x but not for z, and u(x) > u(z).
  4. Completeness makes sure every pair is compared, so u represents ⪰.
  5. It fails on some infinite sets, for example lexicographic preferences on the plane have no real valued utility.

Question 14. Show that a concave utility gives a positive risk premium.

Model answer
  1. Jensen: for concave u, E[u(X)] ≤ u(E[X]).
  2. The certainty equivalent satisfies u(CE) = E[u(X)] ≤ u(E[X]).
  3. u is increasing, so CE ≤ E[X], and the risk premium E[X] − CE ≥ 0.
  4. Equality holds only for a lottery with no real risk or a linear u.

Question 15. Show that expected utility rankings survive a positive affine change.

Model answer
  1. Let v = a u + b with a > 0.
  2. E[v] = a E[u] + b because expectation is linear.
  3. So E[u(L₁)] ≥ E[u(L₂)] if and only if E[v(L₁)] ≥ E[v(L₂)].
  4. A non affine change such as u² can flip the ranking, which is why vNM utility is cardinal (chapter 2.2).

Question 16. Show that a strictly dominant strategy equilibrium is unique.

Model answer
  1. Suppose s and t are both strictly dominant for player i and s ≠ t.
  2. Since s strictly dominates every other strategy, ui(s, x) > ui(t, x) for every x.
  3. Since t is strictly dominant, ui(t, x) > ui(s, x). Both cannot hold.
  4. So each player has at most one, and the equilibrium is unique. Weak dominance does not give this.

Critique questions

A critique question gives a model and asks what is wrong with it. For example, "a firm uses expected value to price insurance". The answer names the missing piece: the buyer is risk averse, so the willingness to pay exceeds the expected loss by the risk premium. Another favourite: "the players will reach (Quiet, Quiet) because it is Pareto better". The answer: Pareto optimality says nothing about what rational players choose. Each player still gains by deviating.

In the exam
In proofs, name each assumption when you use it: completeness, transitivity, the vNM axioms, common knowledge of rationality. Examiners often ask what breaks when one fails.
One idea, four depths

At school you read preferences, compute expected values and spot a choice that is always better.

At university you solve trees, lotteries and games by hand, step by step, and explain what the number means.

In competitive papers you must recognise the right formula fast, use quick checks to drop options and round only at the end.

At research universities you prove existence of utility, Jensen based risk results, affine invariance and the uniqueness and order independence results for dominance.

Ten mistakes that cost marks

  1. Calling a preference rational without checking transitivity.
  2. Treating ordinal utility as cardinal, for example adding utilities of different people.
  3. Computing expected value when the question asks for expected utility.
  4. Reporting expected utility as the certainty equivalent without applying u inverse.
  5. Forgetting chance nodes when folding back a tree.
  6. Listing a strategy that skips nodes off the path.
  7. Discounting the first payment when it is received today.
  8. Reporting a negative value of information.
  9. Confusing a Pareto optimal profile with an equilibrium.
  10. Deleting weakly dominated strategies when the question asks for strict IESDS.
