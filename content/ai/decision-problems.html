Lesson 1 of 4

What a decision problem is

Every day you choose. Which bus to take, which film to watch, which job offer to accept. The first lecture of the course turns this everyday act into mathematics so that a computer can do it too. A decision problem has three parts.

A decision problemExam favourite
( A, X, c, ⪰ )
A is the set of actions you can take. X is the set of outcomes that can happen. c is the map that says which outcome each action leads to. ⪰ is your preference over outcomes.
The consequence map
c : A → X
With certainty, each action leads to exactly one outcome. Later chapters let an action lead to a lottery over outcomes.
Action value
v(a) = u( c(a) )
We like outcomes, not actions. An action is worth whatever its outcome is worth.
PartEveryday exampleAI example
Actions AGo to INOX or go to PVRMove left, right, up or down
Outcomes XThe film you end up watchingThe square the robot reaches
Consequence cINOX shows AliensMoving right from (1, 1) reaches (2, 1)
Preference ⪰Aliens is better than CasablancaThe charging dock is better than the stairs

The professor stressed one distinction. An action is something you choose. An outcome is something you receive. Utility is given to outcomes, and the value of an action is borrowed from the outcome it leads to.

Real world scenario: a delivery robot

A warehouse robot can take the lift, take the ramp or wait. The lift reaches the shelf in 40 seconds, the ramp in 70 seconds, and waiting reaches nothing. Write A = {{lift, ramp, wait}}, X = {{shelf in 40 s, shelf in 70 s, no delivery}}, and c(lift) = shelf in 40 s. Once the robot can rank the three outcomes, picking an action is a lookup.

Lesson 2 of 4

Weak preference, strict preference and indifference

A preference relation compares two outcomes at a time. The basic building block is weak preference: x ⪰ y means "x is at least as good as y". Everything else is built from it.

Weak preferenceExam favourite
x ⪰ y
x is at least as good as y. It allows a tie.
Strict preferenceExam favourite
x ≻ y ⟺ x ⪰ y and not y ⪰ x
x is better and the reverse does not hold.
IndifferenceExam favourite
x ∼ y ⟺ x ⪰ y and y ⪰ x
Both directions hold, so the two are equally good.

Mathematically a relation is just a set of ordered pairs. For X = {A, B, C} with A ≻ B, A ≻ C and B ∼ C the lecture writes the sets out in full.

RelationPairs it contains
⪰ weak(A, A), (B, B), (C, C), (A, B), (A, C), (B, C), (C, B)
≻ strict(A, B), (A, C)
∼ indifference(A, A), (B, B), (C, C), (B, C), (C, B)
Worked example from the lecture: A ≻ B ∼ C ≻ D
  1. Start with the reflexive pairs, since every outcome is at least as good as itself: (A, A), (B, B), (C, C), (D, D).
  2. A beats everything below it: (A, B), (A, C), (A, D).
  3. B and C both beat D: (B, D), (C, D).
  4. B and C tie, so both directions go in: (B, C), (C, B).
  5. |⪰| = 4 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 11 pairs
  6. Strict pairs are those whose reverse is missing: (A, B), (A, C), (A, D), (B, D), (C, D), so 5. Indifference pairs are the 4 reflexive ones plus (B, C) and (C, B), so 6.
Common mistake
Equality and indifference are different ideas. Equality means two things are the same object. Indifference means two different outcomes are judged equally good. Tea and coffee are not equal, but you may be indifferent between them.
Lesson 3 of 4

Completeness and transitivity

Not every set of pairs is a sensible preference. The course calls a preference rational when it has two properties.

CompletenessExam favourite
for all x, y ∈ X:   x ⪰ y or y ⪰ x
You can always compare. You may say better, worse or equally good, but you may not leave a pair unanswered.
TransitivityExam favourite
x ⪰ y and y ⪰ z ⟹ x ⪰ z
Rankings chain together without contradiction.
Rational preference
rational = complete + transitive
A rational agent has such a preference and picks an action whose outcome it likes most.

When completeness fails

Picture two cars. One is cheaper, less powerful and more reliable. The other is dearer, more powerful and less reliable. If you have no way to trade price against power against reliability, you cannot rank them. That is incompleteness, and it is not the same as indifference. Indifference says "they are equally good". Incompleteness says "I cannot say".

When transitivity fails

Suppose A ≻ B, B ≻ C and C ≻ A. Nothing can be called best, since each outcome loses to another. Worse, someone can exploit you. They sell you B for A plus a coin, then C for B plus a coin, then A for C plus a coin. You end up holding A again with three coins fewer. This is called a money pump.

Homework 1: if ⪰ is transitive, is ≻ transitive?
  1. Assume x ≻ y and y ≻ z. Then x ⪰ y and y ⪰ z, so transitivity of ⪰ gives x ⪰ z.
  2. We still need "not z ⪰ x". Suppose instead that z ⪰ x.
  3. From z ⪰ x and x ⪰ y transitivity gives z ⪰ y.
  4. But y ≻ z says z ⪰ y is false. That is a contradiction.
  5. So x ≻ z, and strict preference is transitive.
Homework 2: if ⪰ is transitive, is ∼ transitive?
  1. Assume x ∼ y and y ∼ z. That gives four facts: x ⪰ y, y ⪰ x, y ⪰ z, z ⪰ y.
  2. Chain x ⪰ y and y ⪰ z to get x ⪰ z.
  3. Chain z ⪰ y and y ⪰ x to get z ⪰ x.
  4. Both directions hold, so x ∼ z.
Does it carry over from ⪰?To ≻To ∼
TransitivityYes, proof aboveYes, proof above
CompletenessNo. If x ∼ y then neither x ≻ y nor y ≻ xNo. If x ≻ y then x ∼ y is false and so is y ∼ x
ReflexivityNo. x ≻ x never holdsYes. x ⪰ x in both directions
Going deeper: Why completeness implies reflexivity

Completeness says that for every pair x, y at least one of x ⪰ y or y ⪰ x holds. Nothing stops us from choosing y = x. Then the statement reads "x ⪰ x or x ⪰ x", so x ⪰ x. A complete relation is therefore always reflexive. This is why every rational weak preference contains the diagonal pairs (A, A), (B, B) and so on.

One idea, four depths

To choose well you must be able to compare any two options, and your comparisons must not go round in a circle.

A preference is a set of ordered pairs. Strict preference and indifference are built from weak preference. A rational preference is complete and transitive. Transitivity of ⪰ passes to ≻ and to ∼, but completeness does not.

A complete and transitive relation is a total preorder. Its indifference classes are equivalence classes, and ≻ orders those classes strictly. Dropping completeness gives a partial preorder, which is the starting point of multi criteria decision making and Pareto analysis.

Experiments by Tversky (1969) found intransitive choices in people when options differ slightly on several attributes. Research on regret theory, context dependent preferences and incomplete preference models (Aumann 1962, Bewley 1986) asks how much of rational choice survives once these axioms are weakened.

Lesson 4 of 4

From intuition to research

Preferences are the raw material of every later chapter. This lesson takes the same idea from an everyday picture to an open research question, so you can stop at the depth you need.

Real world scenario: the money pump at a juice stall

Suppose Riya prefers tea to juice, juice to coffee and coffee to tea. A stall owner hands her tea, then offers juice for a small fee of 1 rupee, then coffee for another rupee, then tea again for a third. Each swap looks good to her, yet after three swaps she holds the same tea and is 3 rupees poorer. Cyclic preferences can be exploited without end, which is why transitivity is treated as a rule of rational choice.

Work it out: is this preference transitive?
  1. Riya: tea ≻ juice, juice ≻ coffee, coffee ≻ tea.
  2. Transitivity would need tea ≻ coffee from the first two.
  3. But she states coffee ≻ tea, so the relation has a cycle.
  4. Not transitive. Each round of the pump costs her 3 rupees.
Going deeper: the analysis

A complete and transitive weak preference is called a weak order. With n options there are n! strict rankings with no ties, but more weak orders, because ties are allowed. For 3 options there are 6 strict rankings and 13 weak orders, for 4 options 24 and 75. These are the ordered Bell (Fubini) numbers. Every weak order can be drawn as a ladder of indifference classes, best class on top.

Going deeper: the research view

Arrow’s impossibility theorem (1951) shows that even when every person is transitive, a group may not be. Three voters with rankings A B C, B C A and C A B make the majority prefer A to B, B to C and C to A: the Condorcet paradox. In AI this matters for preference learning from many annotators, where pooled human comparisons can cycle and a single reward model cannot fit them all.

Common mistake
Completeness does not mean you like every option. It means you can compare any two, possibly by saying you are indifferent.
