Step 1Rational choice

1.1

Decision problems and preferences

Open chapter

Decision problems, the three preference relations, completeness, transitivity, cycles and what carries over from weak to strict preference.

Key ideas

  • Decision problem (A, X, c, ⪰): actions, outcomes, consequence map c : A → X, preference. Utility goes on outcomes, v(a) = u(c(a)).
  • Three relations Weak x ⪰ y. Strict x ≻ y means x ⪰ y and not y ⪰ x. Indifference x ∼ y means both directions.
  • Rationality Complete (every pair compared) plus transitive (rankings chain). Cycles have no best element and allow a money pump.
  • What carries over Transitivity of ⪰ passes to ≻ and ∼. Completeness does not pass to either.
  • Indifference is not incompleteness Indifferent: equally good. Incomplete: cannot compare. Equality: the same object.

1.2

Utility functions

Open chapter

Utility representation, ordinal invariance, the existence theorem with its construction proof, and counting rankings.

Key ideas

  • Representation x ⪰ y ⟺ u(x) ≥ u(y). Strict gives >, indifference gives =. Not "=" for weak preference.
  • Ordinal Only order matters. Any strictly increasing f gives f(u), another valid utility. Gaps and ratios mean nothing.
  • Existence Finite X + complete + transitive ⟹ a utility exists. Build it by peeling off top classes: A ≻ B ∼ C ≻ D gives 3, 2, 2, 1.
  • Best exists In finite X an endless chain of improvements must cycle, which transitivity forbids. Ties at the top are allowed.
  • Counting n strict outcomes give n! rankings (5! = 120). With ties: 1, 3, 13, 75 weak orders.

Key ideas

Step 2Choice under risk

2.1

Lotteries and distributions

Open chapter

Lotteries, the CDF and its properties, intervals, densities and continuous outcomes.

Key ideas

  • Lottery Action a gives P(xᵢ | a) = pᵢ with pᵢ in [0, 1] and Σ pᵢ = 1. Nature picks the outcome.
  • CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x | a). Non decreasing, 0 at −∞, 1 at +∞, right continuous. F(50) = 0.7 is "at most 50".
  • Intervals P(r < X ≤ s) = F(s) − F(r). Notes example 0.8 − 0.3 = 0.5.
  • Density F = ∫ f, f = F′. P(X = x) = 0 for continuous X. Densities can exceed 1.
  • Compound paths Multiply along each path, add the paths that reach the same outcome: 0.6 × 0.8 + 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.6.

2.2

Expected utility and compound lotteries

Open chapter

Expected utility, reducing compound lotteries, the R&D example, the reversal under non affine changes and why utility under risk is cardinal.

Key ideas

  • Expected utility EU(a) = Σ P(xᵢ | a) u(xᵢ), or ∫ u dF. Choose arg max EU, not arg max E[X].
  • R&D compound lottery P(10 | G) = 0.625 × 0.9 + 0.375 × 0.5 = 0.75, EU(G) = 7.5 > EU(S) = 5. Multiply along paths, add paths.
  • Reversal u = 9, −1, 10, 0: EU(G) 6.5 > 5. u(B) = −8: 4.75 < 5. Same ranking, opposite choice.
  • Affine invariance Only v = a u + b with a > 0 keeps every EU comparison. Utility under risk is cardinal.
  • Other rules and PV Optimistic (P best), pessimistic (P worst). PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ. vNM axioms give EU.

2.3

Risk attitudes and the St Petersburg paradox

Open chapter

Risk attitudes from concavity, certainty equivalent and risk premium, and the St Petersburg paradox with its log utility resolution.

Key ideas

  • Three attitudes Concave u (ln, √): averse, E[u] ≤ u(E[X]). Linear: neutral. Convex (x²): loving. Jensen with the chord picture.
  • Certainty equivalent u(CE) = E[u(X)]. Risk premium = E[X] − CE. √x with 100 at 0.2: EU = 2, CE = 4, sure 30 wins.
  • Fair gamble +1 or −1 at 50/50 has mean 0. Play when 0.5u(1) + 0.5u(−1) > u(0). Averse agents refuse.
  • St Petersburg Pays 2ⁿ⁻¹ w.p. 1/2ⁿ. E[X] = Σ ½ = ∞. Log utility: E = ln 2, CE = 2. x² still diverges.
  • Practice 5 u = x², 0 or 10: E[X] = 5, u(E[X]) = 25, E[u] = 50, so risk loving.

Backward induction

Open chapter

Backward induction on the R&D and marketing tree, full strategies, the film studio problem and Bellman's principle.

Key ideas

  • Method From the last decision back: chance node = Σ p × value, decision node = max. Never compare raw leaves at a chance node.
  • R&D tree Success: m 11 > d 8. Failure: m 3 < d 4. Go = 0.625 × 11 + 0.375 × 4 = 8.375 > skip 5.

  • Why it works Bellman's principle of optimality: each tail of an optimal plan is optimal. Folding is linear in the tree size.

3.3

The value of information

Open chapter

The value of perfect and imperfect information, the MBA and venue examples, and why information is never worth less than zero.

Key ideas

  • VOI formula VOI = E[max_a u(a, s)] − max_a E[u(a, s)]. Choose per state then average, minus average per action then choose.
  • MBA Cost 10, states 0.25/0.5/0.25. No info: 9 (MBA) vs 8. Oracle: 22, 8, 4 gives 10.5. VOI = 1.5.
  • Never negative You can ignore the oracle, so VOI ≥ 0. It is 0 when one action is best in every state.
  • Venue practice C Outdoor 50/−20, indoor 30/25 at 0.5 each. 27.5 without, 37.5 with, VOI = 10. 90% forecast: 6.75.
  • Imperfect signals Update by Bayes, pick the best per signal, average. Worth 0 if it cannot change the decision (q = 0.5).

5.1

Dominance and the dominant strategy equilibrium

Open chapter

Strict and weak dominance, the four dominance words, the dominant strategy equilibrium with its uniqueness proof, and its limits.

Key ideas

  • Strict domination sᵢ ▷ s′ᵢ: vᵢ(sᵢ, s₋ᵢ) > vᵢ(s′ᵢ, s₋ᵢ) for every s₋ᵢ. Row player: first numbers across columns. Column player: second numbers down rows.
