When an action has more than one outcome

So far each action led to one known outcome. Real life is rarely like that. Investing in research may succeed or fail. A crop may yield a lot or a little depending on the rain. The course models this by adding a second player called nature. You choose an action, then nature picks the outcome at random.

Nature is not a person. It stands for weather, markets, disease, traffic, and anything else outside your control. What you know is the probability with which nature picks each outcome, and that probability can depend on your action.

Lecture example of a simple lottery
  1. Action a gives x₁ with 0.6, x₂ with 0.3, x₃ with 0.1.
  2. Each probability lies between 0 and 1.
  3. 0.6 + 0.3 + 0.1 = 1, so this is a valid lottery
Lesson 2 of 3

The cumulative distribution function

When the outcome can be any number, such as a crop yield of 1.2, 1.21 or 1.2135 tonnes, we cannot list every outcome with its own probability. Instead we ask: what is the chance the outcome is at most x? That function of x is the CDF.

CDF of an actionExam favourite
Fa(x) = P( X ≤ x | a )
The probability that the outcome is at most x when you take action a.
Interval probabilityExam favourite
P( r < X ≤ s | a ) = Fa(s) − Fa(r)
Everything up to s minus everything up to r.

The four properties of every CDF

PropertyFormulaPlain meaning
Non decreasingx < y ⟹ F(x) ≤ F(y)More room below means at least as much probability
Lower limitF(x) → 0 as x → −∞Nothing lies below everything
Upper limitF(x) → 1 as x → +∞Everything lies below infinity
Right continuousF(x) = lim F(x + h) as h ↓ 0At a jump the filled dot sits on the upper step
Common mistake
F(50) = 0.7 means a 70% chance the outcome is at most 50. It does not mean a 70% chance the outcome is exactly 50. This misreading is the most common exam error on CDFs.
Practice problem 8: F(50) = 0.8 and F(20) = 0.3. Find P(20 < X ≤ 50)
  1. Use the interval rule P(r < X ≤ s) = F(s) − F(r).
  2. Here r = 20 and s = 50.
  3. P = 0.8 − 0.3 = 0.5

Densities and continuous outcomes

For a continuous outcome the CDF has a slope. That slope is the probability density function, the PDF. The density says how crowded the probability is near x. It is not itself a probability, and it can be bigger than 1.

From density to CDFExam favourite
Fa(x) = ∫−∞x fa(t) dt
Add up the density from the far left up to x.
From CDF to density
fa(x) = ddx Fa(x)
Wherever the derivative exists.
Single points
P( X = x ) = 0   for a continuous X

Real world scenario: a fertilizer decision

A farmer choosing how much fertilizer to buy faces a different yield distribution for each choice. Light fertilizer might give a yield uniform on 20 to 40 tonnes, heavy fertilizer uniform on 15 to 60. Heavy has more upside and more downside. The CDF of each action is what the farmer compares, and chapter 2.2 shows how expected utility turns that comparison into one number per action.

Going deeper: First order stochastic dominance

Action a dominates action b in the first order sense when Fa(x) ≤ Fb(x) for every x, with strict inequality somewhere. In words, a is less likely than b to fall below any threshold. Every agent who prefers more to less then prefers a, whatever its risk attitude. When the two CDFs cross, the choice depends on the utility function, which is why expected utility is needed.

One idea, four depths

When you cannot be sure what will happen, list what could happen and how likely each thing is. That list is a lottery.

An action under risk gives a distribution P(· | a). The CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x) is non decreasing, goes from 0 to 1 and is right continuous. Intervals use F(s) − F(r). A density is the slope of F.

The CDF determines the distribution completely, and expectations are written as Lebesgue Stieltjes integrals ∫ u dF, which handle discrete, continuous and mixed cases with one formula. First and second order stochastic dominance compare CDFs directly.

Distributional reinforcement learning (Bellemare, Dabney and Munos 2017) learns the whole return distribution, often through its quantile function, the inverse of the CDF, rather than only its mean. Risk sensitive policies then optimize a quantile or a tail measure such as CVaR.
