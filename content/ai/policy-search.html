Policy search: tune the policy directly

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 21.5 (4th ed., 22.5), Williams (1992), and Sutton and Barto (2018), chapter 13. The uploaded slides and transcripts do not cover this step.

What it is
Policy search writes the policy as a formula π_θ with parameters θ and changes θ to raise the policy value ρ(θ), the expected total reward of following π_θ. It never needs to learn exact values for every state.
Why we need it
Sometimes the best policy is simple even when the values are complicated: "steer towards the centre of the road" is easier than predicting the exact future reward of every position. Policy search also works with continuous actions, such as how hard to brake, where taking a max over actions is hard.
How it works
Use a stochastic policy, such as a softmax over action preferences, so that ρ(θ) changes smoothly with θ. Estimate the gradient of ρ from trials and climb it: θ ← θ + α ∇ρ(θ).
Policy valueExam favourite
ρ(θ) = E[Σ γᵗ Rₜ | actions from π_θ]
What policy search maximises.
Softmax policy
π_θ(a | s) = e^(θ · f(s, a)) ÷ Σ over b of e^(θ · f(s, b))
Every action keeps some chance, so the policy explores.
Finite difference gradient
∂ρ ÷ ∂θᵢ ≈ (ρ(θ + h eᵢ) − ρ(θ)) ÷ h
Works for any policy, but needs many trials per parameter and is noisy.
Common mistake
With a greedy policy (always the best action under some score) ρ(θ) jumps when the best action changes, so its gradient is zero almost everywhere. That is why policy gradient methods use stochastic policies.

The policy gradient and REINFORCE

Source: Williams (1992), Simple statistical gradient following algorithms for connectionist reinforcement learning, and Sutton, McAllester, Singh and Mansour (2000), the policy gradient theorem.

What it is
The policy gradient theorem gives ∇ρ as an average we can sample: E[∇ log π_θ(a | s) × (return after taking a)]. REINFORCE uses one trial at a time as a sample of this average.
Why we need it
It needs no model and no derivative of the world, only the derivative of our own policy, which we wrote down. Actions followed by high returns become more likely, actions followed by low returns less likely.
How it works
Run a trial. For each step t, with return Gₜ from then on, update θ ← θ + α (Gₜ − b) ∇ log π_θ(aₜ | sₜ). The baseline b, for example the average return so far, does not change the expected update but makes it much less noisy.
Policy gradient theoremExam favourite
∇ρ(θ) = E[∇_θ log π_θ(a | s) Q^π(s, a)]
Only the gradient of the policy appears.
REINFORCE update
θ ← θ + α (Gₜ − b) ∇_θ log π_θ(aₜ | sₜ)
b is a baseline that does not depend on the action.
Softmax gradient
∂ log π(a) ÷ ∂θ_k = 1[k = a] − π(k)
For a softmax over preferences θ.
Why a baseline is safe
E[∇ log π(a)] = Σ π(a) ∇ log π(a) = ∇ Σ π(a) = ∇ 1 = 0
So subtracting b changes no expected value.
Worked example: one REINFORCE step on two actions
  1. θ = (0, 0), so π = (0.5, 0.5). The agent picks action 1 and gets r = 1. α = 0.4.
  2. Gradient of log π(1): (0 − 0.5, 1 − 0.5) = (−0.5, 0.5).
  3. No baseline: θ ← (0, 0) + 0.4 × 1 × (−0.5, 0.5) = (−0.2, 0.2).
  4. With baseline b = 0.5 the step is half as big: θ = (−0.1, 0.1)
Lesson 3 of 8

Actor critic and other refinements

Source: Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983), Konda and Tsitsiklis (2000), Ng and Jordan (2000) for PEGASUS, and Schulman and others (2017) for PPO.

What it is
An actor critic method keeps two learners: the actor is the policy π_θ, the critic learns a value function V_w. The critic’s TD error tells the actor whether an action turned out better or worse than expected.
Why we need it
REINFORCE waits for the end of a trial and its returns are noisy. The critic gives a lower variance signal after every step, so learning is faster and works for tasks that never end.
How it works
After each step: δ = r + γ V_w(s′) − V_w(s). Critic: w ← w + β δ ∇V_w(s). Actor: θ ← θ + α δ ∇ log π_θ(a | s). Correlated sampling (PEGASUS) reuses the same random numbers when comparing two policies, which removes much of the noise from finite difference estimates.
Actor criticExam favourite
δ = r + γ V(s′) − V(s), θ ← θ + α δ ∇ log π_θ(a | s)
δ estimates the advantage of the action taken.
Advantage
A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s)
How much better a is than the policy’s average action.
PPO clipped objective
E[min(r_t(θ) Â, clip(r_t(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Â)], with r_t = π_θ ÷ π_old
Keeps each policy update small and safe.
Worked example: a TD error as the actor’s signal
  1. The critic says V(s) = 2.5. The agent acts, gets r = 1 and lands in s′ with V(s′) = 2. γ = 0.9.
  2. δ = 1 + 0.9 × 2 − 2.5 = 1 + 1.8 − 2.5.
  3. δ = 0.3 is positive: the action did better than expected.
  4. the actor raises the chance of that action, by α × 0.3 × ∇ log π
Lesson 4 of 8

Learning from an expert: imitation and inverse RL

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 21.6, Ng and Russell (2000) for inverse reinforcement learning, and Abbeel and Ng (2004) for apprenticeship learning.

What it is
Instead of a reward, the agent may watch an expert. Imitation learning (behaviour cloning) copies the expert’s actions with supervised learning. Inverse reinforcement learning works out a reward function that explains the expert’s behaviour, then plans with it.
Why we need it
Writing a good reward by hand is hard: a reward for "drive well" is much harder to write than a few hours of a good driver’s data. A learned reward also carries over to new situations the expert never met.
How it works
Behaviour cloning fits π(a | s) to the expert’s pairs. Inverse RL searches for a reward under which the expert’s policy is optimal (or nearly), often matching expected feature counts, and then solves the MDP for that reward.
Real world scenario: Helicopter aerobatics

Abbeel, Coates, Quigley and Ng (2007) used apprenticeship learning and policy search to fly a model helicopter through stunts such as flips, rolls and tic tocs, learned from a few demonstrations by an expert pilot. Policy search with PEGASUS had earlier learned to hover the same helicopter (Ng and others, 2004).

The maths: deriving the policy gradient

Log derivative trickExam favourite
∇ π(a) = π(a) ∇ log π(a)
Turns a gradient of a sum into an expectation.
One step (bandit) case
∇ρ = ∇ Σ π(a) R(a) = Σ π(a) ∇ log π(a) R(a) = E[∇ log π(a) R(a)]
Sample a, multiply the gradient of log π(a) by its reward.
Exact bandit gradient for a softmax
∂ρ ÷ ∂θ_k = π(k) (μ_k − ρ)
An action with above average reward gets pushed up.
Variance with a baseline
Var[(G − b) ∇ log π] is smallest near b = E[G]
The usual choice is an estimate of V(s).
Worked example: the exact gradient for two arms with means 0.3 and 0.7, at π = (0.5, 0.5)
  1. ρ = 0.5 × 0.3 + 0.5 × 0.7 = 0.5.
  2. ∂ρ ÷ ∂θ₁ = π(1) (μ₁ − ρ) = 0.5 × (0.7 − 0.5).
  3. That is 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1. For arm 0: 0.5 × (0.3 − 0.5) = −0.1.
  4. ∇ρ = (−0.1, 0.1): push towards the better arm

Lesson 8 of 8

Chapter summary

  1. Policy search tunes θ of a policy π_θ to raise ρ(θ), the expected return. It handles continuous actions and simple policies well.
  2. Use stochastic (softmax) policies so ρ(θ) is smooth. ∂ log π(a) ÷ ∂θ_k = 1[k = a] − π(k).
  3. REINFORCE: θ ← θ + α (G − b) ∇ log π(a | s). A baseline cuts noise without changing the expected update.
  4. Actor critic uses the critic’s TD error δ in place of G − b, for faster, lower variance learning.
  5. Imitation learning and inverse RL learn from an expert when a reward is hard to write.
