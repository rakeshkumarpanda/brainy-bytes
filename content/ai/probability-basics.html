Lesson 1 of 8

Why agents need probability

Source: Russell and Norvig, AIMA 3rd ed., sections 13.1 and 13.2 (4th ed., sections 12.1 and 12.2). The uploaded slides and transcripts do not cover this step, so it follows the textbook.

What it is
Probability gives a number between 0 and 1 to a sentence, saying how strongly the agent believes it. 1 means sure it is true, 0 means sure it is false. Everything in between is a degree of belief.
Why we need it
Logic needs facts that are surely true or false. Real agents rarely have that. A dentist cannot list every cause of a toothache (laziness), does not know all of medicine (theoretical ignorance), and has not run every test on this patient (practical ignorance). Logic rules like "Toothache ⇒ Cavity" are simply false for many patients.
How it works
Give each possible world a probability. The probability of a sentence is the sum over the worlds where it is true. New evidence changes beliefs by conditioning: throw away the worlds the evidence rules out and rescale the rest.
Kolmogorov axiomsExam favourite
0 ≤ P(ω) ≤ 1 for every world ω, and the sum of P(ω) over all worlds is 1
Everything else follows from these.
Probability of a sentence
P(φ) = Σ P(ω) over worlds ω where φ is true
A sentence is a set of worlds.
Inclusion and exclusion
P(a ∨ b) = P(a) + P(b) − P(a ∧ b)
Do not count the shared worlds twice.
Conditional probabilityExam favourite
P(a | b) = P(a ∧ b) ÷ P(b), when P(b) > 0
Belief in a after learning only b.
Remember
Why these rules and no others: de Finetti (1931) showed that an agent whose beliefs break the axioms can be offered a set of bets it accepts and always loses (a Dutch book). So a rational agent that bets on its beliefs must follow the axioms.
Common mistake
P(a | b) is not "if b then a with probability p". It is belief in a when b is all the agent knows. If more evidence c arrives, the agent must use P(a | b ∧ c).

The full joint distribution and inference by enumeration

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 13.3, figure 13.3 (the dentist example).

What it is
A random variable has a set of possible values, such as Cavity ∈ {true, false}. The full joint distribution P(Toothache, Catch, Cavity) gives the probability of every combination of values.
Why we need it
From the full joint we can answer every question about these variables: a marginal such as P(cavity), or a conditional such as P(cavity | toothache).
How it works
Marginalise (sum out) the variables we do not care about. Condition by keeping only the rows that match the evidence. Normalise by dividing by the total that is left, written with α = 1 ÷ P(evidence).
Marginalisation
P(Y) = Σ over z of P(Y, z)
Sum out the other variables Z.
Inference by enumerationExam favourite
P(X | e) = α P(X, e) = α Σ over y of P(X, e, y)
X query, e evidence, y hidden variables.
Size of the full joint
2ⁿ entries for n true or false variables (2ⁿ − 1 free numbers)
This is why we need Bayesian networks.
Worked example: P(cavity ∨ toothache)
  1. The worlds where cavity or toothache is true: all four cavity rows, plus the two no cavity rows with a toothache.
  2. Cavity rows: 0.108 + 0.012 + 0.072 + 0.008 = 0.2.
  3. No cavity, toothache rows: 0.016 + 0.064 = 0.08.
  4. P(cavity ∨ toothache) = 0.2 + 0.08 = 0.28
Lesson 3 of 8

Bayes' rule

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 13.5. Thomas Bayes (1763) and Pierre Simon Laplace (1812).

What it is
Bayes' rule turns a probability around: from P(effect | cause), which is easy to measure, to P(cause | effect), which we need for diagnosis.
Why we need it
A doctor knows how often a disease causes a symptom. That number stays the same in an epidemic. The chance that a symptom means the disease changes with how common the disease is, and Bayes’ rule takes care of that.
How it works
Multiply the likelihood P(e | c) by the prior P(c), then divide by P(e). Find P(e) by adding the same product over every possible cause (total probability).
Bayes' ruleExam favourite
P(c | e) = P(e | c) P(c) ÷ P(e)
posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ evidence.
Total probability
P(e) = P(e | c) P(c) + P(e | ¬c) P(¬c)
The bottom line of Bayes’ rule.
Odds form
posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio, where the likelihood ratio is P(e | c) ÷ P(e | ¬c)
Easy to update one test at a time.
Worked example: the medical test (base rate)
  1. Prior: 1 percent of people have the disease, P(d) = 0.01.
  2. The test finds 90 percent of real cases, P(+ | d) = 0.9, and wrongly says positive for 9 percent of healthy people, P(+ | ¬d) = 0.09.
  3. P(+) = 0.9 × 0.01 + 0.09 × 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0891 = 0.0981.
  4. P(d | +) = 0.009 ÷ 0.0981.
  5. P(d | +) ≈ 0.0917, only about 9 percent
Common mistake
The base rate fallacy: most people guess about 90 percent for the test above. Healthy people are so many that their few false positives outnumber the true positives.
Lesson 4 of 8

Independence, conditional independence and naive Bayes

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., sections 13.4 and 13.5.2, and the naive Bayes text classifier in section 13.5.3 (4th ed., 12.6).

What it is
Two variables are independent when learning one tells nothing about the other: P(a | b) = P(a). They are conditionally independent given C when, once C is known, one tells nothing more about the other: P(a | b, c) = P(a | c).
Why we need it
Independence splits a huge joint table into small pieces. Absolute independence is rare, but conditional independence is common: Toothache and Catch both depend on Cavity, but given Cavity they no longer depend on each other.
How it works
Write the joint as a product of small tables. With a cause and n effects that are conditionally independent given the cause, the joint needs 2n + 1 numbers instead of 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1. This is the naive Bayes model.
Independence
P(a ∧ b) = P(a) P(b)
Same as P(a | b) = P(a).
Conditional independenceExam favourite
P(a ∧ b | c) = P(a | c) P(b | c)
Written a ⫫ b | c.
Naive Bayes
P(Cause, e₁, ..., eₙ) = P(Cause) × Π P(eᵢ | Cause)
Then P(Cause | e) = α P(Cause) Π P(eᵢ | Cause).
Laplace (add one) smoothing
P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + 1) ÷ (total words in c + vocabulary size)
A word never seen with class c no longer gives probability 0.
Worked example: check Toothache ⫫ Catch | Cavity in the dentist table
  1. P(toothache ∧ catch | cavity) = 0.108 ÷ 0.2 = 0.54.
  2. P(toothache | cavity) = (0.108 + 0.012) ÷ 0.2 = 0.6.
  3. P(catch | cavity) = (0.108 + 0.072) ÷ 0.2 = 0.9.
  4. 0.6 × 0.9 = 0.54, so they are conditionally independent given Cavity
Remember
Naive Bayes is called naive because the effects are rarely truly independent given the class. It still classifies well in practice (spam filters, document topics), because the class with the highest score is often right even when the probabilities themselves are off.
Lesson 5 of 8

The maths: rules every probability step uses

Product rule
P(a ∧ b) = P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a)
Bayes’ rule comes from these two forms.
Chain ruleExam favourite
P(x₁, ..., xₙ) = Π over i of P(xᵢ | x₁, ..., xᵢ₋₁)
Always true. Bayesian networks shorten each condition.
Bayes with background evidence
P(c | e, b) = P(e | c, b) P(c | b) ÷ P(e | b)
Keep b on every term.
Expected value
E[X] = Σ x P(X = x)
Used again for decisions and for reinforcement learning.
Variance
Var[X] = E[X²] − (E[X])²
How spread out X is.
Worked example: Monty Hall with Bayes’ rule
  1. The car is behind door 1, 2 or 3, each with prior 1/3. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is, opens door 3 and shows a goat.
  2. Likelihood of "host opens 3": if the car is behind 1, he picks 2 or 3 at random, so 1/2. If behind 2, he must open 3, so 1. If behind 3, he cannot open it, so 0.
  3. P(host opens 3) = 1/2 × 1/3 + 1 × 1/3 + 0 × 1/3 = 1/2.
  4. P(car behind 2 | host opens 3) = (1 × 1/3) ÷ 1/2 = 2/3.
  5. switching wins with probability 2/3, staying with 1/3
Going deeper: Where probabilities come from

The frequentist view says a probability is a long run frequency. The Bayesian (subjective) view says it is a degree of belief that must obey the axioms, updated with Bayes’ rule. The principle of indifference gives equal probability to symmetric outcomes. AI systems mostly take the Bayesian view, while learning the numbers from data (chapter on learning in AIMA chapter 20).

Bottom line: probability is the standard because it is the only system that rational betting forces (Dutch book) and because Bayesian networks make it efficient.

Real world scenario: A spam filter that learns

Paul Graham’s 2002 essay "A Plan for Spam" made naive Bayes spam filters popular. Each word gets P(word | spam) and P(word | ham) from mail the user has already sorted. A new message is spam when α P(spam) Π P(wordᵢ | spam) is larger than the same product for ham. Smoothing keeps a word seen only in ham from making spam impossible.

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Probability is a number for how sure you are. When you learn something new, cross out the cases that cannot happen and share the belief among the cases left.

Axioms, the full joint, marginalising and conditioning, Bayes’ rule with total probability, independence and conditional independence, naive Bayes with smoothing.

Exchangeability (de Finetti), conjugate priors (Beta for a coin, Dirichlet for word counts), maximum likelihood versus maximum a posteriori estimates, and why the full joint is exponential: the motivation for graphical models.

Research: probabilistic programming languages (Stan, Pyro) that compile models into inference code, calibration of neural network probabilities, and imprecise probability for agents that cannot pin down a single number.

Chapter summary

  1. Probability measures degree of belief. The Kolmogorov axioms, forced by the Dutch book argument, fix its rules.
  2. The full joint distribution answers every query: marginalise hidden variables, keep worlds that match the evidence, normalise with α.
  3. Bayes' rule P(c | e) = P(e | c) P(c) ÷ P(e) turns causal knowledge into diagnosis. Watch the base rate.
  4. Independence and conditional independence break the joint into small factors. Naive Bayes needs 2n + 1 numbers for a cause with n effects.
  5. The full joint has 2ⁿ entries, too many for real problems. Bayesian networks, next, fix this.
