Lesson 1 of 8

Seeing, doing and imagining: the ladder of causation

Source: Pearl (2009), Causality, 2nd ed., Pearl and Mackenzie (2018), The Book of Why, and AIMA 4th ed., section 13.5 (causal networks). The uploaded slides and transcripts do not cover this topic.

What it is
A causal network is a Bayesian network whose arrows mean "directly causes". It answers three kinds of question: seeing (what does the evidence tell me), doing (what happens if I act), and imagining (what would have happened if things had been different).
Why we need it
Probability alone mixes up correlation and cause. Ice cream sales and drownings rise together because hot weather causes both. Banning ice cream would not save swimmers. An agent that acts on the world needs to know what its actions cause, not only what goes with what.
How it works
Seeing uses conditioning: P(y | x). Doing uses the do operator: P(y | do(x)) means we set X to x by our own hand, which cuts every arrow into X. Imagining uses a structural causal model to replay one case with a change.
RungQuestionWritten asExample
1. Association (seeing)What if I see x?P(y | x)Patients who took the drug recovered more often.
2. Intervention (doing)What if I do x?P(y | do(x))Would recovery rise if we gave everyone the drug?
3. Counterfactual (imagining)What if I had done x instead?P(y_x | x′, y′)This patient took the drug and recovered. Would they have recovered without it?
Common mistake
A plain Bayesian network can be read in a non causal order and still give the right P(y | x). It can only answer rung 2 questions when its arrows are causal.
Lesson 2 of 8

The do operator and graph surgery

Source: Pearl (2009), sections 1.3 and 3.2, and Spirtes, Glymour and Scheines (1993), the manipulation theorem.

What it is
do(X = x) is an action that sets X to x from outside the system. In the graph it removes every arrow into X and fixes X. The rest of the network stays the same.
Why we need it
When we observe the sprinkler on, it also tells us the day is probably not cloudy, so rain is less likely. When we switch the sprinkler on ourselves, the weather is untouched. Conditioning cannot tell these apart, and the do operator can.
How it works
Use the truncated product: the same product of CPTs as the network, but leave out the table of X and plug in X = x everywhere.
Truncated factorisationExam favourite
P(v | do(x)) = Π over i with Vᵢ not X of P(vᵢ | parents(Vᵢ)), with X = x
The table of X is removed.
Root causes
if X has no parents, P(y | do(x)) = P(y | x)
Nothing to cut, so seeing equals doing.
Average causal effect
ACE = P(y | do(x)) − P(y | do(¬x))
The quantity a randomised trial estimates.
Worked example: seeing versus doing for the sprinkler
  1. Seeing: P(Rain | Sprinkler = true) = Σ_c P(c) P(s | c) P(r | c) ÷ Σ_c P(c) P(s | c) = (0.5 × 0.1 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.2) ÷ (0.05 + 0.25).
  2. That is 0.09 ÷ 0.3 = 0.3. The sprinkler being on suggests a sunny day.
  3. Doing: cut Cloudy → Sprinkler. Rain is untouched: P(Rain | do(s)) = Σ_c P(c) P(r | c) = 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.2.
  4. P(Rain | do(Sprinkler = true)) = 0.5, not 0.3
Remember
A randomised controlled trial (Fisher, 1935) performs do(X) in the real world: a coin, not the patient or the doctor, decides the treatment, so every arrow into the treatment is cut.
Lesson 3 of 8

Confounders, the back door criterion and Simpson's paradox

Source: Pearl (1993, 2009), section 3.3, Simpson (1951), and Charig and others (1986) for the kidney stone data.

What it is
A confounder is a common cause of the treatment and the outcome. It opens a back door path T ← Z → R, so the observed link between T and R mixes the real effect with the effect of Z.
Why we need it
We often cannot run an experiment. The back door criterion says when we can still compute P(r | do(t)) from observational data, and how.
How it works
A set Z satisfies the back door criterion for (T, R) when no node in Z is a descendant of T, and Z blocks every path between T and R that starts with an arrow into T. Then adjust: average P(r | t, z) over the population share P(z).
Back door adjustmentExam favourite
P(r | do(t)) = Σ_z P(r | t, z) P(z)
Weight each group by its share of everyone, not its share among those treated.
Compare with seeing
P(r | t) = Σ_z P(r | t, z) P(z | t)
The only difference is P(z | t) in place of P(z).
Role of ZGraphAdjust for Z?
ConfounderT ← Z → RYes: it closes the back door
MediatorT → Z → RNo: it would remove part of the effect you want
ColliderT → Z ← RNo: conditioning on it creates a false link (Berkson’s paradox)
Common mistake
Simpson’s paradox is not a fault in arithmetic. Which answer is right, the pooled one or the one inside groups, depends on the causal graph. With stone size as a confounder, the group answer (A is better) is right. If Z were a mediator, the pooled answer would be right.
Lesson 4 of 8

Counterfactuals with structural causal models

Source: Pearl (2009), chapter 7, and Balke and Pearl (1994).

What it is
A structural causal model (SCM) writes each variable as a function of its parents and a hidden noise term U: Y = f(X, U). A counterfactual asks what Y would have been in this very case if X had been different.
Why we need it
Questions about blame, fairness and regret are counterfactual: "would she have been hired if she were a man", "would the patient have lived without the drug". Rung 2 answers are about populations. Rung 3 answers are about one case.
How it works
Three steps. Abduction: use what happened to work out the noise U for this case. Action: replace the equation of X by X = x′. Prediction: compute Y from the changed model with the same U.
Worked example: Y = 2X + U
  1. We saw X = 1 and Y = 3.
  2. Abduction: 3 = 2 × 1 + U, so U = 1 for this case.
  3. Action: set X = 0.
  4. Prediction: Y = 2 × 0 + 1.
  5. Y would have been 1
Going deeper: Identification and the do calculus

Pearl’s three rules of the do calculus (1995) turn expressions with do into expressions without it whenever that is possible. The calculus is complete: if the rules cannot remove every do, no method can compute the effect from observational data alone (Shpitser and Pearl, 2006, Huang and Valtorta, 2006). The front door adjustment P(y | do(x)) = Σ_m P(m | x) Σ_x′ P(y | m, x′) P(x′) handles a hidden confounder when the effect passes through an observed mediator M, as in smoking → tar → cancer.

Lesson 5 of 8

The maths: adjustment, front door and effects

Back doorExam favourite
P(y | do(x)) = Σ_z P(y | x, z) P(z)
Z blocks every back door path and has no descendants of X.
Front door
P(y | do(x)) = Σ_m P(m | x) Σ_x′ P(y | m, x′) P(x′)
M carries all of the effect and has no hidden common cause with X.
Inverse probability weighting
P(y | do(x)) = Σ over units with X = x of 1 ÷ (N P(x | z))
Same answer as back door adjustment, used with many variables in Z.
Average causal effect
ACE = P(y | do(x)) − P(y | do(¬x))
Kidney stones: 0.833 − 0.779 ≈ 0.054.
Worked example: a front door problem
  1. P(x) = 0.5. P(m | x) = 0.9, P(m | ¬x) = 0.1. P(y | m, x) = 0.6, P(y | m, ¬x) = 0.4, P(y | ¬m, x) = 0.3, P(y | ¬m, ¬x) = 0.1.
  2. For m: Σ_x′ P(y | m, x′) P(x′) = 0.6 × 0.5 + 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.5.
  3. For ¬m: 0.3 × 0.5 + 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.2.
  4. Weight by P(m | x): 0.9 × 0.5 + 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.45 + 0.02.
  5. P(y | do(x)) = 0.47

Bottom line: run an experiment when you can. When you cannot, draw the causal graph first, then pick the formula the graph allows.

Real world scenario: Causal reasoning in practice

Medical guidelines rest on randomised trials because they cut every arrow into the treatment. Online companies run A/B tests, which are small randomised trials, before changing a web page. Economists won the 2021 Nobel prize (Card, Angrist and Imbens) for natural experiments and instrumental variables. In AI, causal models help with fairness (would the decision change if only the protected attribute changed) and with robustness, since causal links stay the same when the data distribution shifts.

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Seeing two things together does not mean one causes the other. To find out, change one thing yourself and watch what happens to the other.

A causal network adds meaning to the arrows. do(x) cuts the arrows into X. The truncated product, the back door adjustment and Simpson’s paradox follow from this.

The do calculus is complete for identification. Counterfactuals need structural equations, not just CPTs. Markov equivalent graphs differ in causal meaning, so data alone cannot always fix the arrows.

Research: causal representation learning (finding causal variables in raw data such as images), causal reinforcement learning, invariant risk minimisation, and testing whether large language models can reason about interventions and counterfactuals.

Chapter summary

  1. Three rungs: seeing P(y | x), doing P(y | do(x)), imagining counterfactuals.
  2. do(x) cuts the arrows into X. The truncated product drops the table of X. Seeing and doing agree for root causes.
  3. Back door adjustment: P(r | do(t)) = Σ_z P(r | t, z) P(z) when Z blocks every back door path and is not caused by T.
  4. Simpson’s paradox: kidney stone treatment A looks worse overall (78 against 83 percent) but is better in each group, and better under do (83.3 against 77.9 percent).
  5. Counterfactuals: abduction, action, prediction on a structural causal model.
