Lesson 1 of 7

Exact inference by enumeration

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 14.4.1 (4th ed., 13.3.1), figures 14.8 and 14.9.

What it is
Inference means computing P(query | evidence) from the network. Enumeration writes the answer as a sum of products of CPT entries, summing over every value of every hidden variable.
Why we need it
It is the simplest correct method and the reference that every faster method must agree with.
How it works
Go through the variables in topological order. For a query or evidence variable, multiply in its table entry. For a hidden variable, try both values, recurse, and add. Do this for each query value, then normalise.
EnumerationExam favourite
P(B | j, m) = α Σ_e Σ_a P(B) P(e) P(a | B, e) P(j | a) P(m | a)
Hidden variables E and A are summed out.
Moving factors outside
α P(B) Σ_e P(e) Σ_a P(a | B, e) P(j | a) P(m | a)
P(B) does not depend on e or a, so it moves out of both sums.
Cost
time O(2ⁿ), memory O(n)
Depth first over the hidden variables.
Worked example: P(b, j, m) for the burglary network
  1. P(b) = 0.001. For each e and a, multiply P(e) P(a | b, e) P(j | a) P(m | a).
  2. e true: 0.002 × (0.95 × 0.9 × 0.7 + 0.05 × 0.05 × 0.01) = 0.002 × 0.598525.
  3. e false: 0.998 × (0.94 × 0.9 × 0.7 + 0.06 × 0.05 × 0.01) = 0.998 × 0.59223.
  4. Sum: 0.00119705 + 0.59104554 = 0.59224259. Times P(b): 0.00059224.
  5. P(¬b, j, m) ≈ 0.0014919 in the same way, so P(b | j, m) = 0.00059224 ÷ 0.00208414 ≈ 0.284
Common mistake
Enumeration repeats work: P(j | a) P(m | a) is computed again for each value of e. Variable elimination stores such parts once.
Lesson 2 of 7

Variable elimination

Source: Zhang and Poole (1994), Dechter (1999), and AIMA 3rd ed., section 14.4.2.

What it is
Variable elimination does the sums of enumeration from the inside out and keeps the results as tables called factors, so nothing is computed twice.
Why we need it
It turns many repeated products into a few table operations. On a polytree (a network with at most one path between any two nodes) it runs in time linear in the size of the network.
How it works
Make one factor per CPT with the evidence plugged in. For each hidden variable in some order: multiply all factors that mention it (a pointwise product) and sum the variable out. At the end multiply what is left and normalise.
Pointwise product
f(X, Y) × g(Y, Z) = h(X, Y, Z), with h(x, y, z) = f(x, y) g(y, z)
The result covers the union of the variables.
Summing outExam favourite
h(X, Z) = Σ_y f(X, y, Z)
Add the entries for each value of the removed variable.
Cost
exponential in the size of the largest factor made, which depends on the order
The best possible largest factor size, minus one, is the treewidth of the graph.
Remember
Irrelevant variables: every variable that is not an ancestor of the query or the evidence can be removed before starting. Summing it out only gives factors full of 1s, as the second tip of the animation shows.
Going deeper: Complexity of exact inference

On polytrees, variable elimination and Pearl’s message passing are linear. In general, exact inference in Bayesian networks is #P-hard (Cooper, 1990), because it can count the solutions of a SAT formula. Finding the best elimination order is NP-hard, so programs use greedy rules such as min fill or min degree. The junction tree algorithm (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter, 1988) groups variables into clusters so that all marginals come from one pass of messages.

Lesson 3 of 7

Approximate inference by sampling

Source: AIMA 3rd ed., section 14.5 (4th ed., 13.4). Likelihood weighting: Fung and Chang (1989), Shachter and Peot (1989). Gibbs sampling: Geman and Geman (1984).

What it is
Sampling methods draw many random worlds from the network and count. The fraction of samples where the query holds estimates its probability.
Why we need it
Exact inference can take exponential time on big networks with many loops. Sampling gives an answer of any wanted accuracy, and more samples give a better answer.
How it works
Prior sampling sets each variable from its CPT, parents first. Rejection sampling throws away samples that disagree with the evidence. Likelihood weighting fixes the evidence and weights each sample by how likely that evidence was. Gibbs sampling walks from world to world, resampling one hidden variable at a time from its Markov blanket.
Rejection sampling
P(X | e) ≈ N(X, e) ÷ N(e)
Consistent, but wastes the fraction 1 − P(e) of samples.
Likelihood weightExam favourite
w = Π over evidence variables Eᵢ of P(eᵢ | parents(Eᵢ))
Estimate: sum of weights with X true ÷ sum of all weights.
Gibbs step
P(x | mb(X)) = α P(x | parents(X)) Π over children Y of P(y | parents(Y))
Only the Markov blanket is needed.
Error of an estimate
standard error ≈ √(p (1 − p) ÷ N)
Four times the samples halves the error.
Worked example: one likelihood weighted sample for P(Rain | Sprinkler = true, WetGrass = true)
  1. Cloudy is sampled from P(Cloudy) = 0.5: say true.
  2. Sprinkler is evidence (true): weight × P(s | c) = 0.1.
  3. Rain is sampled from P(Rain | c) = 0.8: say true.
  4. WetGrass is evidence (true): weight × P(w | s, r) = 0.99.
  5. w = 0.1 × 0.99 = 0.099, the textbook example
Common mistake
Likelihood weighting suffers when evidence comes late in the order (at the leaves): the samples ignore it while choosing the earlier variables, so most of them get tiny weights.
Lesson 4 of 7

The maths: why the samplers give the right answer

Prior sampling is consistent
S(x₁, ..., xₙ) = Π P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ)) = P(x₁, ..., xₙ)
Each world is drawn with exactly its probability.
Likelihood weighting is consistentExam favourite
S_WS(z, e) w(z, e) = Π P(zᵢ | parents) × Π P(eᵢ | parents) = P(z, e)
The sampling distribution times the weight is the true joint.
Gibbs sampling
the posterior P(x | e) is the stationary distribution of the Markov chain
Detailed balance: π(x) q(x → x′) = π(x′) q(x′ → x).
Variable elimination size
eliminating X makes a factor over all variables that share a factor with X
Bad orders build large factors.
Worked example: a Gibbs step for Cloudy, with Sprinkler = true and Rain = false
  1. The Markov blanket of Cloudy is its two children, Sprinkler and Rain. Cloudy has no parents, and its children have no other parents.
  2. Cloudy true: P(c) P(s | c) P(¬r | c) = 0.5 × 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.01.
  3. Cloudy false: 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.2.
  4. P(Cloudy | s, ¬r) = 0.01 ÷ 0.21 ≈ 0.0476

Bottom line: use variable elimination or the junction tree when the treewidth is small, and Gibbs sampling or other MCMC methods when it is not.

Real world scenario: Inference at work

Turbo codes and LDPC codes in mobile phones and Wi-Fi are decoded with belief propagation on a loopy graph (McEliece, MacKay and Cheng, 1998). Stan and PyMC run Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, a modern MCMC method, for statistics in medicine and science. Bayesian network tools such as Hugin and GeNIe use the junction tree for exact answers in medical and fault diagnosis.

Lesson 6 of 7

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Exact inference adds up every way the world could be. Sampling plays the world many times at random and counts what happened.

Enumeration sums products over hidden variables. Variable elimination stores factors and sums out one variable at a time. Rejection sampling, likelihood weighting and Gibbs sampling estimate the answer, with error about √(p(1 − p) ÷ N).

Exact inference is #P-hard, and even approximating it within a fixed error is NP-hard in general (Dagum and Luby, 1993). Treewidth decides exact cost. Variational methods turn inference into optimisation of a simpler distribution by minimising a KL divergence.

Research: amortised inference, where a neural network learns to output posteriors fast (as in variational autoencoders), sequential Monte Carlo (particle filters) for changing worlds, and knowledge compilation into arithmetic circuits for fast exact queries.

Lesson 7 of 7

Chapter summary

  1. Enumeration: P(X | e) = α Σ over hidden y of Π CPT entries. O(2ⁿ) time, O(n) memory. P(b | j, m) ≈ 0.284.
  2. Variable elimination multiplies factors and sums out hidden variables one at a time. Its cost depends on the largest factor, set by the order and the treewidth.
  3. Exact inference is linear on polytrees and #P-hard in general.
  4. Rejection sampling wastes samples that disagree with rare evidence. Likelihood weighting fixes evidence and weights samples by its likelihood.
  5. Gibbs sampling resamples one variable from its Markov blanket and converges to the posterior.
