Lesson 1 of 8

What intelligence and AI mean

Source: slide Set 1 (Introduction) and the class of 18 January 2026. Agents and environments follow Russell and Norvig, chapter 2.

Think of the people around you. A friend sees you carrying heavy bags, guesses you need help, and holds the door. Nobody told them to. They noticed, understood, and acted. That everyday skill is what we call intelligence: taking in what is around you, making sense of it, and choosing a sensible action.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the study and building of machines that do the same: sense their surroundings, reason about them, and act well. The slides start with a simple question: what makes humans different from other living things? We plan, we imagine, we explain, we help each other, and we learn from very few examples.

What it is
A field that builds computer programs that perceive, reason, learn and act so as to do well at a task.
Why we need it
Many tasks are too big, too fast or too boring for people: finding a route through a city, sorting spam, diagnosing faults, playing chess, steering a robot. AI lets a machine make those choices by itself.
How it works
We describe the task as an agent inside an environment. The agent gets percepts through sensors, and its agent program picks an action for its actuators. AI methods (search, logic, probability, learning) are different ways to write that program.

The four approaches to AI

Russell and Norvig sort definitions of AI along two questions: should the machine copy humans or be rational (do the right thing)? And should we judge its thinking or its acting?

Like a humanRationally (the right thing)
ThinkingThinking humanly: model how people think (cognitive science, brain studies).Thinking rationally: follow correct rules of reasoning (logic, the "laws of thought").
ActingActing humanly: behave so that people cannot tell it is a machine (the Turing test).Acting rationally: choose the action expected to do best (the rational agent approach, used in this course).
Remember
In class the four boxes were explained like this: thinking humanly is how we think, thinking rationally is making correct decisions in our head, acting humanly is doing what we think, and acting rationally is working it out so that it leads to a real, measurable result.

The Turing test (1950): a person chats by text with a hidden machine and a hidden human. If the person cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine passes. Turing also said we should not try to build an adult mind directly, but a baby machine that can learn, and then teach it. The lecture repeated this idea: study babies, not adults.

A social machine: the man machine symbiosis scene

The slides show a scene with a person and a helpful robot. To behave well in that scene the robot needs much more than a fast processor. It needs:

SkillWhat it means in plain wordsExample in the scene
Social etiquetteKnowing polite and expected behaviourWaiting for the person to finish speaking
Intuitive psychologySensing that someone is in trouble or worried, and wanting to helpNoticing the person is struggling and offering a hand
Awareness of its own limits (proprioception)Knowing what its own body can and cannot doNot trying to lift something too heavy
Common senseEveryday facts everyone knowsGlass breaks if dropped
Domain knowledgeExpert facts about the taskHow a machine is repaired
Intuitive physicsA feel for how objects move and fallA ball rolls down a slope

The slides then list the layers of behaviour such a machine shows, which come from Marvin Minsky's Society of Mind (1986): instinctive (built in reactions), learned (habits from experience), deliberative (thinking through options), reflective (thinking about its own thinking), self reflective (thinking about itself as a person with goals), and self conscious (caring about its values, such as "scoring a good deed point").

Real world scenario: a delivery robot in an office

A delivery robot meets a person carrying a heavy box near a closed door. An instinctive layer stops it from bumping into them. A learned layer knows doors in this building open outward. A deliberative layer plans: put down my parcel, open the door, wait. A reflective layer notices "last time I blocked the corridor, so stand to the side". Today's robots do the first three layers well. The last layers are still research.

The questions slide asks what thoughts are, how we build a model of the world, and what makes curiosity and creativity work. These are open research questions. This course focuses on the parts we can already build well: search, reasoning, planning, probability and learning.

Lesson 3 of 8

Agents, environments and PEAS

An agent is anything that senses its environment and acts on it. A person, a thermostat, a chess program and a self driving car are all agents.

Agent functionExam favourite
f : P* → A
Maps every percept sequence (everything the agent has sensed so far, P*) to an action in the set A. It is the mathematical description of behaviour.
Agent program
agent = architecture + program
The program runs on the physical machine (the architecture) and computes the agent function one percept at a time.
Rational agentExam favourite
choose a* = argmaxa E[performance | percepts so far, a]
For every percept sequence, pick the action that is expected to give the best performance measure, given what the agent knows.

Rational is not the same as all knowing. A rational agent does the best it can with the information it has. If it crosses the road after looking both ways and is hit by a falling plane, it was still rational. Rational agents also gather information (look before crossing) and learn from experience.

Four kinds of agent program, from simple to clever

From simple to clever
  1. Simple reflex agent: if this, then that. It looks only at the current percept. Fast, but it fails when it cannot see the whole state.
  2. Model based reflex agent: keeps an internal state (a model of the world it cannot see right now), updated from each percept.
  3. Goal based agent: knows what it wants to reach, and searches or plans a sequence of actions to get there. This is where search (chapters 1.2 to 4.2) comes in.
  4. Utility based agent: when many outcomes reach the goal, prefers the ones with higher utility (happiness). It handles trade offs and uncertainty.
  5. Learning agent: any of the above, plus a part that improves the program from feedback (a critic and a learning element). Reinforcement learning, chapter 10, is one way.
Common mistake
A table driven agent stores one action for every possible percept sequence. With 4 percepts and a lifetime of T steps it needs 4 + 4² + ... + 4T rows. For a taxi camera this number is larger than the atoms in the universe. Agent programs exist to produce good behaviour from a small program instead of a huge table.

The maths behind a rational agent

Two small formulas sit under the whole course.

Performance over a lifetime
U = ∑t=1T rt
Add up the score rt earned at each time step t. In the vacuum world rt = number of clean squares (minus 1 per move if moving costs energy).
Expected performance of an actionExam favourite
E[U | a] = ∑s′ P(s′ | a) U(s′)
Weight each possible result s′ by its probability. A rational agent picks the action with the largest value.
Size of a table driven agentExam favourite
rows = ∑t=1T |P|t = |P|(|P|T − 1) ⁄ (|P| − 1)
|P| is the number of different percepts, T the lifetime. A geometric series, so it grows exponentially in T.
Worked example: which action is rational?
  1. A delivery robot can take the lift or the stairs. The lift arrives in time with probability 0.7 and then scores 10. If it is late it scores 2.
  2. The stairs always arrive, but slowly, and score 7.
  3. E[lift] = 0.7 × 10 + 0.3 × 2 = 7 + 0.6 = 7.6
  4. E[stairs] = 1 × 7 = 7
  5. The rational choice is the lift, 7.6 against 7, even though the lift is sometimes late. Rational means best on average with what you know, not lucky every time.
Worked example: vacuum score
  1. Both squares dirty, agent in A, simple reflex agent, 4 time steps, no move cost.
  2. Time 1: Suck in A. A is clean, B dirty. Score +1, total 1.
  3. Time 2: Right. Still 1 clean square. Total 2.
  4. Time 3: Suck in B. Both clean. +2, total 4.
  5. Time 4: Left. Both clean, +2. Total = 6

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

An AI is a machine that notices what is going on and picks a good thing to do, like a helpful friend who sees you need help and opens the door.

An agent maps percept sequences to actions. Describe the task with PEAS and the environment with its six properties. Pick the simplest agent program (reflex, model based, goal based, utility based, learning) that works.

A rational agent maximises expected performance given its percept history and prior knowledge. Partial observability forces the agent to keep a belief state. Sequential, stochastic tasks lead to Markov decision processes (chapter 10.1).

Bounded rationality (Simon) asks what is optimal when computing itself is costly. Open questions include how to specify the performance measure so that it matches what people really want (the value alignment problem), and how common sense and the reflective layers of the slides can be learned rather than hand written.

Going deeper: why rationality is defined for each percept sequence

Rationality depends on four things: the performance measure, the agent's prior knowledge, the actions it can take, and the percept sequence so far. Two agents with the same actions can both be rational yet act differently, because one knows more. This is why we say "rational given what it knows", and why gathering information can itself be the rational action.

Going deeper: the research view: from Turing to large language models

AI as a field began at the Dartmouth workshop in 1956. Early systems used logic and search (1956 to 1970s), then expert systems (1980s), then probability and machine learning (1990s and 2000s), then deep learning (2012 onward) and large language models (2020s). Each wave added a new way to write the agent program. The agent view of this chapter covers all of them.

In the exam
A rational agent is not one that is always right. It is one that chooses the best action given what it knows. Bad luck does not make a choice irrational.

Chapter summary

  1. Intelligence means sensing, understanding and choosing a good action. AI builds machines that do this.
  2. Four approaches: thinking or acting, humanly or rationally. This course builds rational agents: act to get the best expected performance.
  3. An agent maps percept sequences to actions. Describe a task with PEAS and the environment by observability, determinism, episodes, change, discreteness and number of agents.
  4. Agent programs grow from simple reflex to model based, goal based, utility based and learning agents. A table driven agent is impossible in practice because it needs ∑ |P|t rows.
  5. A social machine needs etiquette, intuitive psychology, common sense and layers from instinctive to self conscious behaviour (Minsky).
