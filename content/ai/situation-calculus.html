Lesson 1 of 8

What planning is

Source: Russell and Norvig, AIMA 3rd ed., chapter 10 and 4th ed., chapter 11. The uploaded slides do not cover planning, so this step follows the textbook and the papers it cites.

What it is
Planning is finding a sequence of actions that takes an agent from a starting state to a state where its goals hold. Classical planning assumes the world is fully observable, deterministic, static and known.
Why we need it
A search agent (step 2) needs a hand written successor function and a heuristic for each new problem. A planning agent reads a general description of its actions in logic, so the same planner works in every domain, and it can use the structure of the description (which facts an action changes) to search much faster.
How it works
Describe states with logical facts, and actions with what they need (preconditions) and what they change (effects). Then search for a plan, either by logical deduction (this chapter), by searching through states with STRIPS actions (chapter 8.2), or by refining a partial plan (chapter 8.3).

STRIPS (Fikes and Nilsson, 1971)sets of ground factsprecondition, add list, delete listFast state space planners

Lesson 2 of 8

Situation calculus

Source: McCarthy and Hayes, 1969, and AIMA 3rd ed., section 10.4.2.

What it is
Situation calculus is a way to describe change in first order logic. A situation is a snapshot of the world after a sequence of actions. The function Result(a, s) names the situation after doing action a in situation s.
Why we need it
Plain first order logic has no notion of time: a fact is just true or false. To plan, we must say what is true before and after each action, and reason about sequences of actions.
How it works
Add one extra argument, the situation, to every relation that can change. At(Robot, A, S0) says the robot is in room A at the start. Write axioms that say when an action is possible and how it changes these relations.
PartMeaningExample
Initial situation S0the startAt(Robot, A, S0), At(Gold, B, S0)
Result(a, s)the situation after action a in sS1 = Result(Go(B), S0)
Fluenta relation or function whose value changes, with the situation as last argumentHolding(Gold, s)
Atemporal (eternal) predicatetrue or false in every situationGold(G1), Adjacent(A, B)
Possibility axiomwhen an action can be donePoss(Grab, s) ⇔ ∃x At(Gold, x, s) ∧ At(Robot, x, s) ∧ ¬Holding(Gold, s)
Effect axiomwhat an action changesPoss(Grab, s) ⇒ Holding(Gold, Result(Grab, s))
Lesson 3 of 8

The frame problem and successor state axioms

Source: McCarthy and Hayes, 1969 (the frame problem), and Reiter, 1991 (successor state axioms).

Effect axioms say what changes. They say nothing about what stays the same. After Go(B), is the gold still in room B? Nothing in the effect axioms of Go says so, so the logic cannot conclude it. This is the frame problem.

Version of the problemWhat goes wrong
Representational frame problemWriting a frame axiom such as At(Gold, x, s) ∧ ¬Holding(Gold, s) ⇒ At(Gold, x, Result(Go(y), s)) for every pair of action and fluent: A × F axioms
Inferential frame problemCarrying every unchanged fact forward one action at a time costs time for every fact at every step
Qualification problemListing every condition for an action to succeed is endless: the robot can grab the gold unless it is glued down, too heavy, ...
Ramification problemIndirect effects: when the robot moves while holding the gold, the gold moves too
Successor state axiom (one per fluent F)Exam favourite
Poss(a, s) ⇒ [F(Result(a, s)) ⇔ (a made F true) ∨ (F(s) ∧ a did not make F false)]
A fluent is true after an action if the action made it true, or if it was already true and the action did not make it false.
The successor state axioms of the robot world
  1. Holding(Gold, Result(a, s)) ⇔ a = Grab ∨ (Holding(Gold, s) ∧ a ≠ Release).
  2. At(Robot, x, Result(a, s)) ⇔ a = Go(x) ∨ (At(Robot, x, s) ∧ ¬∃y a = Go(y)).
  3. At(Gold, x, Result(a, s)) ⇔ (Holding(Gold, s) ∧ a = Go(x)) ∨ (At(Gold, x, s) ∧ ¬(Holding(Gold, s) ∧ ∃y a = Go(y))). This also handles the ramification: the held gold moves with the robot.
  4. Unique action axioms say different action names are different: Grab ≠ Release, Go(x) ≠ Grab, and Go(x) = Go(y) only if x = y.
  5. F successor state axioms replace A × F frame axioms
Lesson 4 of 8

Deductive planning: a plan is a proof

Source: Green, 1969, "Application of theorem proving to problem solving".

What it is
In deductive planning the goal is a query to a theorem prover: is there a situation s in which the goal holds? A constructive proof must name such an s, and that name is the plan.
Why we need it
It uses one general reasoner for everything: the same prover answers questions and makes plans. Every plan comes with a proof that it works.
How it works
Ask ∃s At(Gold, A, s) ∧ ¬Holding(Gold, s). A resolution prover with answer extraction (it keeps track of what the variable s was bound to) returns s = Result(Release, Result(Go(A), Result(Grab, Result(Go(B), S0)))). Read the term from the inside out to get the plan: Go(B), Grab, Go(A), Release.
Remember
The search above is not a resolution prover. It looks through the same situation terms breadth first, which is what a fair prover ends up doing for this small problem, and it finds the same answer term.
Strength of deductive planningWeakness
Very expressive: quantifiers, functions, partial knowledgeGeneral theorem provers are slow and have no planning heuristics
A proof that the plan worksNo guarantee of a short plan
Clear meaning (semantics)The frame problem must be handled with care, and the search space of proofs is huge

These weaknesses led Fikes and Nilsson to STRIPS in 1971: keep the idea of preconditions and effects, but drop full logic and search over states directly. That is chapter 8.2.

The maths: counting axioms and situations

Frame axioms neededExam favourite
A × F
One for each action and fluent pair that the action does not change. 20 actions and 50 fluents: up to 1,000.
Successor state axioms neededExam favourite
F
One per fluent, however many actions there are. 50 fluents: 50 axioms.
Situations up to depth d
1 + b + b² + ... + bd
b actions possible in each situation. Situations are terms, so two different action sequences are always two different situations, even when the world looks the same.
Worked example: 4 actions, depth 3
  1. Depth 0: 1 situation (S0).
  2. Depth 1: 4. Depth 2: 16. Depth 3: 64.
  3. 1 + 4 + 16 + 64 = 85.
  4. 85 situation terms, but only 6 different world states in the robot world
Worked example: is Release possible in S2 = Result(Grab, Result(Go(B), S0))?
  1. Poss(Release, s) ⇔ Holding(Gold, s).
  2. Holding(Gold, Result(Grab, S1)) ⇔ Grab = Grab ∨ ... which is true.
  3. So Holding(Gold, S2) holds.
  4. Release is possible in S2

Situation calculus against other ways to describe actions

Representing actions
Situation calculusSTRIPS and PDDLEvent calculus
LogicFull first orderGround facts, no quantifiers in statesFirst order, time points
Frame problemSolved by successor state axiomsSolved by the STRIPS assumption: facts not in the delete list staySolved by circumscription over events
Actions at the same timeHardNo (sequential plans)Yes, events happen at time points
Planner speedSlow (theorem proving)Fast (heuristic search)Slow
Used forTheory, GOLOG robot programs, reasoning about what is knownPractical planners, competitionsNarratives, commonsense reasoning

Bottom line: situation calculus gives the clearest meaning to actions and change. STRIPS keeps the useful part, preconditions and effects, and gives up expressiveness for speed.

Real world scenario: Shakey the robot

Shakey (SRI, 1966 to 1972) was the first mobile robot that reasoned about its own actions. Its first planner, QA3, used Green's deductive planning. It was too slow, so the team built STRIPS, and also the A* search algorithm (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael, 1968) to plan its routes.

Lesson 7 of 8

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Keep a diary of the world: what is true at the start, and after each action. A rule for each fact says how one action changes it, and everything else stays as it was.

Situations S0, Result(a, s). Fluents take a situation argument. Possibility and effect axioms. The frame problem and Reiter's successor state axioms. Green's deductive planning extracts a plan from a proof of ∃s Goal(s).

Reiter's basic action theories make regression possible: to check if a goal holds after a plan, rewrite it step by step into a question about S0 only. GOLOG (1997) builds a programming language on top of situation calculus, and knowledge fluents describe what the agent knows after sensing.

Research continues on epistemic planning (planning about other agents' knowledge), verification of GOLOG agent programs, and on mixing logic based action theories with probabilistic and learned models.

Lesson 8 of 8

Chapter summary

  1. Classical planning finds action sequences in a fully observable, deterministic, static and known world.
  2. Situation calculus names situations with S0 and Result(a, s). Fluents take the situation as an extra argument. Possibility and effect axioms describe actions.
  3. The frame problem: effect axioms do not say what stays the same. Successor state axioms fix it with one axiom per fluent instead of A × F frame axioms.
  4. Deductive planning (Green, 1969) proves ∃s Goal(s). The binding of s, read from the inside out, is the plan.
  5. Theorem proving is slow for planning, which led to STRIPS.
