Decisions that unfold over time

In chapter 2.2 the firm chose once, at the start. Lecture 4 adds a second choice later. After it learns whether research worked, the firm decides whether to spend on a marketing campaign. Because the second decision can depend on what has already happened, the firm can react to news. That is what makes this a decision over time.

Backward inductionExam favourite
start at the last decision, replace each chance node by its expected value, keep the best action at each decision node, and move one level back
Repeat until you reach the root.
At a chance node
V(n) = ∑k pk V(childk)
Nature moves, so you average.
At a decision node
V(n) = maxa V(childa)
You move, so you take the best.
Lesson 2 of 3

Solving the R&D and marketing tree

Backward induction, exactly as in class
  1. After success: E[m] = 0.9 × 13 + 0.1 × (−7) = 11 and E[d] = 0.9 × 9 + 0.1 × (−1) = 8. Market, and carry 11 back.
  2. After failure: E[m] = 0.5 × 13 + 0.5 × (−7) = 3 and E[d] = 0.5 × 9 + 0.5 × (−1) = 4. Do not market, and carry 4 back.
  3. Research chance node: 0.625 × 11 + 0.375 × 4 = 6.875 + 1.5 = 8.375.
  4. Skip: 0.5 × 10 + 0.5 × 0 = 5.
  5. 8.375 > 5: go for research, market only if it succeeds
Common mistake
Never compare raw leaf payoffs where nature moves. 13 is not "better than" −7 in any useful sense at a chance node, because the firm does not choose between them. Convert every chance node to its expected value first, and compare only at nodes where the firm chooses.

The cross check: list every full strategy

A strategy is a complete plan that says what to do at every point you might reach, even points you will not reach. Here a strategy says what to do after success and what to do after failure.

After success, after failureExpected payoff
M, M (always market)0.625 × 11 + 0.375 × 3 = 8.000
M, D (market only after success)0.625 × 11 + 0.375 × 4 = 8.375, the best
D, M (market only after failure)0.625 × 8 + 0.375 × 3 = 6.125
D, D (never market)0.625 × 8 + 0.375 × 4 = 6.500

Both methods give 8.375. The professor's point was about effort. With k binary decision nodes there are 2k full strategies, and listing them quickly becomes hopeless. Backward induction visits each node once.

Real world scenario: a vaccine maker

How does a firm know research has succeeded? A vaccine maker learns it from clinical trial results. That observable signal is what lets the marketing decision depend on the trial. Planning "if the trial works we launch a big campaign, if not we keep it small" is exactly the M, D strategy.

Practice problem A: the film studio

A studio picks a big or a small budget. With a big budget, nature sets the audience buzz: good with 0.4, weak with 0.6. After seeing the buzz the studio decides whether to push extra marketing. A small budget gives 6 or 1 with equal chance.

Good (0.4)0.8: 18 or 00.8: 14 or 2

Fold the tree back
  1. Good buzz: push 0.8 × 18 + 0.2 × 0 = 14.4, no push 0.8 × 14 + 0.2 × 2 = 11.6. Push, value 14.4.
  2. Weak buzz: push 0.3 × 18 + 0.7 × (−4) = 2.6, no push 0.3 × 10 + 0.7 × 1 = 3.7. Hold back, value 3.7.
  3. Big budget: 0.4 × 14.4 + 0.6 × 3.7 = 5.76 + 2.22 = 7.98.
  4. Small budget: 0.5 × 6 + 0.5 × 1 = 3.5.
  5. Choose the big budget, push only on good buzz, value 7.98
Going deeper: Why backward induction is correct: the principle of optimality

Bellman's principle says that whatever the first decision, the rest of an optimal plan must be optimal for the situation that decision creates. Suppose the best plan used a sub plan that was not best from some node. Replacing it by the better sub plan raises the value of that node without changing the probability of reaching it, so the whole plan improves, which is a contradiction. Backward induction applies this from the leaves upward. Its cost is linear in the size of the tree, while listing strategies is exponential in the number of decision nodes.

One idea, four depths

Plan from the end. Ask what you would do at the last step in each situation, then use those answers to decide the step before, and so on back to today.

Average at chance nodes, take the max at decision nodes, and fold from the leaves to the root. A strategy is a full contingent plan. The R&D tree gives 8.375 by both folding and listing the four strategies.

Backward induction is dynamic programming on a finite tree and rests on Bellman's principle of optimality. In games it gives subgame perfect equilibria. With shared sub states the tree becomes a graph and memoisation turns exponential work into polynomial work.

Markov decision processes generalize the tree to cycles and infinite horizons, solved by value iteration V(s) = maxa Σ P(s′ | s, a)[r + γ V(s′)]. Monte Carlo tree search, as in AlphaGo, estimates the same backups by sampling when the tree is too large to fold exactly.
