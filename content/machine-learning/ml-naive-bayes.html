Lesson 1 of 11

Bayes rule as a classifier

Source: Manning, Raghavan and Schutze, Introduction to Information Retrieval chapter 13, Jurafsky and Martin, Speech and Language Processing chapter 4, Murphy, Machine Learning: A Probabilistic Perspective chapter 3.5, Ng and Jordan (2002), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 1.9.

What it is
Naive Bayes classifies with Bayes rule: P(class | features) ∝ P(class) × P(features | class). It makes one big simplification, the "naive" assumption: given the class, every feature is independent, so P(features | class) is just the product of one probability per feature.
Why we need it
It trains in a single pass of counting, needs very little data, handles thousands of features (like every word in a dictionary) with ease, and gives a probability. It was the first successful spam filter and is still a strong baseline for text.
How it works
Training: count how often each class appears (the prior) and how often each feature value appears within each class (the likelihoods). Prediction: for each class, multiply the prior by the likelihood of every feature of the new example, and pick the class with the largest product. Dividing by the sum turns the scores into probabilities.

Naive Bayes ruleExam favourite
P(c | x1, ..., xd) ∝ P(c) Πj P(xj | c)
Prior times one likelihood per feature.
PredictionExam favourite
ŷ = argmaxc [log P(c) + Σj log P(xj | c)]
Logs turn the product into a sum.
Real world scenario: a doctor with a checklist

A doctor knows flu is common in winter (the prior). Fever makes flu more likely, a cough makes it more likely again, a rash makes it less likely. The doctor combines each clue one by one, as if each told its own story. That is naive Bayes: often a little overconfident, but quick and usually pointing the right way.

Text classification with multinomial naive Bayes

What it is
MultinomialNB works on word counts. P(word | class) is the share of that class’s words that are this word. With CountVectorizer in front, it becomes a complete text classifier in two lines.
Why we need it
Email filtering, support ticket routing, sentiment and language detection all start from word counts. Naive Bayes trains in seconds on millions of documents and needs no feature scaling.
How it works
CountVectorizer turns each text into word counts. MultinomialNB learns P(word | class) with smoothing α and the class priors. predict_proba then gives the class probabilities for new texts.

Lesson 3 of 11

Smoothing and log probabilities

What it is
Laplace smoothing adds a small count α (usually 1) to every word in every class. Log probabilities add logs instead of multiplying tiny numbers.
Why we need it
Without smoothing, a word that never appeared in ham during training gets P(word | ham) = 0, and one such word makes the whole ham score 0, no matter how hammy the rest of the message is. Without logs, multiplying hundreds of small probabilities underflows to exactly 0.0 on a computer, as the program shows.
How it works
Smoothed P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + α) / (total words in c + αV), where V is the vocabulary size. Compare classes by log P(c) + Σ log P(w | c). scikit-learn does both for you.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 log_probabilities.py.
Laplace (add alpha) smoothingExam favourite
P(w | c) = (nw,c + α) / (nc + αV)
Every word gets a small chance, so no probability is ever 0.
Lesson 4 of 11

Gaussian naive Bayes for numbers

What it is
GaussianNB handles numeric features by fitting a normal (bell) curve per feature and per class. P(xj | c) is the height of that curve at the feature value.
Why we need it
Measurements such as length, weight or blood pressure are numbers, not counts. Gaussian naive Bayes gives a fast probabilistic classifier for them with just a mean and a variance per feature and class.
How it works
For each class and feature, compute the mean μ and variance σ2 from the training data. For a new example, multiply the prior by the normal density of every feature value, and normalise.

Gaussian likelihoodExam favourite
p(xj | c) = (1 / √(2πσjc2)) exp(−(xj − μjc)2 / (2σjc2))
One mean and one variance per feature and class.

Lesson 6 of 11

Strengths and weaknesses

What it is
Naive Bayes is fast and data efficient, but its probabilities are usually overconfident, and it cannot learn interactions between features.
Why we need it
Correlated features are counted as if they were separate evidence. "free", "FREE" and "free!!!" all say the same thing, but naive Bayes multiplies them in three times, pushing probabilities towards 0 or 1.
How it works
Use naive Bayes for its ranking and speed, not for exact probabilities. If you need trustworthy probabilities, calibrate it (Supervised 4.3). With enough data, a discriminative model like logistic regression usually wins on accuracy.

Remember

Ng and Jordan (2002) showed that naive Bayes reaches its best error with fewer examples, while logistic regression reaches a lower best error with more examples, so naive Bayes shines when data is tiny and its assumptions roughly hold, as with text.

The maths, all in one place

Bayes ruleExam favourite
P(c | x) = P(x | c) P(c) / P(x)
P(x) is the same for every class, so it can be ignored when comparing.
Naive assumptionExam favourite
P(x1, ..., xd | c) = Πj P(xj | c)
Features independent given the class.
Multinomial likelihoodExam favourite
P(w | c) = (nw,c + α) / (nc + αV)
Smoothed word share.
Bernoulli likelihood
P(x | c) = Πj pjcxj (1 − pjc)1 − xj
Absent features count through (1 − p).
Log scoreExam favourite
score(c) = log P(c) + Σ log P(xj | c)
Pick the largest. No underflow.
Worked example: the spam filter by hand
  1. Prior: 4 spam and 4 ham, so P(spam) = P(ham) = 0.5. Vocabulary V = 18, each class has 14 words.
  2. P(free | spam) = (2 + 1) / (14 + 18) = 0.0938. P(free | ham) = (0 + 1) / 32 = 0.0312.
  3. P(money | spam) = 3/32 = 0.0938, P(money | ham) = 2/32 = 0.0625. Same numbers for "now".
  4. spam: 0.5 × 0.09383 = 4.12 × 10−4, ham: 0.5 × 0.0312 × 0.06252 = 6.1 × 10−5
  5. P(spam | message) = 4.12 / (4.12 + 0.61) = 0.871, as the program prints.
Going deeper: Naive Bayes is a linear classifier

For multinomial or Bernoulli naive Bayes with two classes, the log odds log P(spam | x) − log P(ham | x) = log(P(spam)/P(ham)) + Σj xj log(P(wj | spam)/P(wj | ham)), which is linear in the counts xj. So naive Bayes and logistic regression draw the same kind of boundary. Naive Bayes sets the weights by counting (generative), logistic regression by maximising the conditional likelihood (discriminative). Gaussian naive Bayes with a shared variance per feature is also linear, and with separate variances its boundary is quadratic.

From beginner to expert

One idea, four depths

Naive Bayes counts how often each clue appears with each answer during training, then multiplies the clues for a new example to see which answer is most likely.

Build text classifiers with CountVectorizer and MultinomialNB, use GaussianNB for numbers and BernoulliNB for binary features, and explain smoothing and log probabilities.

Explain why its probabilities are overconfident, show that it is a linear classifier for text, and compare it with logistic regression using the generative vs discriminative view.

Study the Ng and Jordan learning rate results, Bayesian networks that relax the independence assumption, semi supervised naive Bayes with EM (Nigam and others, 2000), and why naive Bayes classifies well even when its independence assumption is badly wrong (Domingos and Pazzani, 1997).

Intermediate

Expert
Analyse its bias, calibration and linear form against discriminative models.

Chapter summary

  1. Naive Bayes: P(c | x) ∝ P(c) Π P(xj | c), assuming features are independent given the class.
  2. Training is counting: class priors and per class feature likelihoods. Prediction picks the largest log score.
  3. Laplace smoothing (add α) stops a single unseen word from making a probability 0.
  4. Add log probabilities instead of multiplying, to avoid underflow.
  5. MultinomialNB for word counts, BernoulliNB for 0 or 1 features, GaussianNB for numbers.
  6. Fast, good with little data and many features, but overconfident and blind to feature interactions.
  7. For text it is a linear classifier, the generative cousin of logistic regression.
