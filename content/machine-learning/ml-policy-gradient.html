Learning the policy directly: REINFORCE

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 13.1 to 13.3 and example 13.1, and Williams, Simple statistical gradient-following algorithms (1992).

What it is
A policy gradient method keeps a parameterised policy π(a|s, θ), for example a softmax over action preferences, and adjusts θ by gradient ascent on the expected return J(θ). It needs no value table to choose actions. REINFORCE is the simplest such method: after each episode it raises the log probability of every action taken in proportion to the return that followed.
Why we need it
Policies can be simpler than value functions, they handle continuous actions naturally, and they can learn stochastic policies. In the short corridor the agent cannot tell its states apart, and the best policy presses right with probability 0.59. A greedy value based method can only press always right or always left, and both never finish.
How it works
Run an episode with the current policy. For each step t compute the return Gt and update θ += αGt∇ln π(At|St, θ). For a softmax policy the gradient of ln π(a) with respect to the preferences is one hot(a) minus the probabilities.

The policy gradient theorem and baselines

Source: Sutton, McAllester, Singh and Mansour (NeurIPS 1999), and Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 13.2 and 13.4.

What it is
The policy gradient theorem gives the gradient of J without differentiating the state distribution: it is the expected value of qπ(s, a)∇ln π(a|s). Subtracting any baseline b(s) that does not depend on the action leaves this expectation unchanged but can make each sample far less noisy.
Why we need it
Changing the policy changes which states are visited, and that effect is hard to compute. The theorem shows we can ignore it, which is what makes sampling the gradient possible. Raw returns are very noisy, so a good baseline, usually the state value v(s), is the cheapest way to speed up learning.
How it works
Use Gt − b(St) in place of Gt. A learned v̂(s, w) is the usual choice, trained like gradient Monte Carlo. The quantity Gt − v(St) estimates the advantage: how much better this action was than the policy’s average.

In the exam
For the fixed policy p = 0.3 the true gradient is 8.48. Over 20,000 episodes the plain REINFORCE estimate averages 8.53 with a standard deviation of 39.65, and with the baseline b(s) = v(s) it averages 8.59 with a standard deviation of 25.33. Same direction on average, about 36% less noise per episode.
Why a baseline adds no bias
  1. The extra term is Ea ~ π[b(s)∇ln π(a|s)] = b(s) Σa π(a|s)∇ln π(a|s).
  2. π ∇ln π = ∇π, so the sum is b(s) ∇ Σa π(a|s)
  3. Σa π(a|s) = 1 for every θ, so its gradient is 0
  4. The baseline term averages to exactly 0. It only changes the noise, never the expected direction.

Lesson 3 of 10

Actor critic methods

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) section 13.5, Konda and Tsitsiklis (NeurIPS 1999), Mnih and colleagues, Asynchronous methods for deep RL (ICML 2016), and Schulman and colleagues, Generalized advantage estimation (ICLR 2016).

What it is
An actor critic method learns two things: the actor is the policy, and the critic is a value function. After each step the critic’s TD error δ = r + γv̂(s′) − v̂(s) tells the actor whether the action turned out better or worse than expected, and the actor moves in that direction.
Why we need it
REINFORCE must wait for the end of an episode and suffers from noisy returns. Bootstrapping from the critic lets the actor learn after every step, with much lower variance, at the cost of some bias from an imperfect critic. A2C, A3C, PPO, DDPG and SAC are all actor critic methods.
How it works
Each step: compute δ, update the critic w += αwδ∇v̂(s), and update the actor θ += αθδ∇ln π(a|s). In deep RL both are networks, often sharing layers. Generalised advantage estimation (GAE) mixes n step advantages with a parameter λ to trade bias against variance.

On the cliff walk from chapter 2.3, a tabular actor critic improves from an average reward of −47.1 in its first 100 episodes to about −19 in later blocks. PPO: small, safe policy updates

Source: Schulman and colleagues, Trust Region Policy Optimization (ICML 2015) and Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (2017), and Ouyang and colleagues, Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (2022).

What it is
Proximal policy optimisation (PPO) collects a batch with the current policy, then takes several epochs of gradient steps on that batch. To stop the new policy drifting too far from the one that collected the data, it clips the probability ratio r = πnew(a|s) / πold(a|s) to [1 − ε, 1 + ε] inside a pessimistic minimum.
Why we need it
Plain policy gradient uses each sample once and can collapse after one large step. TRPO solved this with a KL constraint but needs second order optimisation. PPO gets most of the benefit with a few lines of code. It became the default on policy algorithm and was used for OpenAI Five and for the RLHF stage of InstructGPT.
How it works
Compute advantages with GAE and normalise them. For K epochs, maximise the average of min(r A, clip(r, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) A), with ε = 0.2 in the paper, plus a value loss and often an entropy bonus. Samples on the flat part of the minimum give no gradient.

In the exam
With the clip, average episode length grows from 23.4 to 172.8 in 15 iterations of about 1000 steps, reusing each batch 10 times. The ratios still leave [0.8, 1.2] a little, because the clip only silences samples already outside the range while the others keep pushing. Without the clip this easy, linear problem also learns, but the ratios swing from 0.11 to 1.92 in the first iteration: the new policy moves far from the data, which with neural networks often ends in collapse.

Lesson 5 of 10

Continuous actions: Gaussian policies, DDPG, TD3 and SAC

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) section 13.7, Silver and colleagues, Deterministic policy gradient algorithms (ICML 2014), Lillicrap and colleagues (ICLR 2016), Fujimoto, van Hoof and Meger (ICML 2018), and Haarnoja and colleagues (ICML 2018).

What it is
For a continuous action such as a motor torque, a policy can output the mean μ(s) and standard deviation σ(s) of a Gaussian, and the agent samples from it. Off policy actor critic methods for continuous control learn a Q function as the critic: DDPG uses a deterministic actor, TD3 fixes its overestimation, and SAC adds an entropy bonus to keep exploring.
Why we need it
Robots, cars and many control problems have continuous actions, where taking a max over actions, as Q learning does, is itself an optimisation problem. These methods reuse replay data, so they need far fewer environment steps than on policy methods such as PPO.
How it works
Gaussian policy: ∇μ ln π(a|s) = (a − μ) / σ2. DDPG: the actor follows ∇aQ(s, a) through the action. TD3: two critics and take the smaller, delay actor updates, and add noise to target actions. SAC: maximise reward plus α times the policy’s entropy.

Worked example: one REINFORCE step for two actions
  1. Preference h = 0, so p(right) = 0.5. The agent pressed right (a = 1) and the return from that step was G = −4. Step size α = 0.1.
  2. ∇h ln π(right) = a − p = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5
  3. h += 0.1 × (−4) × 0.5 = −0.2, so p(right) falls to 0.450
  4. With a baseline b = −6, the same step uses G − b = 2, so h += 0.1 × 2 × 0.5 = +0.1 and p(right) rises to 0.525.
  5. Without a baseline every return here is negative, so every action taken is pushed down, and only the size of the push separates good from bad. The baseline makes better than average actions go up and worse ones go down.
Going deeper: Natural gradients and why trust regions work

Ordinary gradient ascent measures a step by how far θ moves, but two policies with nearby parameters can behave very differently, and two with distant parameters can behave alike. The natural policy gradient (Kakade 2001) measures the step by how much the policy itself changes, using the KL divergence. To second order, KL(πθ || πθ+Δ) ≈ ½ΔTFΔ, where F is the Fisher information matrix, so the natural gradient is F−1∇J. TRPO takes the largest natural gradient step whose average KL stays below δ, using conjugate gradients to avoid forming F, and it comes with a bound that guarantees improvement of a lower bound on J. PPO drops the second order machinery and keeps only the idea of staying close, through the clip.

Chapter summary

  1. Policy gradient methods learn π(a|s, θ) directly by gradient ascent on the expected return, and can learn stochastic and continuous policies.
  2. REINFORCE: θ += αGt∇ln π(At|St). In the short corridor it finds the best p(right) of 0.59.
  3. The policy gradient theorem removes the need to differentiate the state distribution. A baseline leaves the gradient unbiased and cuts its noise.
  4. Actor critic: the critic’s TD error replaces the return. GAE blends n step advantages with λ.
  5. PPO reuses each batch for several epochs and clips the probability ratio to keep updates small. TRPO uses a KL trust region.
  6. Continuous control: Gaussian policies, DDPG, TD3 and SAC.
