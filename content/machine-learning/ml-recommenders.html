Lesson 1 of 10

What a recommender does, and content based filtering

Source: Leskovec, Rajaraman and Ullman, Mining of Massive Datasets chapter 9, and Ricci, Rokach and Shapira, Recommender Systems Handbook chapter 1.

What it is
A recommender system guesses which items (films, songs, products, posts) a person will like and shows the best few first. The data is a large user by item matrix of ratings, clicks or purchases, and almost all of it is empty, because each person has met only a tiny share of the items. Content based filtering describes each item by its features (genre, words, price) and recommends items whose features match what the person liked before.
Why we need it
Shops, streaming services and news sites have far more items than anyone can browse. A good ranked list saves people time and drives most of what they watch or buy on large platforms. Content based filtering needs no other users, so it works for a brand new item as soon as its features are known.
How it works
Turn each item into a feature vector. Build a taste profile for the person as the average of the vectors of items they liked. Score every unseen item by the cosine similarity between its vector and the profile, and recommend the highest scores.

Lesson 2 of 10

Collaborative filtering: people like you and items like this

Source: Resnick, Iacovou, Suchak, Bergstrom and Riedl, GroupLens (CSCW 1994), and Sarwar, Karypis, Konstan and Riedl, Item-based Collaborative Filtering Recommendation Algorithms (WWW 2001).

What it is
Collaborative filtering uses only the ratings, not item features. User based filtering finds users whose past ratings agree with yours and predicts your rating of a film from theirs. Item based filtering finds items rated in the same pattern as items you rated, and predicts from your own ratings of those similar items.
Why we need it
It finds links that features miss: people who like one quiet foreign drama often like a certain documentary, even though the two share no genre. It needs no hand made features at all, only a record of who liked what.
How it works
Centre each user’s ratings on that user’s mean, because some people rate everything high. Measure similarity with the cosine of centred ratings on items both users rated (this is the Pearson correlation). Keep the k most similar users who rated the target item, and predict the user’s mean plus a similarity weighted average of their centred ratings.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 user_cf.py.
In the exam
User 1 agrees with user 0 (similarity 0.776) and rated the film 1, well below user 1’s mean of 2.75. User 2 disagrees with user 0 (similarity −0.653) and loved the film. Both signals point the same way, so the guess for user 0 is 1.81, far below user 0’s mean of 3.33. A negative similarity flips the neighbour’s opinion. Many systems simply keep only positive similarities.

Matrix factorisation: hidden tastes learned from ratings

Source: Koren, Bell and Volinsky, Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems (IEEE Computer 2009), the Netflix Prize write up.

What it is
Matrix factorisation gives every user u a short vector pu and every item i a short vector qi of k hidden numbers, called latent factors. The predicted rating is r̂ = μ + bu + bi + pu · qi: the overall mean, plus how generous the user is, plus how well liked the item is, plus how well the user’s tastes match the item’s traits. The factors are learned only from the known ratings.
Why we need it
It was the core of the winning methods in the Netflix Prize (2006 to 2009). It handles sparse data better than neighbour methods, because every rating helps shape factors that all users and items share. The learned factors often line up with real traits such as serious against light hearted, without anyone naming them.
How it works
Minimise the squared error on known ratings plus a penalty λ on the size of all factors and biases. Train with stochastic gradient descent (SGD), one rating at a time, or with alternating least squares (ALS), which fixes the item factors and solves every user exactly, then swaps. Choose k and λ on held out ratings.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 mf_sgd.py.
In the exam
Guessing the average gives a test RMSE of 1.068. For the first 10 epochs SGD is no better, because the small random factors need time to grow. By epoch 20 test RMSE falls to 0.689 and by epoch 60 to 0.36, while train RMSE is 0.2. Run the same program with 30 users, 20 films, only 30% of ratings seen and λ = 0.02, and it overfits: train RMSE 0.194 but test RMSE 1.145, worse than the average guess. With sparse data the penalty is not optional.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 als.py.
Remember
ALS reaches a test RMSE of 0.319 after only 3 sweeps and 0.259 after 10, against 1.068 for the average guess. Each sweep solves small k by k linear systems exactly, so it needs far fewer passes than SGD. ALS also parallelises well, since every user can be solved at the same time, which is why Spark MLlib uses it.
Going deeper: Implicit feedback and weighted ALS

With clicks or plays there are no low ratings, only signs of interest. Hu, Koren and Volinsky (2008) turn every cell into a preference pui (1 if the user interacted, else 0) and a confidence cui = 1 + α rui, where rui counts the interactions. They then minimise Σ cui(pui − pu · qi)2 over all cells, including the empty ones, plus the usual penalty. ALS stays fast because the sum over empty cells can be rewritten with one shared k by k matrix. The Python package implicit offers this method, and so does Spark MLlib with implicitPrefs set to true.

Measuring recommendations and starting cold

Source: Jarvelin and Kekalainen, Cumulated Gain-based Evaluation of IR Techniques (ACM TOIS 2002), and Schein, Popescul, Ungar and Pennock, Methods and Metrics for Cold-Start Recommendations (SIGIR 2002).

What it is
Recommenders are judged in two ways. Rating error (RMSE or MAE) measures how close predicted ratings are to real ones. Ranking metrics measure the short list a person actually sees: precision@k (share of the top k that are relevant), recall@k (share of the relevant items that reach the top k), NDCG@k (rewards hits near the top more) and MRR (how early the first hit comes).
Why we need it
People look at the first few items only. A model can have a low RMSE and still put the wrong films first. The cold start problem, a new user or new item with no history, is where collaborative methods fail completely, so every real system needs a fallback.
How it works
Hold out some later interactions of each user, recommend from the earlier ones, and score the list against what the user really chose next. Split by time rather than at random, to avoid using the future. For cold start, show popular items with a shrunk score, ask new users a few questions, and use content features for new items.

Lesson 5 of 10

Recommenders at scale and their side effects

Source: Covington, Adams and Sargin, Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations (RecSys 2016), Yi and colleagues, Sampling-Bias-Corrected Neural Modeling for Large Corpus Item Recommendations (RecSys 2019), and Chaney, Stewart and Engelhardt, How Algorithmic Confounding in Recommendation Systems Increases Homogeneity (RecSys 2018).

What it is
Large systems work in two stages. Candidate generation quickly picks a few hundred items out of millions, often with a two tower neural network that maps users and items to vectors and finds the nearest item vectors with approximate nearest neighbour search. Ranking then scores those candidates with a richer model that uses many features, such as time of day and recent clicks, and predicts watch time or the chance of a click.
Why we need it
Scoring millions of items with a big model for every page view is too slow. Splitting the work keeps answers fast. Recommenders also change what people see, so they change the data they learn from next, which can narrow choices and favour already popular items.
How it works
Train the towers on past interactions, index the item vectors with a library such as FAISS, rank with gradient boosting or a deep network, then re rank for variety, freshness and business rules. Test changes with online A/B tests, since offline metrics on logged data are biased by what the old system chose to show.
A typical serving path
  1. A user opens the app. Look up or compute the user vector from recent activity.
  2. Candidate generation: approximate nearest neighbour search returns about 500 items from millions in a few milliseconds.
  3. Ranking: a model with many features scores the 500 candidates.
  4. Re ranking: remove items already seen, add variety, apply rules.
  5. Log what was shown and what was clicked. This log becomes the next training data.
Remember
Popularity bias and feedback loops: items that are shown get clicks, clicks make them shown more, and items that were never shown never get a chance. Exploration (sometimes showing less certain items) and corrections such as inverse propensity weighting reduce this. Measure variety and coverage, not only clicks.

Worked example: one SGD update
  1. Known rating r = 4. Current guess r̂ = 3, so the error e = 1.
  2. Take η = 0.1, λ = 0.1, pu = (1, 0) and qi = (0.5, 0.5).
  3. pu ← (1, 0) + 0.1 × (1 × (0.5, 0.5) − 0.1 × (1, 0)) = (1.04, 0.05)
  4. qi ← (0.5, 0.5) + 0.1 × (1 × (1, 0) − 0.1 × (0.5, 0.5)) = (0.595, 0.495)
  5. The new dot product is 1.04 × 0.595 + 0.05 × 0.495 = 0.644, up from 0.5, so the guess moved towards 4.
Going deeper: Why it is called factorisation, and its link to SVD

Stack the user vectors as rows of P (size users by k) and the item vectors as rows of Q (items by k). Then all predictions together form P QT, a rank k matrix, so the method approximates the rating matrix by a product of two thin matrices. If every cell were known, the best rank k approximation in squared error would be the truncated SVD (the Eckart and Young theorem, as in the PCA chapter). With most cells missing, SVD cannot be applied directly, and filling the gaps with zeros or means biases it. That is why the loss sums only over known cells and why it is solved with SGD or ALS. The loss is not convex in P and Q together, but it is convex in either one when the other is fixed, which is exactly the property ALS uses.

Chapter summary

  1. A recommender ranks items for each person from a huge, mostly empty user by item matrix.
  2. Content based filtering matches item features to a taste profile. It handles new items but only finds more of the same.
  3. Collaborative filtering uses ratings only: centred cosine similarity between users or items, then a weighted average.
  4. Matrix factorisation predicts μ + bu + bi + pu · qi, trained on known cells with a penalty by SGD or ALS.
  5. Judge lists with precision@k, recall@k, NDCG and MRR on a time based split. RMSE alone misses the order.
  6. Cold start needs fallbacks: shrunk popularity, a few questions, content features. At scale use two stages and watch feedback loops.
