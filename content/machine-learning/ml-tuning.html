Lesson 1 of 11

Parameters and hyperparameters

Source: Bergstra and Bengio (2012), Li and others, Hyperband (2018), Cawley and Talbot (2010), Platt (1999), Zadrozny and Elkan (2002), Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005), Guo and others (2017), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide sections 3.2, 3.3 and 1.16.

What it is
Parameters are learned from the data during fit, such as the weights of a linear model or the splits of a tree. Hyperparameters are chosen by you before training, such as C and gamma for an SVM, max_depth for a tree, or the learning rate for boosting.
Why we need it
The same algorithm can underfit or overfit badly depending on its hyperparameters. Tuning them with validation, not by guessing or by looking at the test set, is often worth more accuracy than switching algorithms.
How it works
Sweep one hyperparameter and plot the training and validation scores (a validation curve) to see where underfitting turns into overfitting. Then search several at once with GridSearchCV or RandomizedSearchCV, always inside cross validation.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 validation_curve.py.
Remember
Training accuracy climbs to 1.0 as the tree deepens, but validation accuracy peaks at depth 2 (0.928) and then slips. To the left of the peak the tree underfits, to the right it overfits. The validation curve shows exactly where to stop.
Real world scenario: tuning a guitar

The strings of a guitar are its parameters: they vibrate on their own when played. The tuning pegs are the hyperparameters: you set them before you play, by ear, and a small turn changes everything that follows. You tune by listening to how it sounds, not by reading the notes off the sheet music you will perform.

Grid search and random search

What it is
Grid search tries every combination of listed values. Random search draws a fixed number of combinations at random from ranges or distributions you give.
Why we need it
A grid grows exponentially: 5 values for each of 4 hyperparameters is 625 settings, times the number of folds. Usually only one or two hyperparameters really matter, and random search tests many more distinct values of each for the same budget (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012).
How it works
GridSearchCV(pipe, param_grid, cv=5) and RandomizedSearchCV(pipe, distributions, n_iter=50, cv=5). Name pipeline steps with a double underscore, such as svc__C. Search scale parameters like C, gamma, alpha and learning rate on a log scale with scipy.stats.loguniform.

Smarter searches: halving and Bayesian optimisation

What it is
Successive halving starts many candidates on a small budget (few rows or few trees), keeps the best third, gives them more budget, and repeats. Bayesian optimisation fits a model of the score as a function of the hyperparameters and picks the next setting where it expects the most improvement.
Why we need it
Most candidates are obviously bad after a little training, so spending the full budget on each is wasteful. These methods reach the same quality with far fewer full fits, which matters for big data and deep learning.
How it works
scikit-learn offers HalvingGridSearchCV and HalvingRandomSearchCV (import sklearn.experimental.enable_halving_search_cv first). Optuna and scikit-optimize provide Bayesian optimisation with tree structured Parzen estimators or Gaussian processes, plus pruning of bad trials.

Grid searchEvery combination1 or 2 hyperparameters, few values

Nested cross validation

What it is
Nested cross validation wraps the whole tuning procedure inside an outer cross validation. The inner loop chooses the hyperparameters, and the outer loop scores that choice on folds it never saw.
Why we need it
The best inner score is optimistic: you picked the maximum of many noisy scores, so part of its height is luck. If you report it as your expected performance, you will be disappointed later. Nested cross validation measures the whole recipe "tune, then train" honestly.
How it works
cross_val_score(GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=inner), X, y, cv=outer). It costs outer folds × inner folds × settings fits. Use it to report performance, then run the plain search once on all data to get the final model.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 nested_cv.py.
In the exam
On 150 rows with 30 settings, the best inner score is 0.953 but the nested estimate is 0.933. Two points of the inner score were the luck of picking the best of 30. With a locked test set you would see the same drop.
Lesson 5 of 11

Calibrating probabilities

What it is
A classifier is calibrated when its probabilities mean what they say: among all cases given 0.7, about 70% are really positive. A reliability diagram plots predicted probability against observed frequency, and CalibratedClassifierCV fixes a miscalibrated model.
Why we need it
Many models rank well but give distorted probabilities. Naive Bayes and boosted trees push them towards 0 and 1, random forests and SVM scores pull them towards the middle. Risk scores, expected costs, pricing and thresholds chosen from costs all need honest probabilities.
How it works
Fit a calibrator on held out predictions: Platt scaling (method="sigmoid") fits a logistic curve to the scores and suits small data. Isotonic regression (method="isotonic") fits any increasing step function and needs more data. calibration_curve gives the points of the reliability diagram.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 calibration.py.
Remember
Naive Bayes says 0.97 for its most confident bin, yet only 83% of those are positive, and says 0.03 where 24% are positive. After isotonic calibration the predicted and observed values match within about 0.04 and the Brier score falls from 0.203 to 0.168. This is the fix promised in the naive Bayes chapter.
Platt scalingExam favourite
p′ = 1 / (1 + exp(−(A s + B)))
s is the model score, A and B fitted on held out data.
Expected calibration errorExam favourite
ECE = Σb (nb/n) |mean predictedb − observedb|
Weighted gap between the curve and the diagonal.

Imbalanced classes: weights and thresholds

What it is
With a rare positive class, a default model often predicts the majority class too often. Two simple fixes: class weights make mistakes on the rare class cost more during training, and a tuned threshold moves the decision point after training.
Why we need it
They target the metric you care about (recall, F1, cost) instead of accuracy. Both are simpler and usually as effective as generating synthetic examples (SMOTE), which can add noise and must be done inside the folds.
How it works
class_weight="balanced" in most scikit-learn classifiers weights each class by n / (classes × nclass). TunedThresholdClassifierCV(model, scoring="f1") picks the threshold by cross validation. Evaluate with recall, precision and PR AUC, never accuracy alone.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 class_weight.py.
In the exam
With 5% positives the default logistic regression finds only half of them (recall 0.5). Balanced weights find 92%, but precision drops to 0.28: more catches, many more false alarms. Whether that trade is right depends on the costs, which is why the threshold should be tuned for the metric you care about.

Remember
Tuning the threshold for F1 moves it from 0.5 to 0.25 and lifts F1 a little, from 0.625 to 0.632. With a different metric, such as a cost per missed case, the best threshold would be different again.

The maths, all in one place

Grid sizeExam favourite
|grid| = Πj (number of values for hyperparameter j)
Grows exponentially.
Random search coverageExam favourite
P(at least one try in the top q share) = 1 − (1 − q)n
60 tries: 95% chance of the top 5%.
Balanced class weightExam favourite
wc = n / (K × nc)
K classes, nc rows in class c.
Platt scalingExam favourite
p′ = sigmoid(A s + B)
Two numbers fitted by log loss.
Nested CV cost
kouter × kinner × |grid| fits
Plus refits.
Worked example: why 60 random tries are enough
  1. Suppose any setting in the best 5% of the space is good enough.
  2. One random try misses that region with probability 0.95.
  3. Sixty independent tries all miss with probability 0.9560.
  4. 0.9560 = 0.046, so P(hit) = 1 − 0.046 = 0.954
  5. This holds however many hyperparameters there are, which is why random search scales where grids do not (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012).
Going deeper: Calibration in theory and in deep networks

A proper scoring rule such as log loss is minimised in expectation by the true probabilities, so a model trained by log loss with enough data and capacity tends to be calibrated. Margin based methods (SVMs, boosting with exponential loss) are not trained that way, which is why Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) found boosted trees and SVMs need calibration while logistic regression and bagged trees often do not. Guo and others (2017) showed that modern deep networks are overconfident, and that temperature scaling, dividing all logits by one learned number T, fixes most of it. Calibration is also only on average: a model can be calibrated overall and badly miscalibrated for a subgroup, which matters for fairness.

Chapter summary

  1. Hyperparameters are chosen before training. A validation curve shows where underfitting turns into overfitting.
  2. GridSearchCV tries every combination, RandomizedSearchCV samples them. Random search covers important settings better.
  3. Search scale parameters on a log scale. Successive halving and Bayesian optimisation save time on expensive models.
  4. The best inner CV score is optimistic. Nested cross validation or a locked test set gives the honest number.
  5. Calibration makes probabilities honest: Platt (sigmoid) for small data, isotonic for large. Check with a reliability diagram.
  6. For rare classes use class_weight and a tuned threshold, judged by the metric you care about.
