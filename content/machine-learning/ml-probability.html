Lesson 1 of 13

Probability: counting how likely things are

Source: Blitzstein and Hwang, Introduction to Probability chapters 1 to 5 and 10, Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning chapter 6, Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning chapters 1.2 and 1.6, and Cover and Thomas, Elements of Information Theory chapter 2.

What it is
Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means it never happens, 1 means it always happens, and 0.5 means half the time. A random variable is a number whose value depends on chance, like the result of a dice roll.
Why we need it
Real data is noisy and the future is uncertain. A spam filter cannot be sure an email is spam, but it can say "93% likely". Probability gives models a clean way to talk about what they do not know, and most loss functions come straight from it.
How it works
Count the outcomes you care about and divide by all equally likely outcomes. For harder cases, combine simple probabilities with a few rules (the sum rule, the product rule and conditional probability), or simulate many times and count, as the program below does.

RuleFormulaExample with one die
ComplementP(not A) = 1 − P(A)P(not 6) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6
Sum rule (or)P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)P(even or at least 5) = 3/6 + 2/6 − 1/6 = 4/6
Product rule (and)P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A)Two sixes in a row = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36
Independent eventsP(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)Dice rolls do not affect each other
ConditionalP(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)P(6 | even) = (1/6) / (3/6) = 1/3
Remember
P(A | B) is read "the probability of A given B". It means: only look at the cases where B happened, and ask how often A happened among them.
Real world scenario: a weather forecast

When the forecast says "70% chance of rain", it means that on many days with weather like today, it rained on about 7 out of 10 of them. A classifier that says "70% spam" makes the same kind of promise. If it is well calibrated, about 7 in 10 of the emails it scores at 0.7 really are spam.

Bayes rule: updating a belief with new evidence

What it is
Bayes rule turns P(evidence | cause) into P(cause | evidence). It says how much to change a belief (the prior) after you see new evidence, giving the updated belief (the posterior).
Why we need it
We usually know how a cause produces evidence (how often a sick person tests positive), but we want the reverse (how likely a person with a positive test is sick). Our intuition gets this badly wrong when the cause is rare. Bayes rule gets it right, and it is the base of naive Bayes classifiers and Bayesian machine learning.
How it works
Multiply the prior by the likelihood of the evidence, then divide by the total chance of the evidence so the answers add up to 1. The counting picture above shows the same thing with people: positives that are really sick, divided by all positives.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 bayes_test.py.
Bayes ruleExam favourite
P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B)
Posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence.
Total probabilityExam favourite
P(B) = P(B | A) P(A) + P(B | not A) P(not A)
Adds up every way B can happen.
Worked example: the medical test by hand
  1. 1% are sick, the test finds 95% of sick people, and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people.
  2. P(+) = 0.95 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0095 + 0.0495 = 0.059.
  3. P(sick | +) = 0.0095 / 0.059 = 0.161
  4. So only about 16% of the people who test positive are really sick, even with a "95% accurate" test. The disease is so rare that the false alarms outnumber the real cases.
In the exam
Ignoring the prior is called the base rate fallacy. It is a common exam trap and a real problem in fraud and disease detection, where the event you look for is rare.

The distributions you will meet everywhere

What it is
A probability distribution lists every value a random variable can take and how likely each one is. A few shapes appear again and again in real data, so they have names.
Why we need it
If you know which shape your data has, you can pick the right model and loss. Counts of rare events follow a Poisson shape, yes or no answers follow a Bernoulli shape, and measurements like height follow a normal (bell) shape.
How it works
Each named distribution has a formula with one or two parameters. Once you set the parameters (for example the mean and spread of a normal), you can compute the chance of any value, or draw random samples, with scipy.stats and numpy.

For a PDF, a single exact value has probability 0, and you get probabilities from areas under the curve, which is what the CDF (cumulative distribution function) adds up.
Lesson 4 of 13

Mean, variance and correlation

What it is
The mean (average) is the centre of the data, the median is the middle value, the variance and standard deviation measure the spread, and covariance and correlation measure how two features move together.
Why we need it
Before you train any model you describe the data. The mean and spread tell you whether to scale features. Correlation tells you which features carry similar information and which are related to the target.
How it works
The mean adds the values and divides by the count. The variance averages the squared distances from the mean, and the standard deviation is its square root, so it has the same unit as the data. Correlation divides the covariance by both standard deviations, so it always lands between −1 and 1.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 mean_var_corr.py.
Mean (expected value)Exam favourite
E[X] = Σ x P(x), sample mean x̄ = (1/n) Σ xi
The centre.
VarianceExam favourite
Var(X) = E[(X − μ)2] = E[X2] − (E[X])2
Average squared distance from the mean.
CorrelationExam favourite
r = Cov(X, Y) / (σX σY)
Between −1 and 1. 0 means no straight line relation.
Linearity of expectation
E[aX + bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y]
Always true, even when X and Y depend on each other.
Variance of a sum
Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2 Cov(X, Y)
The covariance term vanishes when X and Y are independent.
Common mistake
The sample standard deviation divides by n − 1 instead of n (ddof=1 in numpy, the default in pandas). This small change corrects for the fact that the sample mean is closer to the data than the true mean is. The program shows 12.9 with n and 13.79 with n − 1.
In the exam
Correlation is not causation. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, but one does not cause the other. Correlation also only measures straight line relations: y = x2 on symmetric x has correlation 0.
Lesson 5 of 13

The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem

What it is
The law of large numbers says that the average of many samples gets close to the true mean. The central limit theorem (CLT) says more: the average of n samples follows a bell shaped normal distribution, whatever shape the original data had, and its spread shrinks like σ / √n.
Why we need it
It explains why we can trust a test score measured on a few thousand examples, how wide the error bars on that score should be, and why the normal distribution appears in so many statistical tests.
How it works
Each average mixes many independent values, so the highs and lows cancel. The cancelling makes the averages pile up near the mean in a bell shape, and more values per average means more cancelling, so a narrower bell.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 clt_demo.py.
Standard error of the meanExam favourite
SE = σ / √n
To halve the error bar, you need four times as much data.
Remember
A model scores 90% accuracy on 400 test examples. The standard error is √(0.9 × 0.1 / 400) = 0.015, so the real accuracy is likely within about 90% ± 3%. Two models that differ by 1% on this test set may not really differ at all.

Maximum likelihood: where loss functions come from

What it is
Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) picks the parameters that make the data you saw most likely. The likelihood is the probability of the data, seen as a function of the parameters.
Why we need it
It is the reason behind the losses you will use. Minimising mean squared error is MLE when the noise is normal. Minimising log loss (cross entropy) is MLE for a yes or no target. So a loss is not a random choice, it comes from an assumption about the data.
How it works
Write the probability of every data point using the model, multiply them (they are independent), take the log so the product becomes a sum, and find the parameters that make it largest. Minimising the negative log likelihood is the same thing, which is how libraries do it.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 mle_coin.py.
LikelihoodExam favourite
L(θ) = Πi P(xi | θ)
Product over independent data points.
Log likelihoodExam favourite
log L(θ) = Σi log P(xi | θ)
Sums are easier than products and do not underflow.
Coin MLEExam favourite
p̂ = heads / n
Set the derivative of h log p + t log(1 − p) to 0.
Normal noise gives MSE
−log L = (1 / (2σ2)) Σ (yi − ŷi)2 + constant
Maximising likelihood is minimising squared error.
Bernoulli gives log loss
−log L = −Σ [y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p)]
The loss of logistic regression.
Worked example: the coin MLE with calculus
  1. Data: h = 7 heads and t = 3 tails. log L(p) = 7 log p + 3 log(1 − p).
  2. Derivative: 7/p − 3/(1 − p). Set it to 0.
  3. 7(1 − p) = 3p, so 7 = 10p.
  4. p̂ = 0.7
  5. This matches the grid search in the program and the simple rule heads / n.
Going deeper: MAP: adding a prior turns into regularisation

Maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation maximises P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ) P(θ). Taking logs adds log P(θ) to the log likelihood. A normal prior on the weights adds −λ||w||2, which is exactly ridge (L2) regularisation, and a Laplace prior gives lasso (L1). You will use both in Supervised 1.2.

Lesson 7 of 13

Entropy, cross entropy and KL divergence

What it is
Entropy measures how uncertain a distribution is. Cross entropy measures how well a predicted distribution matches the true one. KL divergence is the extra cost of using the prediction instead of the truth.
Why we need it
Cross entropy is the loss used to train almost every classifier, from logistic regression to large language models. Entropy is also how decision trees choose splits (information gain), and KL divergence appears in variational autoencoders and in modern reinforcement learning.
How it works
Entropy averages −log p over the outcomes: rare outcomes carry more surprise. Cross entropy averages −log q, the surprise of the model, over what really happens. When the true label is one class, it is simply −log of the probability the model gave to that class.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 entropy_loss.py.
EntropyExam favourite
H(p) = −Σ pi log2 pi
In bits. A fair coin has 1 bit, a sure thing has 0.
Cross entropyExam favourite
H(p, q) = −Σ pi log qi
p is the truth, q the prediction. This is the classification loss.
KL divergenceExam favourite
KL(p || q) = Σ pi log(pi / qi) = H(p, q) − H(p)
Always 0 or more, and 0 only when p = q. Not symmetric.
In the exam
The program shows KL(p || q) = 0.5108 but KL(q || p) = 0.3681. KL divergence is not a true distance because swapping the two distributions changes the answer.
Lesson 8 of 13

Hypothesis tests and confidence intervals

What it is
A hypothesis test asks whether a difference you measured could be just luck. The p value is the chance of seeing a difference at least this big if there were really no difference. A confidence interval gives a range of believable values for the true difference.
Why we need it
You will compare models, features and website versions (A/B tests). A model that is 1% better on one test set may be no better at all. Tests tell you when a difference is real enough to act on.
How it works
Assume there is no difference (the null hypothesis). Use the central limit theorem to know how much the measured difference would wobble by chance (the standard error). Divide the measured difference by that wobble to get a z score, and turn it into a p value. If p is below a chosen level, usually 0.05, call the result significant.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 ab_test.py.
z scoreExam favourite
z = (measured − expected under null) / standard error
How many standard errors away the result is.
95% confidence intervalExam favourite
estimate ± 1.96 × standard error
1.96 is where the normal curve leaves 2.5% in each tail.
TermPlain meaning
Null hypothesisThe boring explanation: there is no real difference
p valueHow surprising the data would be if the null were true
Significance level αThe cut off you chose before the test, often 0.05
Type I errorSaying there is a difference when there is none (false alarm)
Type II errorMissing a difference that is really there
PowerThe chance of finding a real difference. More data gives more power
Common mistake
A p value is not the chance that the null hypothesis is true. If you test 20 useless features at the 0.05 level, about one will look significant by luck. Correct for many tests (for example the Bonferroni correction divides α by the number of tests).

Only look at the cases where B happened.
Bayes ruleExam favourite
P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B)
Posterior from prior and likelihood.
Expectation and varianceExam favourite
E[X] = Σ x P(x), Var(X) = E[X2] − E[X]2
Centre and spread.
Standard errorExam favourite
SE = σ / √n
From the central limit theorem.
Log likelihoodExam favourite
log L(θ) = Σ log P(xi | θ)
Maximise it, or minimise its negative.
Cross entropyExam favourite
H(p, q) = −Σ p log q
The classification loss.
Worked example: expectation and variance of a die
  1. E[X] = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6 = 21 / 6 = 3.5.
  2. E[X2] = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36) / 6 = 91 / 6 = 15.167.
  3. Var(X) = 15.167 − 3.52 = 15.167 − 12.25 = 2.917
  4. σ = √2.917 = 1.708. This is the 1.708 used in the central limit program.
Worked example: binomial probability
  1. What is the chance of exactly 2 heads in 4 fair flips?
  2. C(4, 2) = 4! / (2! 2!) = 6 ways.
  3. P = 6 × 0.52 × 0.52 = 6 / 16 = 0.375
  4. scipy.stats.binom.pmf(2, 4, 0.5) gives 0.375 as well.
Going deeper: Jensen and the reason log loss punishes confident mistakes

log is concave, so Jensen’s inequality gives E[log X] ≤ log E[X]. Gibbs’ inequality, KL(p || q) ≥ 0, follows from it, which proves that cross entropy is smallest exactly when the predicted distribution equals the true one. That is why minimising cross entropy produces calibrated probabilities. Because −log q grows without limit as q goes to 0, a model that gives probability 0.001 to the true class pays −ln(0.001) = 6.9, about 19 times the cost of predicting 0.7.

From beginner to expert

One idea, four depths

Probability is a number from 0 to 1 saying how likely something is. Bayes rule updates a belief when new facts arrive. The mean is the centre of data and the standard deviation is its spread.

Use the sum, product and conditional rules, apply Bayes rule with the base rate, know the Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson and normal distributions, compute mean, variance and correlation, and know that averages shrink their error like σ / √n.

Derive losses from maximum likelihood (MSE from normal noise, log loss from Bernoulli), relate MAP to regularisation, use entropy, cross entropy and KL divergence, and run hypothesis tests and confidence intervals with care about multiple testing.

Exponential families unify the common distributions and give generalised linear models. MLE is consistent and asymptotically normal with variance given by the inverse Fisher information (the Cramér Rao bound). Concentration inequalities (Hoeffding, Chernoff) give finite sample guarantees that underpin PAC learning theory.

Beginner
Compute simple probabilities, use Bayes rule on a medical test, and describe data with mean and spread.
Intermediate
Work with named distributions in scipy, derive the coin MLE, and run an A/B test with a confidence interval.
Expert
Explain where every common loss comes from, reason with KL divergence, and use concentration bounds.

Chapter summary

  1. Probability runs from 0 (never) to 1 (always). Use the complement, sum, product and conditional rules.
  2. Bayes rule: P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B). Never ignore the prior (the base rate).
  3. Named distributions: Bernoulli (yes or no), binomial (yes count), Poisson (event count), normal (bell), categorical (classes).
  4. Describe data with the mean, median, variance and standard deviation. Correlation runs from −1 to 1 and is not causation.
  5. The central limit theorem: averages become normal with spread σ / √n.
  6. Maximum likelihood picks the parameters that make the data most likely. Normal noise gives MSE, yes or no data gives log loss.
  7. Cross entropy is the classification loss. KL divergence is the extra cost of a wrong distribution.
  8. A p value says how surprising the data is if there were no difference. Confidence interval = estimate ± 1.96 SE.
