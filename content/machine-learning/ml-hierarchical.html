Lesson 1 of 10

Hierarchical clustering: a tree of merges

Source: Ward (1963), Sibson, SLINK (1973), The Elements of Statistical Learning section 14.3.12, and the SciPy 1.18 cluster.hierarchy and scikit-learn 1.8 AgglomerativeClustering documentation.

What it is
Agglomerative hierarchical clustering starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one is left. The full history of merges is a tree called a dendrogram. Cutting the tree at some height gives a flat clustering.
Why we need it
It needs no k in advance: one run holds every clustering from n clusters down to 1, and the dendrogram shows how strongly the groups are separated. Many real groupings are nested (species inside a genus inside a family, or cities inside regions inside countries), and a tree shows that directly.
How it works
Compute all pairwise distances. Merge the closest pair of clusters, then update the distances from the new cluster to the others using a linkage rule. Repeat n − 1 times. Cut where the jump in merge height is largest, or where you get the number of clusters you need.

In the exam
Six points need exactly five merges. The first three merges join close pairs at distance 1 or 3. Then the jumps grow: 4 and finally 8. The big jump from 4 to 8 says the data has two natural groups at the top level, and the gap from 1 to 3 says three groups also make sense.

Remember
SciPy writes the tree as a table Z with one row per merge. Original points are numbered 0 to 5, and the cluster made by row r gets the number 6 + r. fcluster cuts the tree, either at a height or to get a chosen number of clusters.
Real world scenario: a family tree read backwards

Picture everyone at a big family reunion. First, siblings stand together. Then sibling groups join their cousins, then cousins join the wider family, until everyone is one family. A photo taken at any moment of that process is one clustering. The dendrogram is the whole album.

Lesson 2 of 10

Linkage: how far apart are two clusters?

What it is
The linkage decides the distance between two clusters. Single uses the closest pair of points, complete the farthest pair, average the mean over all pairs, and Ward the increase in total squared error caused by the merge.
Why we need it
The linkage changes the result more than anything else. Single linkage follows chains and finds long, curved shapes, but one stray point can bridge two groups. Complete and Ward prefer compact, round groups of similar size. Ward is the default in scikit-learn.
How it works
AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=3, linkage="ward") or scipy.cluster.hierarchy.linkage(X, method="ward"). Ward needs Euclidean distances. The others accept any distance, so you can cluster with cosine or edit distance through a precomputed matrix.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 linkages.py.
In the exam
Single linkage follows the two moons perfectly (ARI 1.0) because each moon is a chain of close points. On the blobs it fails completely: it leaves 296 points in one cluster and makes two tiny clusters from 4 stray points. Ward does the opposite: worst on the moons, best on the blobs (0.808). There is no best linkage, only a best linkage for the shape of your data.

Lesson 3 of 10

DBSCAN: clusters are dense regions

Source: Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu, A Density Based Algorithm for Discovering Clusters in Large Spatial Databases with Noise (KDD 1996), Schubert and others, DBSCAN Revisited, Revisited (ACM TODS 2017), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 2.3.7.

What it is
DBSCAN (density based spatial clustering of applications with noise) defines a cluster as a dense region. A core point has at least minPts points within distance eps, counting itself. Core points within eps of each other join the same cluster. A non core point within eps of a core point is a border point. Every other point is noise.
Why we need it
DBSCAN needs no k, finds clusters of any shape, and labels outliers as noise instead of forcing them into a cluster. That makes it a strong choice for maps and GPS points, sensor readings, and any data with odd shapes and stray points.
How it works
Find the neighbours of every point within eps. Mark the core points. Start a cluster at any unvisited core point and keep adding the neighbours of every core point it reaches. Repeat until every core point has a cluster. Points left over are noise and get the label −1.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 dbscan_by_hand.py.
In the exam
Points 0 to 4 form one dense group and points 5 to 8 another, so all nine are core points and make two clusters. Points 9 and 10 have nobody within 0.8, so they are noise. Nobody asked for 2 clusters: the density alone decided it.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 sklearn_dbscan.py.
Remember
DBSCAN recovers both moons perfectly (ARI 1.0 on the real points) and labels 12 of the 15 random points as noise. The other 3 landed close to a moon and joined it as border points. k-means on the same data cuts straight across the moons (ARI 0.241).
Real world scenario: finding towns on a night satellite photo

From space at night, a town is a bright patch where many lights are close together. A lone farmhouse is a single light with nobody nearby. DBSCAN finds towns the same way: a patch of points that are each close to several others is a cluster, whatever its shape, and a lonely light is noise.

Lesson 4 of 10

Choosing eps and minPts

What it is
DBSCAN has two settings. minPts (min_samples in scikit-learn) is how many points make a region dense. eps is the radius of the neighbourhood. The k distance plot helps choose eps: sort every point’s distance to its k-th nearest neighbour and look for the knee where the curve shoots up.
Why we need it
eps decides almost everything. Too small and most points become noise, with real clusters shattered into pieces. Too large and separate clusters merge into one. A good eps sits just above the distances inside clusters.
How it works
Choose minPts first: a common rule is 2 × the number of features, and at least 4 or 5. Then compute each point’s distance to its minPts-th neighbour (counting itself, as scikit-learn does), sort, and set eps near the knee. Scale the features first, because eps is one radius for every direction.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 choose_eps.py.
In the exam
Half the points have their 5th neighbour within 0.079 and 99% within 0.179, then the largest is 0.285. That knee suggests eps around 0.2, which gives exactly 2 clusters and no noise. At 0.1 the moons break into 8 pieces with 16 noise points, at 0.05 almost everything is noise, and from 0.4 the two moons merge into one cluster.
A quick recipe for DBSCAN settings
  1. Scale the features, for example with StandardScaler.
  2. Set minPts to about 2 × the number of features (at least 4).
  3. Plot the sorted distance to the minPts-th neighbour.
  4. Set eps at the knee of that curve.
  5. Check the number of clusters and the share of noise. More than a few percent of noise in clean data usually means eps is too small.
Lesson 5 of 10

Clusters of different density: HDBSCAN and OPTICS

Source: Campello, Moulavi and Sander, Density Based Clustering Based on Hierarchical Density Estimates (PAKDD 2013), Ankerst and others, OPTICS (SIGMOD 1999), and the scikit-learn 1.8 HDBSCAN and OPTICS documentation.

What it is
DBSCAN uses one eps for the whole dataset, so it struggles when clusters have different densities. HDBSCAN runs DBSCAN for every eps at once, builds a tree of the dense regions, and keeps the clusters that stay stable across many eps values. OPTICS orders the points so that a single reachability plot shows the clusters at every density as valleys.
Why we need it
Real data rarely has one density: a city centre is much denser than its suburbs. HDBSCAN needs only one easy setting, the smallest cluster size you care about, and it is often the best first choice for density based clustering.
How it works
HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=20).fit_predict(X), available in scikit-learn since version 1.3. OPTICS(min_samples=20, xi=0.05) extracts clusters from steep drops in the reachability plot.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 densities.py.
In the exam
With one tight, one wide and one medium group, eps 0.3 suits the tight group but shatters the wide one into pieces (8 clusters, 161 noise points). eps 1.5 suits the wide group but merges two groups (2 clusters). eps 1.0 happens to work here, but you only know that because the true labels are known. HDBSCAN finds the 3 groups (ARI 0.952) without any eps, and OPTICS also finds 3 (ARI 0.94).
Going deeper: How HDBSCAN builds its tree

HDBSCAN replaces the plain distance with the mutual reachability distance dmreach(a, b) = max(core(a), core(b), d(a, b)), where core(a) is the distance from a to its k-th neighbour. Points in sparse areas are pushed further from everything. It builds the minimum spanning tree of this distance, which is single linkage clustering, and turns it into a tree of clusters by removing edges from the longest down. Each cluster gets a stability score: how long its points stay in it as the density threshold rises. It then picks the set of non overlapping clusters with the highest total stability. Clusters smaller than min_cluster_size are treated as points falling out of a cluster, not as new clusters.

QuestionAnswer
Is k-means a special case of agglomerative clustering?No. Ward uses the same squared error objective, but merges greedily and never moves a point back. The two can give different answers.
Is single linkage related to DBSCAN?Yes. Single linkage cut at height eps gives the connected groups of DBSCAN with minPts = 1 or 2, and HDBSCAN is single linkage on a density aware distance.
Can DBSCAN predict a new point?Not directly. scikit-learn DBSCAN has no predict. Assign a new point to the cluster of its nearest core point within eps, or noise otherwise.

Worked example: one Ward merge cost
  1. Cluster A has 2 points with mean (0, 0). Cluster B has 3 points with mean (3, 4).
  2. The squared distance between the means is 32 + 42 = 25.
  3. The size factor is (2 × 3) / (2 + 3) = 6 / 5 = 1.2.
  4. Δ(A, B) = 1.2 × 25 = 30
  5. Merging A and B raises the total within cluster squared error by 30. Ward merges the pair with the smallest such rise.
Going deeper: Single linkage is the minimum spanning tree

Build the minimum spanning tree (MST) of the points, with edge weights equal to distances. Kruskal’s algorithm adds edges from shortest to longest, joining two components each time, and that is exactly the sequence of single linkage merges with the same heights. So the single linkage clustering with k clusters is the MST with its k − 1 longest edges removed. This also explains chaining: one short edge through a stray point is enough to join two groups. The cophenetic distance between two points is the height at which they first share a cluster, and the correlation between cophenetic and original distances measures how faithfully a dendrogram represents the data.

Lesson 10 of 10

Chapter summary

  1. Agglomerative clustering merges the two closest clusters n − 1 times. The dendrogram records every merge, and a cut gives any number of clusters.
  2. The linkage matters most: single follows chains but bridges through noise, complete and Ward prefer compact round groups, Ward is the default.
  3. DBSCAN: core points have at least minPts neighbours within eps. Clusters grow through core points, border points join, the rest is noise (label −1).
  4. Choose minPts around 2 × the number of features and eps at the knee of the k distance plot. Scale the features first.
  5. One eps cannot fit clusters of very different density. HDBSCAN handles that with a single setting, min_cluster_size.
  6. Agglomerative clustering costs n2 memory, so it suits thousands of points, while DBSCAN and HDBSCAN scale much further.
