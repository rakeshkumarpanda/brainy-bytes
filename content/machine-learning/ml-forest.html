Lesson 1 of 10

Many trees are wiser than one

Source: Breiman, Bagging Predictors (1996) and Random Forests (2001), Geurts, Ernst and Wehenkel (2006), The Elements of Statistical Learning chapters 8.7 and 15, An Introduction to Statistical Learning chapter 8.2, and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 1.11.

What it is
A random forest trains many decision trees, each on a different random version of the data, and combines them: a majority vote for classification, an average for regression.
Why we need it
A single deep tree has low bias but very high variance: it changes a lot when the data changes. Averaging many such trees keeps the low bias and removes much of the variance, so a forest is far more accurate and stable than one tree, with almost no tuning.
How it works
Two sources of randomness make the trees differ. Each tree trains on a bootstrap sample of the rows (bagging), and at every split it may only look at a random subset of the features (max_features). Then predictions are averaged.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 tree_vs_forest.py.
Remember
One tree scores 0.917. Bagging 200 trees lifts that to 0.96, and the random forest also reaches 0.96. On this dataset the extra feature randomness adds little over bagging, but on data with a few dominant features it usually helps more, as the max_features lesson shows.
Real world scenario: guessing the weight of an ox

At a country fair in 1906, Francis Galton collected about 800 guesses of an ox’s weight. Single guesses were often far off, but the middle of all the guesses was within 1% of the true weight. The errors pointed in different directions and cancelled. A forest relies on the same effect, which only works when the guesses are independent enough.

Lesson 2 of 10

Bootstrap samples and bagging

What it is
Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains each model on a bootstrap sample: n rows drawn at random with replacement from the n training rows. Then it averages the models.
Why we need it
Bootstrap samples are different from each other but each looks like the original data, so every tree is a reasonable model with its own mistakes. On average a sample contains 63.2% of the distinct rows, and the remaining 36.8% (out of bag) can test that tree for free.
How it works
BaggingClassifier(DecisionTreeClassifier(), n_estimators=200) does bagging with any model. RandomForestClassifier does it for trees and adds random features at each split.

Lesson 3 of 10

Why averaging works, and why trees must differ

What it is
Averaging B predictions that each have variance σ2 and pairwise correlation ρ gives variance ρσ2 + (1 − ρ)σ2/B.
Why we need it
The second part shrinks as you add trees, but the first part, ρσ2, never does. So adding trees helps only up to a point, and the real gain comes from making the trees less correlated. That is exactly what the random feature choice at each split does.
How it works
The program simulates averaging with correlation 0, 0.3 and 0.7. With no correlation, 100 trees cut the variance to 1%. With correlation 0.7 the variance stays near 0.7 however many trees you add.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 averaging.py.
Variance of an averageExam favourite
Var = ρσ2 + (1 − ρ)σ2 / B
More trees shrink the second term, less correlation shrinks the first.
In the exam
This formula is the heart of random forests and appears in The Elements of Statistical Learning, equation 15.1. Lower correlation between trees matters more than more trees.
Lesson 4 of 10

Number of trees, out of bag score and max_features

What it is
The main settings are n_estimators (number of trees), max_features (features tried at each split) and the tree size limits such as min_samples_leaf. The out of bag (OOB) score estimates test accuracy without a separate validation set.
Why we need it
More trees never overfit, they only cost time, so the question is when the curve flattens. max_features controls the trade off between strong individual trees and diverse trees. OOB scoring saves data when it is scarce.
How it works
Start with 200 to 500 trees, max_features="sqrt" for classification (the scikit-learn default) and 1.0 for regression, then tune max_features and min_samples_leaf with cross validation. Set oob_score=True to get forest.oob_score_.

Lesson 5 of 10

Forests for regression, and extra trees

What it is
RandomForestRegressor averages the predictions of regression trees. Extra trees (extremely randomized trees) go further and choose the cut points at random too, instead of searching for the best one.
Why we need it
Forests give strong regression models with little tuning. Extra trees are faster to train and the extra randomness often lowers variance a little more. But no model wins everywhere: on small, mostly linear data a linear model can beat any forest.
How it works
Use the same settings as for classification. Averaging trees gives smoother predictions than one tree, but still flat beyond the range of the training targets.

Worked example: majority vote of three independent classifiers
  1. Each of three classifiers is right 70% of the time, independently.
  2. The vote is right when at least 2 of 3 are right.
  3. Exactly 2 right: 3 × 0.72 × 0.3 = 0.441. All 3 right: 0.73 = 0.343.
  4. P(vote right) = 0.441 + 0.343 = 0.784
  5. The vote beats each member (0.70). With correlated members the gain is smaller, which is why forests decorrelate their trees.
Going deeper: Breiman’s strength and correlation bound

Breiman (2001) proved that the generalisation error of a random forest is at most ρ̄(1 − s2)/s2, where s is the strength (the average margin by which the trees vote for the right class) and ρ̄ is the mean correlation between the trees. Error falls when the trees are stronger and less correlated, and max_features trades one against the other. He also showed by the strong law of large numbers that the forest error converges as trees are added, which is why more trees never overfit. Later work (Wager and Athey, 2018) built honest forests with valid confidence intervals, used for estimating treatment effects.

Chapter summary

  1. A random forest averages many decision trees: majority vote (or mean probability) for classes, mean for numbers.
  2. Bagging: each tree trains on a bootstrap sample. About 63.2% of rows are in each sample, the rest are out of bag.
  3. Random max_features at each split makes the trees less correlated.
  4. Variance of the average = ρσ2 + (1 − ρ)σ2/B: decorrelation matters more than more trees.
  5. More trees never overfit. Use 200 to 500, tune max_features and min_samples_leaf, read oob_score_.
  6. Extra trees also randomise the cut points. Always compare with a simple baseline.
