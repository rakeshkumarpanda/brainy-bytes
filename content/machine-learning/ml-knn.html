Lesson 1 of 11

Ask the neighbours

Source: James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani, An Introduction to Statistical Learning chapters 2.2.3 and 4.7.6, Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning chapter 13.3, Cover and Hart (1967), Beyer and others (1999), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 1.6.

What it is
k nearest neighbours (KNN) predicts the class of a new example by finding the k training examples closest to it and taking a majority vote. For numbers, it averages their values instead.
Why we need it
It needs no training and makes no assumption about the shape of the boundary, so it can follow any pattern if there is enough data. It is easy to explain ("these 5 similar customers all left") and it is the idea behind recommendation ("people similar to you liked...") and similarity search.
How it works
Store all the training examples. For a new point, compute its distance to every stored example, sort, keep the k smallest, and vote. The two choices that matter are k and the distance measure, and the features must be scaled so that no feature dominates the distance.

Real world scenario: choosing a restaurant in a new town

You do not know the town, so you ask the five people standing closest to you which restaurant is good. If four say "the dosa place", you go there. You did not build any theory of food, you just trusted the nearest opinions. KNN works exactly like that.

Lesson 2 of 11

Choosing k

What it is
k is the number of neighbours that vote. Small k follows the training data closely, large k smooths it.
Why we need it
k = 1 always gets 100% on the training data, because every point is its own nearest neighbour, but it copies every noisy point (overfitting). A very large k ignores local patterns and predicts the majority class everywhere (underfitting).
How it works
Try a range of k values with cross validation and pick the best. For two classes use an odd k to avoid ties. A rule of thumb starting point is k ≈ √n, but always check with cross validation.

In the exam
In the program, k = 1 scores 1.0 on training data but only 0.86 in cross validation. The best is around k = 7 (0.913), and k = 101 underfits (0.82). Training accuracy is useless for choosing k.
Lesson 3 of 11

Distance measures and scaling

What it is
KNN needs a distance. Euclidean is the straight line, Manhattan adds the differences along each axis (city blocks), Minkowski with power p covers both, and cosine compares directions and ignores length.
Why we need it
The distance decides who counts as a neighbour. Euclidean suits measurements in comparable units, Manhattan is less sensitive to one big difference, and cosine suits text and other count vectors where length means "longer document", not "different topic".
How it works
Pick the metric that matches what "similar" means for your data. Always scale numeric features first (StandardScaler): income in thousands would swamp age in years. In scikit-learn, set metric and p in KNeighborsClassifier.

Minkowski distanceExam favourite
d(a, b) = (Σ |ai − bi|p)1/p
p = 1 Manhattan, p = 2 Euclidean, p → ∞ Chebyshev (largest difference).
Cosine similarityExam favourite
cos(a, b) = a·b / (||a|| ||b||)
1 means the same direction. Cosine distance = 1 − cos.
Common mistake
Foundations 3.2 showed scaling raise KNN accuracy on the wine data from 0.691 to 0.949. Scaling is not optional for KNN.
Lesson 4 of 11

KNN for numbers and weighted votes

What it is
KNeighborsRegressor predicts a number as the average of the k nearest targets. With weights="distance", closer neighbours count more (weight 1/d) in both classification and regression.
Why we need it
Many targets are numbers (price, temperature). Distance weighting also helps when the k neighbours are at very different distances: the one right next to the new point should matter more than one far away.
How it works
Find the k nearest, then average their targets, optionally weighted by 1/distance. The prediction curve is a smooth staircase that follows the data without any formula.

The curse of dimensionality

What it is
In many dimensions, all points end up at almost the same distance from each other. The nearest neighbour is then hardly nearer than the farthest point, so "nearest" stops meaning "similar".
Why we need it
KNN depends completely on distances. With hundreds of features, most of them noise, KNN gets slow and inaccurate.
How it works
The program puts 500 random points in a cube and measures the nearest and farthest distance from a query point. In 2 dimensions the nearest is 30 times closer than the farthest. In 1,000 dimensions the ratio is 0.9: everything is about equally far. Fix it by selecting features or reducing dimensions (PCA, Unsupervised 2.1) before KNN.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 curse_of_dimensions.py.
Going deeper: How fast the data must grow

To keep a neighbourhood that holds a fraction r of uniform data in d dimensions, its side must be r1/d of the whole range. For r = 1% and d = 10 that is 0.63, so the "local" neighbourhood spans 63% of every feature’s range, which is not local at all. Keeping the same density needs data that grows exponentially in d. Beyer and others (1999) proved that for many distributions the ratio of farthest to nearest distance goes to 1 as d grows.

Lesson 6 of 11

Similarity search and tuning

What it is
NearestNeighbors finds the k most similar items without predicting anything. It is the core of "more like this" features. Fast search structures (k-d tree, ball tree) and approximate methods (used in vector databases) avoid comparing against every item.
Why we need it
Brute force KNN compares a query with all n items, which is too slow for millions of items or for answering many queries per second.
How it works
k-d trees split the space into boxes so whole boxes can be skipped, and work well up to about 20 dimensions. For embeddings with hundreds of dimensions, approximate nearest neighbour indexes (HNSW, IVF) trade a tiny bit of accuracy for huge speed. For tuning, put the scaler and KNN in a Pipeline and search k, weights and p together with GridSearchCV.

The maths, all in one place

KNN classificationExam favourite
ŷ = most common class among the k nearest Nk(x)
Majority vote.
KNN regressionExam favourite
ŷ = (1/k) Σi ∈ Nk(x) yi
Local average.
Euclidean distanceExam favourite
d = √Σ(ai − bi)2
The default.
Cost of brute force predictionExam favourite
O(n × d) per query
n stored examples, d features.
Cover and Hart bound
R* ≤ R1NN ≤ 2R*(1 − R*) as n → ∞
With endless data, 1-NN errs at most twice the best possible error R* (two classes).
Worked example: KNN by hand
  1. New person (161, 61). Distances to the 8 people, sorted: 2.0 M, 2.24 M, 2.83 M, 3.16 L, 4.24 M, ...
  2. k = 3: the three nearest are M, M, M.
  3. Votes M = 3, L = 0, so the prediction is M
  4. k = 4 would add an L: votes M = 3, L = 1, still M. The distance 2.0 comes from (163, 61): √((163 − 161)2 + 0) = √4 = 2.
Going deeper: Why KNN works in the limit

As n grows and k grows but k/n shrinks, the k nearest neighbours of x crowd into a tiny ball around x, and their labels become a sample from P(y | x). A majority vote over a growing sample estimates the most likely class, so KNN approaches the Bayes optimal classifier (Stone, 1977). The catch is the rate: in d dimensions the error shrinks like n−2/(d + 2) for smooth problems, which is painfully slow when d is large.

From beginner to expert

One idea, four depths

To label something new, find the most similar examples you already know and copy their most common label.

Scale features, choose k with cross validation, pick Euclidean, Manhattan or cosine distance, use distance weights, and run KNN for both classes and numbers.

Explain the bias and variance effect of k, the curse of dimensionality, the cost of prediction, and when to use k-d trees, ball trees or approximate search.

Know the Cover and Hart bound, consistency of KNN, metric learning (learning the distance itself, for example large margin nearest neighbour), and approximate nearest neighbour indexes such as HNSW behind vector databases.

Beginner
Predict a class by asking the nearest examples.
Intermediate
Tune k, the metric and weights in a scaled pipeline.
Expert
Reason about high dimensions, search structures and the theory of nearest neighbour rules.

Chapter summary

  1. KNN stores the training data and predicts by a majority vote (classes) or an average (numbers) of the k nearest examples.
  2. Small k overfits (k = 1 is 100% on training data), large k underfits. Choose k by cross validation, odd for two classes.
  3. Scale features first. Pick the distance: Euclidean, Manhattan, Minkowski p, or cosine for text and counts.
  4. weights="distance" lets closer neighbours count more.
  5. The curse of dimensionality: in many dimensions all distances look alike. Reduce features first.
  6. No training cost but slow predictions, O(n d) per query. k-d trees and approximate search speed it up.
