Model based RL: learning a model and planning with it

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 8.1 to 8.3 and example 8.1, and Sutton, Dyna, an integrated architecture for learning, planning, and reacting (1991).

What it is
A model predicts what the environment will do: the next state and reward for a state and action. Model based RL learns such a model from experience and uses it to plan, that is to improve values or the policy with simulated experience. Dyna-Q mixes both: every real step updates Q directly, updates the model, and then runs n planning updates on remembered transitions.
Why we need it
Real experience is often slow, costly or risky: a robot wears out, a patient cannot be a test case. Each real step in Dyna is reused many times through the model, so far fewer real steps are needed. Model free methods learn only from what actually happened.
How it works
Keep a table model(s, a) = (s′, r) of the last outcome seen. After each real Q learning update, repeat n times: pick a remembered (s, a), look up (s′, r) and apply the same Q learning update. With a deterministic world this model is exact. With noise, store counts or learn a probabilistic model.

Planning at decision time: MCTS, AlphaZero and MuZero

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 8.8 to 8.11 and 16.6, Silver and colleagues (Nature 2016 and 2017, Science 2018), and Schrittwieser and colleagues, MuZero (Nature 2020).

What it is
Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) plans from the current state each time a move is needed. It grows a search tree with four repeated steps: select a path with an upper confidence rule, expand a new node, evaluate it by a rollout or a value network, and back up the result along the path. AlphaZero guides MCTS with a network that outputs a policy and a value, and trains that network on the search results from self play.
Why we need it
Searching from the current position focuses all computation on the states that matter now. Combining search with learned policy and value networks gave superhuman play in Go, chess and shogi. AlphaGo Zero learned Go with no human games and beat the earlier AlphaGo version that defeated Lee Sedol by 100 games to 0.
How it works
At each node pick the child that maximises Q + U, where U grows with the network’s prior for that move and shrinks as the move is visited. After many simulations, play the move visited most. MuZero goes one step further and learns its own model of rewards, values and policies, so it needs no rules of the game.

Offline RL and imitation learning

Source: Levine, Kumar, Tucker and Fu (2020), Kumar and colleagues, Conservative Q-Learning (NeurIPS 2020), Ross, Gordon and Bagnell, DAgger (AISTATS 2011), and Pomerleau, ALVINN (1989).

What it is
Offline RL (also called batch RL) learns a policy from a fixed dataset of past interactions, with no new exploration. Imitation learning learns from demonstrations: behaviour cloning treats it as supervised learning from states to the expert’s actions, and DAgger asks the expert to label the states the learner itself visits.
Why we need it
Hospitals, factories and recommenders have huge logs but cannot experiment freely. The danger is distribution shift: a Q function trained on the log is unreliable for actions the log never contains, and the max in Q learning seeks out exactly those errors. Cloning has its own problem: one small mistake leads to unfamiliar states, where mistakes pile up.
How it works
Stay close to the data. Behaviour cloning copies it. Conservative methods such as CQL push down the values of actions the data does not support, so the max cannot exploit them. Other methods constrain the policy to the behaviour policy (BCQ, TD3+BC) or never query unseen actions at all (IQL).

RLHF: learning from human preferences

Source: Christiano and colleagues, Deep RL from human preferences (NeurIPS 2017), Ouyang and colleagues, Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (NeurIPS 2022), and Bradley and Terry (1952).

What it is
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) trains a policy towards what people prefer when the reward is hard to write down. People compare two outputs, a reward model learns to score outputs so that preferred ones score higher, and RL (usually PPO) tunes the policy to maximise that score minus a KL penalty that keeps it close to a reference model.
Why we need it
Nobody can write a formula for a helpful, honest answer, but people can tell which of two answers is better. Christiano and colleagues taught agents Atari games and simulated robot skills with feedback on less than 1% of the agent’s interactions. InstructGPT used RLHF to make a 1.3 billion parameter model whose answers labellers preferred to those of the 175 billion parameter GPT-3.
How it works
Three steps, as in InstructGPT. (1) Supervised fine tuning on demonstrations gives the reference policy. (2) Collect comparisons and fit the reward model with the Bradley–Terry loss −ln σ(rw − rl). (3) Run PPO on reward minus β times the KL divergence from the reference.

In the exam
The reward model learns what the labellers prefer, including their mistake: the made up answer seems best to them and gets the highest learned reward, 2.82, though its true quality is −1. With β = 2 the tuned policy still improves true quality from −0.15 to 0.13. With β = 0.1 it puts all its probability on the made up answer and true quality falls to −1. The KL penalty limits how hard the policy can exploit reward model errors.

DPO and learning from AI feedback

Source: Rafailov and colleagues, Direct Preference Optimization (NeurIPS 2023), and Bai and colleagues, Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback (2022).

What it is
Direct preference optimisation (DPO) skips both the separate reward model and the RL loop. Because the best KL regularised policy is π ∝ πrefer/β, the reward can be written as β ln(π/πref) plus a constant, and substituting this into the Bradley–Terry loss gives a loss on the policy alone. RL from AI feedback replaces some human labels with judgements from a model guided by written principles, as in Constitutional AI.
Why we need it
PPO based RLHF needs four models in memory (policy, reference, reward model and value function) and careful tuning. DPO is a simple classification style loss on preference pairs, which made preference tuning far easier to run. AI feedback makes labels cheaper and more consistent, though it inherits the judge’s blind spots.
How it works
For each pair compute the log probability ratio of the winner and the loser under the policy and the reference, take β times their difference, and minimise −ln σ of it. No sampling, no reward model, no value network.

Making RL work safely in the real world

Source: Amodei and colleagues, Concrete Problems in AI Safety (2016), Krakovna and colleagues, Specification gaming examples in AI (DeepMind 2020), and Henderson and colleagues, Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters (AAAI 2018).

What it is
An RL agent optimises exactly the reward it is given, not what the designer meant. Reward hacking or specification gaming is when it finds a loophole: a boat racing agent in CoastRunners learned to circle and hit the same targets forever instead of finishing the race. Real deployments also face unsafe exploration, the gap between simulation and reality, and results that change with the random seed.
Why we need it
RL is used for recommendations, data centre cooling, chip floor planning, robotics and tuning language models. Mistakes in such systems are costly, and the most capable optimiser will find the reward function’s weakest spot first.
How it works
Write rewards carefully and test them against shortcuts. Keep a KL or similar constraint to known good behaviour. Explore in simulation, then transfer with domain randomisation. Evaluate on many seeds with confidence intervals, watch for distribution shift after deployment, and keep a human able to stop the system.

Worked example: why π* ∝ πrefer/β
  1. Maximise Σy π(y)r(y) − βΣy π(y) ln(π(y)/πref(y)) subject to Σy π(y) = 1.
  2. Set the derivative for each y to zero: r(y) − β[ln(π(y)/πref(y)) + 1] − λ = 0
  3. So ln(π(y)/πref(y)) = r(y)/β + constant, giving π(y) ∝ πref(y) er(y)/β
  4. Two answers with πref = 0.5 each and rewards 1 and 0, β = 1: π(first) = e / (e + 1) = 0.731.
  5. Rearranging the same line gives r(y) = β ln(π(y)/πref(y)) + constant, which is the reward DPO uses implicitly.
Going deeper: Model errors and how modern model based RL copes

A learned model is wrong somewhere, and a planner that maximises over the model will find and exploit those errors, just as Q learning exploits value errors. Errors also compound: a one step error of ε can grow quickly over a long imagined rollout. Modern methods respond in several ways. MBPO uses short model rollouts branched from real states, so errors cannot compound far. PETS and similar methods train an ensemble of models and treat their disagreement as uncertainty to avoid. Dreamer learns a compact latent state and trains an actor critic entirely inside imagined latent trajectories. MuZero sidesteps the problem of predicting observations at all, learning only what planning needs: rewards, values and policies.

Chapter summary

  1. Model based RL learns a model and plans with simulated experience. Dyna-Q with n = 50 needs 19.4 steps in episode 5 where plain Q learning needs 271.4.
  2. MCTS plans at decision time. AlphaZero guides it with policy and value networks trained by self play, and MuZero learns its own model.
  3. Offline RL learns from a fixed log. Unseen actions get trusted wrongly, so conservative methods such as CQL push them down. Behaviour cloning and DAgger learn from demonstrations.
  4. RLHF: fit a Bradley–Terry reward model to comparisons, then optimise reward minus β times the KL from a reference. Small β invites reward hacking.
  5. DPO reaches the same KL regularised policy directly from preference pairs, with no reward model or RL loop.
  6. Real deployments need careful rewards, safe exploration, robustness to shift and evaluation over many seeds.
