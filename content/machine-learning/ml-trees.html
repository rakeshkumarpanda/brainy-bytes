Lesson 1 of 11

A model made of yes or no questions

Source: Breiman and others, Classification and Regression Trees (1984), Quinlan, C4.5 (1993), The Elements of Statistical Learning section 9.2, An Introduction to Statistical Learning chapter 8, Strobl and others (2007), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 1.10.

What it is
A decision tree predicts by asking a chain of yes or no questions about the features, such as "petal width ≤ 0.8?". Each answer leads to another question until a leaf gives the prediction: a class for classification or a number for regression.
Why we need it
Trees are easy to read and explain, need no feature scaling, handle numbers and categories, and capture interactions such as "only large and old houses are expensive" on their own. They are also the building block of random forests and gradient boosting, the strongest models for tabular data.
How it works
Training is greedy: at the root, try every feature and every cut point, keep the question that makes the two groups purest, then repeat inside each group. Stop when a group is pure, too small, or the tree reaches its maximum depth.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 tree_iris.py.
Remember
Two questions about one feature already classify 96% of the iris flowers. The tree never looked at sepal length or petal length because petal width was always the best question. You can print any tree with export_text or draw it with plot_tree.
Real world scenario: the twenty questions game

In twenty questions a good player first asks "is it alive?" rather than "is it a giraffe?", because the first question splits the possibilities roughly in half whatever the answer. A decision tree plays the same game with the data: at every step it asks the question that tells it the most.

Lesson 2 of 11

Choosing a split: Gini and entropy

What it is
A split is good when each side is pure, mostly one class. Two common measures of impurity are the Gini impurity 1 − Σpk2 and the entropy −Σpk log2 pk. Both are 0 for a pure group and largest for an even mix.
Why we need it
The tree needs a single number to compare thousands of candidate questions. Weighting each side’s impurity by its size gives exactly that, and the drop in impurity is called the information gain when entropy is used.
How it works
For each feature, sort the values and try the midpoint between every pair of neighbours. Compute the weighted impurity of the two sides and keep the lowest. scikit-learn uses criterion="gini" by default, criterion="entropy" or "log_loss" for entropy.

Overfitting, depth and pruning

What it is
A tree that keeps splitting will end with one training point per leaf: 100% training accuracy and poor test accuracy. You control this by stopping early (pre pruning) or by growing a full tree and cutting it back (post pruning).
Why we need it
Trees have very high variance. Without limits they memorise noise, as the depth table shows: training accuracy reaches 1.0 while test accuracy stalls.
How it works
Pre pruning settings: max_depth, min_samples_leaf, min_samples_split, max_leaf_nodes. Post pruning: cost complexity pruning with ccp_alpha, chosen by cross validation from cost_complexity_pruning_path.

Lesson 4 of 11

Regression trees

What it is
A regression tree predicts a number: each leaf predicts the mean of the training targets that land in it. Splits are chosen to reduce the squared error instead of the Gini impurity.
Why we need it
Regression trees capture jumps and thresholds, such as "price rises sharply above 100 square metres", that a straight line cannot. They are also the trees inside gradient boosting.
How it works
Use DecisionTreeRegressor. The prediction is a step function: flat inside each leaf, jumping at each cut. A deeper tree gives more, smaller steps.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 regression_tree.py.
In the exam
With depth 1 the tree knows only two numbers (0.59 and −0.7), so 0.5, 1.5 and 3.0 all get the same prediction. Depth 5 has 23 steps and follows the sine curve closely, including some of its noise. A tree can never predict outside the range of the training targets, so it cannot extrapolate a trend.

Lesson 5 of 11

Feature importance

What it is
Trees report a feature importance for each feature: the total impurity drop from all splits on that feature, scaled to add up to 1. Permutation importance instead measures how much the test score falls when one feature’s values are shuffled.
Why we need it
Importances help explain a model and spot leaks. But impurity importance is measured on the training data and favours features with many distinct values, even pure noise.
How it works
Read feature_importances_ for a quick look. For decisions, use sklearn.inspection.permutation_importance on held out data.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 importance.py.
Remember
One feature (worst concave points) carries 73% of the impurity importance. A column of pure random noise still gets 0.009 impurity importance because the full tree used it to split a few training points, while its permutation importance on test data is about 0 (−0.005). Strobl and others (2007) documented this bias.
Lesson 6 of 11

Why one tree is not enough

What it is
Trees are unstable: a small change in the training data can change the very first question, and with it the whole tree.
Why we need it
High variance means a single tree’s predictions are noisy. This weakness is the reason ensembles exist: averaging many different trees cancels much of the noise.
How it works
Train a tree on several bootstrap samples (rows drawn with replacement) and look at the root question. Supervised 3.2 averages such trees into a random forest.

In the exam
Five bootstrap samples of the same data give three different root features and between 12 and 19 leaves. Each tree is a reasonable model, but they disagree. That disagreement is variance, and averaging removes it.

Cost complexityExam favourite

Prune leaves until the price outweighs the gain.
Worked example: the best cut for hours studied
  1. Eight students, 3 passed. Before splitting: p = 3/8, G = 1 − 0.3752 − 0.6252 = 0.469.
  2. Cut at 4.5: the left 4 students all failed, so GL = 0.
  3. The right 4 have 3 passes: GR = 1 − 0.752 − 0.252 = 0.375.
  4. weighted G = (4 × 0 + 4 × 0.375) / 8 = 0.1875
  5. No other cut is lower, so the drop is 0.469 − 0.188 = 0.281, as the program prints.
Going deeper: Why greedy, and what it costs

Finding the smallest tree that fits the data exactly is NP complete (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976), so every practical algorithm is greedy: it picks the best split now without looking ahead. Sometimes no single split helps but two together would, as in the XOR pattern, where the greedy tree can stop too early or choose a poor first split. CART (Breiman and others, 1984) builds binary trees with Gini or squared error and prunes with cost complexity. ID3 and C4.5 (Quinlan) use entropy, allow multiway splits on categories, and C4.5 uses the gain ratio to avoid favouring features with many values. Newer work such as optimal sparse trees (GOSDT, 2020) finds provably optimal small trees for modest data sizes.

Chapter summary

  1. A decision tree predicts with a chain of yes or no questions. Leaves hold a class or the mean target.
  2. Each split minimises the weighted Gini impurity or entropy. The drop in entropy is the information gain.
  3. Training is greedy: best split at the root, then repeat in each branch.
  4. Unlimited trees overfit. Limit max_depth or min_samples_leaf, or prune with ccp_alpha chosen by cross validation.
  5. Regression trees give step functions and cannot extrapolate.
  6. Impurity importance is biased towards noisy, many valued features. Prefer permutation importance on test data.
  7. Trees are unstable (high variance). Averaging many trees fixes that.
