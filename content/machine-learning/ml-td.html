Lesson 1 of 9

Learning without a model: Monte Carlo prediction

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 5.1 and 5.2, and example 6.2 (the random walk).

What it is
When the transition probabilities are unknown, the agent can still learn values from experience. Monte Carlo (MC) prediction plays whole episodes and, for each state visited, averages the returns that actually followed it. First visit MC counts only the first visit to a state in an episode, every visit MC counts them all.
Why we need it
Most real problems have no model: nobody can write down the chance of every outcome in a game against people, or in a robot’s contact with the floor. MC needs only the ability to run episodes, in the world or in a simulator, and its estimates have no bias because they average real returns.
How it works
Run an episode with the policy. Walk backwards through it to compute the return G from each step. Update V(s) towards G with a running average or a small constant step size. Repeat for many episodes.

In the exam
The walk starts in C and moves left or right at random. Reaching the right end gives 1, so the true value of a state is its chance of finishing on the right: 1/6 to 5/6. After one episode MC knows only that one walk ended on the right. After 1,000 episodes its averages are within 0.011 of the truth (RMS error). MC is unbiased but needs many episodes because single returns vary a lot.
Monte Carlo updateExam favourite
V(St) ← V(St) + α[Gt − V(St)]
Target: the full return Gt. Waits for the episode to end.
Sample average form
α = 1 / N(St)
Converges to vπ by the law of large numbers.
Lesson 2 of 9

Temporal difference learning: TD(0)

Source: Sutton, Learning to Predict by the Methods of Temporal Differences (1988), and Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 6.1 to 6.3.

What it is
Temporal difference (TD) learning updates after every single step. Its target is the reward just received plus the discounted current estimate of the next state: r + γV(s′). The gap between target and old estimate is the TD error δ. Using an estimate to update an estimate is called bootstrapping.
Why we need it
TD learns during the episode, works for tasks that never end, and its targets vary much less than full returns, so it usually learns faster than MC. It is the core of Q learning, SARSA, actor critic methods and DQN. Tesauro’s TD-Gammon learned to play backgammon at world class level with it in the early 1990s.
How it works
After each step compute δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s) and set V(s) ← V(s) + αδ. Terminal states have value 0. Keep α small and constant for drifting problems, or shrink it over time for exact convergence.

A constant step size trades exact convergence for the ability to track change.

Lesson 3 of 9

Monte Carlo against TD: bias, variance and batch learning

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 6.2 and 6.3, figure 6.2 and example 6.4.

What it is
MC targets (whole returns) are unbiased but high variance: they include the randomness of every later step. TD targets are biased but low variance: they depend on one step of randomness plus a guess that may be wrong. On the same fixed data, MC finds the values that best fit the observed returns, while batch TD finds the values of the most likely Markov model behind the data.
Why we need it
Knowing why TD usually wins, and when it does not, helps choose between them. TD exploits the Markov property. If the state is not truly Markov, MC can be safer.
How it works
Compare them on the same episodes with several step sizes, as below, and look at the error curves over time. For batch data, replay the whole set many times with small steps until the values settle.

In the exam
Averaged over 100 runs, TD with α = 0.05 reaches an error of 0.035 after 100 episodes, against 0.094 for MC with either step size. TD with a large α of 0.15 is fastest early (0.095 after 10 episodes) but then stalls near 0.07, because each step jumps too far. Step size matters as much as the method.

Remember
Eight episodes: A then B with no reward once, then B alone six times with reward 1 and once with 0. Both methods give V(B) = 0.75. MC gives V(A) = 0, because the only return seen after A was 0. Batch TD gives V(A) = 0.75, because A always led to B and B is worth 0.75. TD’s answer is the one a Markov model of the data implies, and it usually predicts future data better.

Between the two: n step TD and TD(&lambda;)

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) chapters 7 and 12, and Singh and Sutton (1996).

What it is
n step TD uses n real rewards and then bootstraps: Gt:t+n = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + ... + γn−1Rt+n + γnV(St+n). n = 1 is TD(0), and n = ∞ is Monte Carlo. TD(λ) averages all n step returns with weights (1 − λ)λn−1, and eligibility traces compute this online, step by step.
Why we need it
An intermediate n often beats both ends: fewer steps of variance than MC, less reliance on wrong guesses than TD(0). Eligibility traces also solve the credit assignment problem neatly: each TD error is shared among recently visited states, more for more recent ones.
How it works
Try n between 2 and 10, or λ between 0.7 and 0.95. With traces: keep e(s) for every state, decay all traces by γλ each step, add 1 to the current state’s trace, and update every state by αδe(s).

TD(λ) with accumulating traces, one step
  1. Compute δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s).
  2. Increase the trace of the current state: e(s) ← e(s) + 1.
  3. For every state x: V(x) ← V(x) + αδe(x).
  4. Decay every trace: e(x) ← γλe(x).
  5. Move to s′. At the end of an episode reset all traces to 0.

Worked example: one TD update and one MC update
  1. V(s) = 0.5, the next state has V(s′) = 0.7, reward 0, γ = 1, α = 0.1.
  2. TD: δ = 0 + 0.7 − 0.5 = 0.2, so V(s) = 0.5 + 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.52
  3. Suppose the episode later ends on the right, so the return from s is G = 1.
  4. MC: V(s) = 0.5 + 0.1 × (1 − 0.5) = 0.55
  5. TD moved towards its neighbour’s guess at once. MC waited and moved towards what really happened.
Going deeper: Why TD converges, and when it can fail

For a fixed policy and a table of values, TD(0) converges to vπ with probability 1 if every state is visited infinitely often and the step sizes meet the Robbins Monro conditions (Sutton 1988, Dayan 1992, Jaakkola, Jordan and Singh 1994). With linear function approximation and on policy data, TD converges to a point close to the best linear fit (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, 1997). The danger appears when three things combine, which Sutton and Barto call the deadly triad: function approximation, bootstrapping and off policy training. Then values can diverge, which is why DQN needs extra tricks such as target networks (chapter 3.1).

Chapter summary

  1. Monte Carlo averages real returns after episodes end. No model, no bias, high variance.
  2. TD(0) updates every step towards r + γV(s′). The TD error δ drives learning. It bootstraps.
  3. TD usually learns faster on Markov problems because its targets vary less. Step size matters as much as the method.
  4. On fixed data, MC fits observed returns, batch TD fits the most likely Markov model (V(A) = 0 against 0.75).
  5. n step TD and TD(λ) sit between TD(0) and MC. Eligibility traces share each TD error with recent states.
  6. Function approximation + bootstrapping + off policy data (the deadly triad) can diverge.
