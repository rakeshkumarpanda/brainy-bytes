Lesson 1 of 10

What makes a point an anomaly

Source: Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar, Anomaly Detection: A Survey (ACM Computing Surveys 2009), Iglewicz and Hoaglin, How to Detect and Handle Outliers (1993), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 2.7.

What it is
An anomaly (or outlier) is a data point that does not fit the pattern of the rest. Anomaly detection finds such points, usually without labels, by learning what normal looks like and scoring how far each point is from it. Simple rules use robust statistics: the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD), or the interquartile range (IQR).
Why we need it
Fraud, machine faults, network attacks, data entry errors and rare diseases are all anomalies. They are rare, so there are few or no labelled examples, and they cost a lot when missed. Cleaning anomalies out of training data also makes other models better.
How it works
For one feature, flag values more than 3.5 robust units from the median, or outside Q1 − 1.5 IQR and Q3 + 1.5 IQR. The mean and standard deviation are themselves pulled by outliers, so z scores can hide the very points you want to find. For many features, use the model based methods in the next lessons.

In the exam
Two typing mistakes (900 and 950 orders on days that usually see about 50) drag the mean to 185 and the standard deviation to 316. Their z scores are only 2.26 and 2.42, so a |z| > 3 rule misses both. This is called masking. The median (51) and MAD (4.45) barely move, so the robust scores are about 190 and 200 and both days are flagged, as they are by the IQR fences 41.5 to 61.5.

(x − median) / (1.4826 × MAD)

Isolation Forest

Source: Liu, Ting and Zhou, Isolation Forest (ICDM 2008), and the scikit-learn 1.8 IsolationForest documentation.

What it is
Isolation Forest builds many random trees. Each tree picks a random feature and a random cut between its smallest and largest value, again and again. Anomalies are few and far away, so they get isolated in few cuts. The average path length over the trees becomes an anomaly score.
Why we need it
It needs no distances or densities, so it is fast (linear in the number of points), works in many dimensions, and scales to millions of rows. It is often the first method to try on tabular data.
How it works
IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, contamination=..., random_state=0).fit(X). score_samples gives a score where lower is more anomalous, and predict returns −1 for anomalies using the contamination share as the cut off. Each tree sees a small sample (256 rows by default), which actually helps.

In the exam
With 100 values, the far value 95 is isolated after 1.39 random cuts on average, while a normal value needs about 10. Turning path length into a score with s = 2−h/c(n) gives 0.89 for the far value and 0.44 for the normal one. Scores near 1 are anomalies, and scores below 0.5 are normal.

Isolation scoreExam favourite
s(x, n) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(n), c(n) = 2(ln(n − 1) + 0.5772) − 2(n − 1)/n
h = path length, c(n) = average path length in a random tree.

Local outlier factor: anomalies relative to their neighbourhood

Source: Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander, LOF: Identifying Density-Based Local Outliers (SIGMOD 2000).

What it is
The local outlier factor (LOF) compares how crowded a point’s neighbourhood is with how crowded its neighbours’ neighbourhoods are. A point in a loose group is fine if its neighbours are just as loose. A point a little outside a very tight group is flagged, because its neighbours are much more crowded than it is.
Why we need it
Real data mixes dense and sparse regions. A single global distance or density threshold either flags the whole sparse region or misses points just outside dense ones. LOF adapts to the local density.
How it works
LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=20).fit_predict(X) labels the training points. To score new points, create it with novelty=True and use score_samples or predict. LOF near 1 is normal and well above 1 is an outlier.

In the exam
A point at distance 1.2 from a group of spread 0.2 is very unusual locally, but it is still much closer to the other points than the edges of the loose group are. LOF ranks it as the single most unusual of 401 points, with an LOF of 4.77 against a typical 1.04. Isolation Forest, which looks at global isolation, ranks it only 13th.

One class SVM, robust covariance and a fair comparison

Source: Scholkopf and others, Estimating the Support of a High-Dimensional Distribution (2001), Rousseeuw and Van Driessen, A Fast Algorithm for the Minimum Covariance Determinant Estimator (1999), and the scikit-learn 1.8 novelty and outlier detection guide.

What it is
A one class SVM draws a boundary around the normal data in a kernel space, with nu as the upper bound on the share of training points left outside. The elliptic envelope fits one robust Gaussian (minimum covariance determinant) and flags points with a large Mahalanobis distance. A Gaussian mixture scores points by their log density.
Why we need it
Different methods assume different shapes of normal data. Knowing them lets you choose: one ellipse, several blobs, any shape. Outlier detection fits on data that already contains anomalies, while novelty detection fits on clean normal data and scores new points.
How it works
Scale the features first. Fit on normal data where you can, score a labelled test set, and compare methods with ROC AUC and especially PR AUC, which is honest when anomalies are rare.

In the exam
The normal data is two round blobs. The Gaussian mixture, one class SVM and LOF all score almost perfectly, because they can model two groups. The elliptic envelope assumes one ellipse around everything, so the empty space between the blobs looks normal to it and its PR AUC drops to 0.92. Isolation Forest is close behind at 0.91. Evaluating detectors when anomalies are rare

What it is
With 1% anomalies, a detector that flags nothing is 99% accurate, so accuracy is useless. Use the precision recall curve and its area (PR AUC, also called average precision), precision at k (how many of the top k flagged are real), and recall at a fixed review budget.
Why we need it
In practice a team can review only so many alerts a day. Precision at that number of alerts is what decides whether the detector is useful. ROC AUC can look excellent while most alerts are still false.
How it works
Collect even a small labelled set of anomalies for evaluation. Choose the alert threshold from the review budget or the cost of a miss against a false alarm, not from a default. Watch out for drift: normal behaviour changes over time, so refit regularly.

Worked example: the Isolation Forest normaliser for 256 points
  1. c(n) = 2(ln(n − 1) + γ) − 2(n − 1)/n with γ = 0.5772.
  2. ln 255 = 5.541, so 2(5.541 + 0.577) = 12.237.
  3. 2 × 255 / 256 = 1.992.
  4. c(256) = 12.237 − 1.992 = 10.245
  5. A point isolated in 5 cuts on average scores 2−5/10.245 = 0.71, so it looks anomalous.
Going deeper: Why 1.4826, and why isolation works

For normally distributed data, the median absolute deviation equals σ × Φ−1(0.75) = 0.6745 σ, so dividing by 0.6745 (multiplying by 1.4826) makes MAD an estimate of σ that ignores up to half the data being outliers: a breakdown point of 50%, against 0% for the standard deviation. Isolation Forest relies on two facts: anomalies are few, and they have unusual values on some feature. A random cut between min and max lands in the large empty space next to an isolated point with high probability, so its expected depth grows much more slowly than the log n depth of typical points. c(n) is the average depth of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree, which normalises scores across sample sizes. Subsampling (256 rows) reduces masking and swamping, where groups of anomalies hide each other or make normal points look odd.

Chapter summary

  1. Anomalies are rare points that do not fit. Detection learns normal and scores distance from it, usually without labels.
  2. The mean and standard deviation are pulled by outliers (masking). Use the median and MAD or IQR fences for single features.
  3. Isolation Forest: random cuts isolate anomalies quickly. Score 2−E[h]/c(n), near 1 is anomalous. Fast and a good default.
  4. LOF compares a point’s local density with its neighbours’, so it handles regions of different density.
  5. One class SVM, elliptic envelope and GMMs assume different shapes of normal. Outlier detection fits on dirty data, novelty detection on clean data.
  6. Evaluate with PR AUC and precision at k, not accuracy, and choose the threshold from the alert budget.
