Lesson 1 of 9

Mixtures of bell curves and the EM algorithm

Source: Dempster, Laird and Rubin, the EM algorithm (1977), Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning chapter 9, The Elements of Statistical Learning section 8.5, and the Stanford CS229 notes on EM.

What it is
A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) says each data point came from one of K bell curves (Gaussians), chosen with probabilities called weights. We see the points but not which curve made each one. The EM algorithm (expectation maximisation) finds the means, spreads and weights that make the data most likely.
Why we need it
A GMM gives soft clustering: every point gets a probability for every cluster, which is more honest for points between groups. It also gives a full density, so you can score how unusual a point is and even generate new data.
How it works
Start with a guess. E step: for every point, compute the probability (the responsibility) that each curve produced it. M step: refit each curve as a weighted mean and spread, with the responsibilities as weights, and set each weight to its share of the responsibility. Repeat until the log likelihood stops rising.

In the exam
The first guess puts both means near the middle. Round by round the curves separate, and by round 9 they sit at 154.8 and 176.2, which are exactly the means of the shorter five and the taller five. The log likelihood rises every round, from −39.65 to −34.52, and then stays put. Responsibility (E step)Exam favourite

Real world scenario: sorting a bag of mixed apples by weight

A bag holds apples from two orchards, one with small apples and one with big ones, but nobody marked which is which. You guess an average weight for each orchard. For each apple you say how likely it came from each orchard. Then you recompute each orchard’s average, letting each apple count in proportion to how likely it is to belong there. After a few rounds the two averages stop changing.

Lesson 2 of 9

Gaussian mixtures in scikit-learn

What it is
In more than one dimension each component has a mean vector and a covariance matrix, which sets its shape and tilt. So the clusters are ellipses, not circles. scikit-learn’s GaussianMixture fits them with EM and gives predict for the most likely component and predict_proba for the probabilities.
Why we need it
k-means always draws straight boundaries halfway between centres and treats every cluster as a round ball of the same size. A GMM learns long, flat or tilted clusters of different sizes, which is much closer to many real datasets.
How it works
GaussianMixture(n_components=2, covariance_type="full", n_init=5, random_state=0).fit(X). EM finds a local maximum, so use n_init above 1, just as with k-means. Scale the features when their units differ.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 sklearn_gmm.py.
In the exam
The two clusters are long and flat, one above the other. k-means splits them left and right (ARI about 0), because that gives the smaller squared distances. A GMM with one start falls into the same trap. With 5 starts it finds the higher log likelihood (−3.787 against −4.045) and the right answer (ARI 0.941). A point halfway between the clusters gets about 50% for each, and a point inside the lower cluster gets 100%.
Lesson 3 of 9

Covariance types and choosing K

What it is
The covariance type limits the shape of each ellipse: spherical (round, one spread each), diag (stretched along the axes), tied (all components share one tilted shape), or full (each its own tilted shape). The BIC (Bayesian information criterion) balances fit against the number of parameters, and lower is better.
Why we need it
More flexible shapes always fit the training data better, so the likelihood alone always prefers more components and full covariances. BIC adds a penalty for every parameter, so you can choose both the covariance type and the number of components without a test set.
How it works
Fit each candidate and call gm.bic(X). BIC = p ln n − 2 ln L, where p is the number of parameters, n the number of points and L the likelihood. AIC = 2p − 2 ln L penalises less and tends to choose more components.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 covariance_types.py.
Remember
One cluster is tilted and one is tall. Full covariance needs the most parameters (11) but fits so much better that its BIC is the lowest by far (3835 against 4062 for tied). Spherical is the cheapest at 7 parameters but cannot follow either shape, so its BIC is the worst.

Lesson 4 of 9

Densities, anomalies, new samples and automatic K

What it is
A fitted GMM is a full probability density. score_samples gives the log density of any point, so very low values flag anomalies. sample draws new points from the model. BayesianGaussianMixture puts a prior on the weights so that unneeded components shrink towards zero weight.
Why we need it
Anomaly detection on sensor data, fraud checks and quality control all need "how normal is this point". A GMM answers that with a number. The Bayesian version saves you from choosing K exactly: give it more components than you need and it switches the extra ones off.
How it works
Fit on normal data, set a threshold at a low percentile of the training log densities, and flag new points below it. For automatic K, use BayesianGaussianMixture(n_components=8, weight_concentration_prior=0.01) and count the components with real weight.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 density_uses.py.
Remember
The threshold is the lowest 1% of the training log densities. A day with normal temperature and power use scores about −6.3. A day at 70 degrees scores −13.2 and a day with 320 units of power scores −25.5, both far below the threshold, so both are flagged. The model can also generate realistic new days.

In the exam
make_blobs made 3 blobs, but two of them overlap so much that they look like one long cluster. Of the 8 components allowed, only 2 keep a weight above 1%, and the others shrink to almost nothing. The prior chose K by itself and judged two overlapping blobs to be one group, which shows that automatic K still needs a check.

k-means and Gaussian mixtures
k-meansGaussian mixture
AssignmentHard: one cluster per pointSoft: a probability for every cluster
Cluster shapeRound, same sizeEllipses of any size and tilt (full covariance)
What it optimisesSum of squared distances (inertia)Log likelihood of the data
AlgorithmLloyd: assign, updateEM: E step, M step
OutputCentres and labelsMeans, covariances, weights and a density
Choosing KElbow, silhouetteBIC, AIC, Bayesian prior

Bottom line: use k-means for speed and round groups, and a GMM when clusters are elliptical, overlap, or when you need probabilities or a density.

In the exam
k-means is the limit of EM for a GMM whose components all share one round covariance σ2I, with σ shrinking to 0. Then each responsibility becomes 0 or 1, the E step becomes "nearest centre", and the M step becomes "mean of the points".

Worked example: one E step for one point
  1. Two components with weights 0.5 and 0.5. At the point x, their densities are 0.3 and 0.1.
  2. Weighted densities: 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15 and 0.5 × 0.1 = 0.05.
  3. Mixture density at x: 0.15 + 0.05 = 0.2.
  4. r1 = 0.15 / 0.2 = 0.75, r2 = 0.05 / 0.2 = 0.25
  5. The point counts as three quarters of a point for component 1 in the next M step.
Going deeper: Why EM never lowers the likelihood

For any distribution q over the hidden labels, Jensen’s inequality gives ln p(x | θ) ≥ Σz q(z) ln (p(x, z | θ) / q(z)), a lower bound called the ELBO. The gap is the KL divergence between q and the true posterior p(z | x, θ). The E step sets q to that posterior, so the gap is zero and the bound touches ln p. The M step raises the bound by changing θ, and since the bound sits below ln p, ln p rises at least as much. So the likelihood never falls, and EM converges to a stationary point, usually a local maximum. A danger peculiar to GMMs is a component that shrinks onto a single point: its variance goes to 0 and the likelihood to infinity. scikit-learn prevents this by adding reg_covar (1e−6 by default) to the diagonal of every covariance.

Chapter summary

  1. A Gaussian mixture models the data as K Gaussians with weights πk, means μk and covariances Σk.
  2. EM alternates the E step (responsibilities rik by Bayes rule) and the M step (weighted counts, means and covariances). The log likelihood never falls.
  3. GMMs give soft assignments and elliptical clusters. k-means is the hard, round limit.
  4. EM finds local maxima: use n_init above 1. Choose K and covariance_type with BIC.
  5. A GMM is a density: score_samples flags anomalies and sample generates new data.
  6. BayesianGaussianMixture switches off unneeded components, but check its choice.
