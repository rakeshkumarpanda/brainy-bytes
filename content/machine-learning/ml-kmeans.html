Lesson 1 of 10

Finding groups without labels

Source: Lloyd (1957), MacQueen (1967), Arthur and Vassilvitskii, k-means++ (2007), Sculley, Web-Scale K-Means (2010), Rousseeuw, Silhouettes (1987), The Elements of Statistical Learning section 14.3, and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 2.3.

What it is
Clustering splits data into groups of similar points without any labels. k-means is the simplest and most used method: it finds k centres and gives every point to its nearest centre, so that the total squared distance from points to their centres (the inertia) is as small as possible.
Why we need it
Most data has no labels. Clustering finds structure on its own: customer segments for marketing, groups of similar documents, colours for image compression, or a first look at a new dataset. k-means is fast, simple and scales to millions of points.
How it works
Lloyd’s algorithm: choose k starting centres, then repeat two steps until nothing changes. Assign each point to its nearest centre. Update each centre to the mean of its points. Each step can only lower the inertia, so it always stops, but possibly in a local minimum.

Update stepExam favourite

The mean minimises the squared distances.
Real world scenario: placing ice cream vans on a beach

Three ice cream vans want to park so that every sunbather walks as little as possible. Each sunbather goes to the nearest van. Then each van moves to the middle of its own customers. After a few rounds of walking and moving, nobody changes van any more. That is k-means.

k-means in scikit-learn

What it is
KMeans in scikit-learn runs Lloyd’s algorithm several times from different starts (n_init) and keeps the run with the lowest inertia. It stores the centres in cluster_centers_, the labels in labels_, and can assign new points with predict.
Why we need it
Clustering is often a step in a bigger system: segment customers once, then put each new customer into a segment. predict does exactly that, using the nearest centre.
How it works
KMeans(n_clusters=4, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X). Scale features first (StandardScaler) so that each feature counts equally in the distance.

Lesson 3 of 10

Choosing k: elbow and silhouette

What it is
k-means needs k in advance. The elbow method plots inertia against k and looks for the bend after which extra clusters help little. The silhouette score compares, for each point, the mean distance to its own cluster (a) with the mean distance to the nearest other cluster (b).
Why we need it
Inertia always falls as k grows (with k = n it is zero), so the lowest inertia is never the answer. The silhouette rewards tight, well separated clusters and has a clear best value.
How it works
Silhouette of a point = (b − a) / max(a, b), between −1 and 1. silhouette_score(X, labels) averages it. Try a range of k, look at both measures, then check the clusters make sense for the purpose.

Lesson 4 of 10

Starting centres and k-means++

What it is
k-means++ chooses starting centres that are spread out: the first at random, then each next one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre already chosen.
Why we need it
Random starts often place two centres in the same group and leave another group with none, giving a bad local minimum. k-means++ makes that much less likely and comes with a guarantee: its expected inertia is within a factor of O(log k) of the best possible.
How it works
k-means++ is the default in scikit-learn (init="k-means++"). Keep n_init above 1 so several starts are tried. MiniBatchKMeans updates the centres from small random batches and is much faster on large data, for a tiny loss in inertia.

Lesson 5 of 10

When k-means fails

What it is
k-means assumes clusters are round blobs of similar size, separated by straight boundaries halfway between centres. It fails on long, curved or nested shapes, on clusters of very different sizes or densities, and it is pulled around by outliers.
Why we need it
Knowing the assumptions tells you when to switch method. Because every point goes to its nearest centre, the boundaries between clusters are always straight lines (a Voronoi diagram), whatever the true shape.
How it works
Check the clusters visually (after PCA if there are many features), compare with other methods, and switch to DBSCAN for odd shapes and noise, hierarchical clustering for nested groups, or Gaussian mixtures for elliptical clusters of different sizes. These are the next two chapters.

The maths, all in one place

ObjectiveExam favourite
J(c, μ) = Σi |xi − μc(i)|2
Minimised over assignments c and centres μ.
Assign stepExam favourite
c(i) = argmink |xi − μk|2
Best c for fixed μ.
Update stepExam favourite
μk = mean of the points with c(i) = k
Best μ for fixed c.
k-means++ seedingExam favourite
P(choose x) = D(x)2 / Σ D(x′)2
D(x) = distance to the nearest chosen centre.
Silhouette
s = (b − a)/max(a, b)
Average over points.
Worked example: one k-means++ choice
  1. One centre is already chosen. Three remaining points have squared distances 1, 4 and 5 to it.
  2. The total is 1 + 4 + 5 = 10.
  3. Each point is chosen with probability D2 / 10.
  4. P(third point) = 5 / 10 = 0.5
  5. Far points are likely, so the next centre usually lands in a group that has none yet.
Going deeper: Why k-means always stops, and how hard it really is

Each assign step chooses the best c for the current centres and each update step the best centres for the current c, so J never increases. There are finitely many ways to assign n points to k clusters, so the algorithm must stop. This is coordinate descent, and it is also the limit of EM for a Gaussian mixture with equal, shrinking variances (next but one chapter). Finding the global minimum is NP hard, even for k = 2 in general dimension (Aloise and others, 2009), and in the worst case Lloyd’s algorithm can need exponentially many rounds (Vattani, 2011), although on real data it converges in a few dozen. k-means++ guarantees an expected J within 8(ln k + 2) of the optimum.

Chapter summary

  1. k-means minimises the inertia Σ|x − μc(x)|2 by repeating assign (nearest centre) and update (mean of the points).
  2. It always converges, but to a local minimum. Use k-means++ starts and n_init > 1.
  3. Scale the features first, or the feature with the biggest units decides everything.
  4. Choose k with the elbow of the inertia curve and the silhouette score, then check the purpose.
  5. k-means expects round clusters of similar size and is hurt by outliers. Boundaries are always straight.
  6. MiniBatchKMeans is much faster on big data with almost the same inertia.
