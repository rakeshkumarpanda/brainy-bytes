The idea: keep the directions where data spreads most

Source: Pearson (1901), Hotelling (1933), Jolliffe, Principal Component Analysis (2002), and The Elements of Statistical Learning section 14.5.

What it is
Principal component analysis (PCA) finds new axes, called principal components, that point along the directions where the data varies most. The first component has the most variance, the second has the most of what is left and is at right angles to the first, and so on. Keeping only the first few components describes each point with fewer numbers.
Why we need it
Many datasets have dozens or thousands of features that move together. PCA removes that overlap: it compresses data, speeds up later models, removes some noise, and lets you plot high dimensional data in 2D. It is the most used dimensionality reduction method.
How it works
Centre each feature at 0. Compute the covariance matrix. Its eigenvectors are the components and its eigenvalues are the variances along them. Sort by eigenvalue, keep the top k eigenvectors, and project the data onto them.

In the exam
Hours and scores rise together, so almost all the variation lies along one line. The first component keeps 99.97% of the variance, so each student can be described by one number instead of two with almost no loss. The direction (0.16, 0.99) is mostly along the score axis, because scores vary in tens of points and hours only in single hours. The scaling lesson comes back to this.
Covariance matrixExam favourite
C = XcT Xc / (n − 1)
Xc is the centred data, n rows.
Eigen equationExam favourite
C v = λ v
v is a component direction, λ the variance along it.
ProjectionExam favourite
Z = Xc Vk
Vk holds the top k eigenvectors as columns.
Real world scenario: taking the best photo of a statue

A photo turns a 3D statue into a 2D picture, so something is always lost. A good photographer walks around until the statue looks widest and most recognisable. PCA does the same with data: among all the ways to flatten it, it picks the view that keeps the points as spread out as possible.

PCA in scikit-learn and the SVD

What it is
scikit-learn’s PCA centres the data and computes the components with the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the centred data, which is more accurate than forming the covariance matrix. It stores the directions in components_, the variances in explained_variance_ and their shares in explained_variance_ratio_.
Why we need it
The SVD Xc = U S VT gives the components directly as the rows of VT, and the variances as S2 / (n − 1). inverse_transform maps the reduced data back to the original features, so you can see exactly what was lost.
How it works
PCA(n_components=2).fit(X), then transform(X) for the new coordinates and inverse_transform for the reconstruction. Signs of components are arbitrary: a component and its negative describe the same axis.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 pca_sklearn.py.
Remember
The SVD gives the same variances as the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix (133.328 and 0.038). Rebuilt from one number each, the points move only slightly onto the line: 4 hours becomes 3.7 and 6 hours becomes 5.9, while the scores stay almost exactly as they were.

How many components to keep

What it is
The explained variance ratio of a component is its eigenvalue divided by the sum of all eigenvalues. A scree plot shows these ratios in order, and the cumulative sum shows how much the first k components keep together.
Why we need it
Too few components lose real structure, and too many keep noise and save little. The choice depends on the purpose: 2 or 3 for a picture, a variance target such as 95% for compression, or whatever gives the best score for a model that follows.
How it works
Look for the elbow where the bars flatten, or pass a fraction: PCA(n_components=0.95) keeps the fewest components that reach 95%. When PCA feeds a model, tune n_components with cross validation like any other setting.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 digits_variance.py.
In the exam
Each digit image has 64 pixel values. The first 2 components keep only 28.5% of the variance, which is enough for a rough picture but not for detail. 21 components keep 90% and 29 keep 95%, so more than half of the 64 numbers are close to redundant.

Scaling, pipelines and pitfalls

What it is
PCA looks for the largest variance, so a feature measured in big units wins even when it is not important. Standardise features first unless they share units. Inside a model, put PCA in a pipeline so that it is fitted only on the training folds.
Why we need it
Unscaled PCA on mixed units mostly rediscovers the feature with the biggest numbers. Fitting PCA on all the data before splitting leaks information from the test set. Both mistakes are common in exams and in practice.
How it works
make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), PCA(n_components=k), model). Remember that PCA ignores the labels: the directions of most variance are not always the ones that separate the classes best.

In the exam
In the wine data, proline goes up to 1680 while hue stays below 1.71. Unscaled, the first component explains 99.8% of the variance, but almost all of that is proline, which tells you nothing new. After scaling, the first component explains 36.2% and is led by flavanoids and total phenols, a real combination of related features.

PCA here trades a little accuracy for a smaller, faster model. It does not always help accuracy, because the label is not used to choose the directions.
Lesson 5 of 10

Kernel PCA and PCA at scale

Source: Scholkopf, Smola and Muller, Nonlinear Component Analysis as a Kernel Eigenvalue Problem (1998), Halko, Martinsson and Tropp, Finding Structure with Randomness (SIAM Review 2011), and the scikit-learn 1.8 decomposition documentation.

What it is
PCA finds only straight directions. Kernel PCA first maps the data into a richer space through a kernel, as an SVM does, and runs PCA there, so it can unfold curved structure. For very large data, randomized PCA finds the top components quickly and IncrementalPCA fits in batches that fit in memory.
Why we need it
Data on circles, spirals or curved surfaces has no good straight direction. Kernel PCA can turn such structure into a few useful numbers. On millions of rows or features, the exact SVD is too slow, and the approximate methods are almost as accurate.
How it works
KernelPCA(n_components=2, kernel="rbf", gamma=...) with gamma tuned. PCA(svd_solver="randomized") for big dense data, IncrementalPCA(batch_size=...) for data that does not fit in memory, and TruncatedSVD for sparse matrices such as text.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 kernel_pca.py.
In the exam
Two circles, one inside the other, cannot be separated by any straight direction, so one PCA component gives 45% accuracy, no better than guessing. Kernel PCA with gamma 2 turns them into one number that separates them perfectly. With gamma 15 the kernel is too narrow and accuracy drops to 71%, so gamma needs tuning.

The maths, all in one place

First componentExam favourite
v1 = argmax|v| = 1 vT C v
The unit direction with the largest variance.
SolutionExam favourite
C v1 = λ1 v1, variance = λ1
The top eigenvector and its eigenvalue.
Explained variance ratioExam favourite
λj / Σi λi
Share of the total variance.
SVD routeExam favourite
Xc = U S VT, λj = sj2 / (n − 1)
Components are the rows of VT.
Reconstruction
X̂ = Z VkT + mean
Map back to the original features.
Reconstruction errorExam favourite
(1/(n − 1)) |Xc − Xc Vk VkT|2 = Σj > k λj
The dropped variance.
Worked example: PCA of a 2 by 2 covariance matrix
  1. C = [[3, 1], [1, 3]].
  2. Eigenvalues solve (3 − λ)2 − 1 = 0, so 3 − λ = ±1.
  3. λ1 = 4, λ2 = 2
  4. For λ = 4 the eigenvector is (1, 1) / √2, the diagonal direction.
  5. Component 1 keeps 4 / (4 + 2) = 2/3 of the variance.
Going deeper: Two views that give the same answer

Pearson in 1901 asked for the line that is closest to the points, minimising the sum of squared perpendicular distances. Hotelling in 1933 asked for the direction of largest variance. For centred data they are the same: each point’s squared distance from the origin splits into the squared length of its projection plus its squared distance to the line, and the total is fixed. So maximising the projected variance is the same as minimising the squared distances. The Eckart and Young theorem (1936) goes further: keeping the top k singular values gives the best rank k approximation of the data in squared error. PCA is optimal among linear projections, and only among those.

Chapter summary

  1. PCA finds orthogonal directions of decreasing variance: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, with the eigenvalues as their variances.
  2. Centre the data, and standardise it when the features have different units.
  3. scikit-learn uses the SVD: λ = s2 / (n − 1). Signs of components are arbitrary.
  4. Choose k with the scree plot, a variance target such as PCA(0.95), or cross validation of the next model.
  5. The reconstruction error equals the sum of the dropped eigenvalues. PCA is the best linear projection for squared error.
  6. PCA ignores labels and sees only straight directions. Kernel PCA handles curves, and randomized or incremental PCA handles big data.
