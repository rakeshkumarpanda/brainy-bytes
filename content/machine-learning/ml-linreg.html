Lesson 1 of 11

Linear regression: predict a number with a straight line

Source: James, Witten, Hastie and Tibshirani, An Introduction to Statistical Learning chapter 3, Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning chapter 3.2, the Stanford CS229 notes, and the scikit-learn 1.8 and statsmodels 0.14 references.

What it is
Linear regression predicts a number (house price, exam score, sales) as a weighted sum of the features plus a constant: ŷ = b0 + b1x1 + ... + bdxd. With one feature it is a straight line. Least squares chooses the weights that make the sum of squared errors as small as possible.
Why we need it
It is the first model to try for any number prediction. It trains in a blink, it is easy to explain ("each extra square metre adds 0.82 lakh"), and it is the base of logistic regression, neural networks and much of statistics. A fancier model must beat it to be worth the trouble.
How it works
For one feature, the best slope is the covariance of x and y divided by the variance of x, and the line passes through the mean point. For many features, the normal equation solves for all weights at once, or gradient descent walks to them step by step. Simple linear regressionExam favourite
b1 = Σ(xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / Σ(xi − x̄)2, b0 = ȳ − b1x̄
The slope is cov(x, y) / var(x).
Least squares lossExam favourite
L(b) = Σ (yi − ŷi)2
Squaring makes every error positive and punishes big errors more.
Real world scenario: a taxi fare

A taxi charges a fixed 50 rupees plus 15 rupees per kilometre. Fare = 50 + 15 × km is a linear model: 50 is the intercept and 15 the slope. Linear regression does the reverse. It looks at many past trips and their fares, and works out the 50 and the 15.

Lesson 2 of 11

Many features and the normal equation

What it is
With several features, the model is ŷ = Xw, where X has one row per example (plus a column of 1s for the intercept) and w holds the weights. The normal equation w = (XTX)−1XTy gives the exact least squares weights in one step.
Why we need it
Real targets depend on many things at once. Each weight then says how much ŷ changes when that one feature goes up by 1 while the others stay fixed, which is very useful for explaining a model.
How it works
Set the gradient of the squared error to zero: XT(Xw − y) = 0. Rearranging gives the normal equation. In code, solve the linear system with np.linalg.solve (or lstsq) instead of computing the inverse, which is slower and less accurate. scikit-learn’s LinearRegression does this for you.

Remember
Read a weight as "holding the other features fixed". Above, the rooms weight is 3.7 lakh per extra room for a flat of the same size and age. If two features are strongly correlated (size and rooms), their separate weights become unstable and hard to read. This is called multicollinearity, and ridge regression (next chapter) fixes it.
Lesson 3 of 11

Fit, predict and measure on real data

What it is
The scikit-learn workflow is always the same: split the data, fit on the training part, predict on the test part, and measure the error with MAE, RMSE and R².
Why we need it
The training error always looks better than reality, because the model has seen those rows. Only the test error tells you how the model will do on new data.
How it works
MAE is the average size of the errors, in the same unit as y. RMSE squares the errors before averaging, so big misses count more. R² says what share of the variation in y the model explains: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than always predicting the mean, and it can even be negative on test data.

Lesson 4 of 11

Gradient descent for linear regression

What it is
Instead of the normal equation, you can find the weights with gradient descent: start somewhere, compute the slope of the mean squared error for each weight, and step downhill. SGDRegressor does this with mini-batches of one row.
Why we need it
The normal equation becomes slow with very many features (it needs a d × d system) and needs all the data in memory. Gradient descent works on huge or streaming data, and it is exactly how neural networks are trained, so it is worth seeing on the simplest model first.
How it works
For the MSE loss the gradients are ∂L/∂w = (2/n) Σ(ŷ − y)x and ∂L/∂b = (2/n) Σ(ŷ − y). Scale the features first so one learning rate suits every weight. The MSE of a linear model is a convex bowl, so gradient descent reaches the same answer as the normal equation.

Lesson 5 of 11

Assumptions and residual checks

What it is
Linear regression assumes: the relation is roughly a straight line (linearity), the errors are independent, the spread of the errors is the same everywhere (homoscedasticity), and, for p values and intervals, the errors are roughly normal.
Why we need it
If the assumptions break, predictions can still be fine, but the weights, p values and confidence intervals can mislead you. Residuals (y − ŷ) show which assumption is broken.
How it works
After fitting, look at the residuals. Their mean should be 0. Their spread should not grow with x or with ŷ. A curve in the residuals means the relation is not linear, so add polynomial terms or a log. Growing spread (heteroscedasticity) suggests a log of y or robust standard errors.

Lesson 6 of 11

Outliers and robust regression

What it is
Because errors are squared, one far point can pull the least squares line badly. Huber regression uses squared error for small errors and absolute error for big ones, so outliers count much less.
Why we need it
Real data has typing mistakes and rare events. A model fitted mostly to one bad row predicts badly for everyone else.
How it works
Huber loss is (1/2)e2 when |e| ≤ δ and δ(|e| − δ/2) when |e| > δ. Small errors behave like least squares, big ones grow only linearly. In the program one wrong value moves the least squares slope from 2 to 4.13, while Huber stays at 2.

Lesson 7 of 11

Reading weights like a statistician

What it is
statsmodels fits the same least squares line but also reports, for each weight, a standard error, a p value and a confidence interval, plus adjusted R².
Why we need it
In science, business and medicine you often need to know whether a feature really matters, not just a prediction. A p value tests "is this weight really zero?", and the interval gives the range of believable values.
How it works
Fit sm.OLS on X with a constant column. A small p value (below 0.05) means the weight is unlikely to be zero. In the program the ads weight is clearly real (interval 2.36 to 2.79, true value 2.5), while the random feature has p = 0.09, so we cannot say it matters.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 statsmodels_ols.py.
Adjusted R²Exam favourite
R²adj = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − d − 1)
Plain R² never falls when you add a feature. The adjusted version does if the feature does not help enough.
In the exam
A feature can be statistically significant and still useless for prediction, or very useful for prediction while its p value is unclear because it is correlated with another feature. Prediction (machine learning) and inference (statistics) answer different questions.

Worked example: the line by hand
  1. x = 1 to 6 (hours), y = 52, 55, 61, 64, 70, 72. x̄ = 3.5, ȳ = 62.333.
  2. x − x̄ = −2.5, −1.5, −0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5. Their squares add to 17.5.
  3. Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) = 74.0. So b1 = 74.0 / 17.5 = 4.229.
  4. b0 = 62.333 − 4.229 × 3.5 = 47.533
  5. Prediction for 7 hours: 47.533 + 4.229 × 7 = 77.13, as the program prints.
Going deeper: Least squares as a projection, and maximum likelihood

Xw can only reach the column space of X. Least squares picks the point in that space closest to y, the orthogonal projection ŷ = X(XTX)−1XTy = Hy, so the residual vector is perpendicular to every column of X (that is the normal equation). If the errors are independent and normal with constant variance, least squares is also the maximum likelihood estimate (Foundations 2.3), and by the Gauss Markov theorem it has the smallest variance among all unbiased linear estimators even without normality.

From beginner to expert

One idea, four depths

Linear regression draws the straight line that best fits the points, then uses it to predict new numbers. "Best" means the smallest total of squared errors.

Fit LinearRegression on several features, read the weights, evaluate on a test set with MAE, RMSE and R², and know the normal equation and the gradient descent version.

Check assumptions with residuals, handle outliers with Huber, read p values and intervals with statsmodels, use adjusted R², and spot multicollinearity.

Understand least squares as a projection with the hat matrix, the Gauss Markov theorem, the bias and variance of the estimator (variance σ2(XTX)−1), leverage and Cook’s distance, and generalised linear models that extend the idea to counts and probabilities.

Beginner
Fit a line to points and use it to predict.
Intermediate
Train and evaluate a multi feature regression and explain its weights.
Expert
Diagnose residuals, choose robust losses, and reason about the estimator with linear algebra.

Chapter summary

  1. Linear regression predicts ŷ = b0 + b1x1 + ... and picks the weights with the smallest sum of squared errors.
  2. One feature: b1 = cov(x, y) / var(x), and the line passes through (x̄, ȳ).
  3. Many features: the normal equation w = (XTX)−1XTy, or gradient descent on scaled features.
  4. Measure on test data: MAE (average miss), RMSE (punishes big misses), R² = 1 − SSE/SST.
  5. Check residuals for curves (not linear) and funnels (heteroscedasticity).
  6. One outlier can pull the line. Huber regression is robust.
  7. statsmodels gives p values and confidence intervals for each weight. Adjusted R² punishes useless features.
