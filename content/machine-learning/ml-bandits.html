Lesson 1 of 10

The bandit problem and running averages

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) sections 2.1 to 2.5.

What it is
A multi armed bandit is the simplest RL problem: there is only one state. You face k slot machines (arms), each paying rewards from its own unknown distribution. Each round you pull one arm and see its reward. The goal is the largest total reward, which means finding the best arm while losing as little as possible on the way.
Why we need it
It isolates the explore or exploit trade off with nothing else in the way. Real uses are everywhere: which ad or headline to show, which price to test, which treatment to give in an adaptive clinical trial, which server answers fastest.
How it works
Keep an estimate Q(a) of each arm’s mean reward and the count N(a) of pulls. After each pull update the estimate with the running average Q ← Q + (r − Q) / N, which needs no list of old rewards. Then choose the next arm with a rule that balances using high estimates and testing uncertain ones.

The running average gives 4, 5, 5, 6, 5, exactly the plain average at each point, without storing the rewards. A constant step size suits arms whose payouts drift over time.

Greedy and epsilon greedy

Source: Sutton and Barto (2018) section 2.3, the 10 armed testbed.

What it is
Greedy always pulls the arm with the highest estimate. ε greedy does the same most of the time but, with a small chance ε, pulls an arm at random.
Why we need it
Greedy can lock onto a worse arm forever after one lucky early reward, because it never tests the others again. A little random exploration keeps every estimate improving, so the best arm is found eventually.
How it works
Pick ε between 0.01 and 0.1 for a start. Lower ε wastes less once the best arm is known but finds it more slowly. A common improvement is to lower ε over time, or to start all estimates optimistically high so that every arm gets tried early.

Lesson 3 of 10

Upper confidence bounds: optimism under uncertainty

Source: Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer, Finite-time Analysis of the Multiarmed Bandit Problem (2002), and Lai and Robbins (1985).

What it is
UCB scores each arm by its estimate plus a bonus for uncertainty: Q(a) + c √(ln t / N(a)). It pulls the arm with the highest score. An arm that has been pulled rarely has a big bonus, so it gets tried. As N(a) grows the bonus shrinks and the score settles on the true mean.
Why we need it
Unlike ε greedy, UCB explores where it matters: it stops testing arms that are clearly bad and keeps testing arms that might still be best. Its regret grows only like log T, which Lai and Robbins proved is the best possible order.
How it works
Pull every arm once, then always pull the arm with the highest Q + bonus. Tune c: about √2 is the classic choice for rewards between 0 and 1, and noisy rewards may need another value. Too large a c explores too much, too small acts greedy.

UCB is better in principle, yet its bonus size still has to fit the noise in the rewards.

Lesson 4 of 10

Thompson sampling and A/B tests

Source: Thompson (1933), Chapelle and Li, An Empirical Evaluation of Thompson Sampling (NeurIPS 2011), and Russo and colleagues, A Tutorial on Thompson Sampling (2018).

What it is
Thompson sampling keeps a probability distribution of belief about each arm’s mean. For click or no click rewards this is a Beta(wins + 1, losses + 1) curve. Each round it draws one random value from every arm’s curve and pulls the arm with the highest draw. The chance an arm is pulled equals the chance it is the best, given the data so far.
Why we need it
It is simple, needs no bonus size, and in practice often matches or beats UCB. Companies use it to choose between web page designs, ads and recommendations, because it moves traffic to the winner while the test is still running, instead of wasting half the visitors on losers as a fixed A/B test does.
How it works
Start every arm at Beta(1, 1), the flat curve. After a click add 1 to wins, after no click add 1 to losses. Each round draw, pick the largest, show, update. For rewards that are not 0 or 1, use other prior and posterior pairs such as normal curves.

In the exam
Over 5,000 visitors and 200 repeats, Thompson sampling earned 323 clicks against 305.3 for an A/B test that split the first half of visitors evenly and then showed the winner. Thompson showed the worst design only 354 times on average, against 844 in the A/B test. The best possible was 350. The A/B test gives a cleaner statistical answer, Thompson gives more clicks while learning.

Lesson 5 of 10

Contextual bandits and where bandits fit

Source: Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire, A Contextual-Bandit Approach to Personalized News Article Recommendation (WWW 2010).

What it is
A contextual bandit sees some features (the context) before each choice, such as the user’s device, time and history, and learns which arm is best for each context. LinUCB models each arm’s expected reward as a linear function of the context and adds a UCB style bonus for uncertainty.
Why we need it
The best headline for a phone user at night may differ from the best one for a desktop user at noon. Contextual bandits personalise, yet stay simpler than full RL because one choice does not change the next context.
How it works
Plain bandit: one state, no context. Contextual bandit: a context each round, but actions do not affect the future. Full RL: actions change the next state. Choose the simplest one that fits the problem.
LinUCB in four lines
  1. For each arm a keep Aa = I (a d by d matrix) and ba = 0 (a vector), where d is the number of context features.
  2. For context x, estimate θa = Aa−1 ba and score pa = θa · x + α √(xT Aa−1 x).
  3. Pull the arm with the highest score and observe reward r.
  4. Update that arm: Aa ← Aa + x xT and ba ← ba + r x.

The maths, all in one place

Action valueExam favourite
q*(a) = E[Rt | At = a]
The true mean of arm a.
Sample average
Qt(a) = (sum of rewards from a) / Nt(a)
Converges to q*(a) by the law of large numbers.
Incremental formExam favourite
Q ← Q + (1/N)(r − Q)
Any step size αn with Σ αn = ∞ and Σ αn2 < ∞ converges.
ε greedy choice
P(greedy arm) = 1 − ε + ε/k
The random pick can also land on it.
UCBExam favourite
argmaxa Q(a) + c √(ln t / N(a))
Regret O(log T).
Beta posteriorExam favourite
Beta(1, 1) + s clicks and f misses ⇒ Beta(1 + s, 1 + f), mean (1 + s)/(2 + s + f)
Conjugate to the Bernoulli.
Lai and Robbins bound
regret(T) ≥ (Σa: Δa>0 Δa / KL(a, a*)) ln T, as T grows
No rule can beat log T.
Worked example: one UCB decision
  1. Round t = 100. Arm A: Q = 0.6 after 25 pulls. Arm B: Q = 0.5 after 4 pulls. c = 1.
  2. ln 100 = 4.605.
  3. Arm A: 0.6 + √(4.605 / 25) = 0.6 + 0.429 = 1.029
  4. Arm B: 0.5 + √(4.605 / 4) = 0.5 + 1.073 = 1.573
  5. UCB pulls arm B: its estimate is lower but much less certain.
Going deeper: Why UCB’s regret is logarithmic

Hoeffding’s inequality says that after N pulls the sample mean is more than u below the true mean with chance at most e−2Nu2. Choosing u = √(2 ln t / N) makes that chance at most t−4, so with high probability every arm’s score is above its true mean. A worse arm with gap Δ is then pulled only while its bonus exceeds Δ, that is while N < 8 ln t / Δ2. Summing over arms gives regret at most about Σ 8 ln T / Δa plus a constant, which matches the Lai and Robbins lower bound up to constants. Thompson sampling has the same order of regret (Agrawal and Goyal, 2012).

Chapter summary

  1. A bandit has one state and k arms with unknown means. Maximise total reward, or equally minimise regret.
  2. Estimate each arm with a running average Q ← Q + (r − Q)/N, or a constant step size for drifting arms.
  3. Greedy can get stuck. ε greedy explores at random with chance ε.
  4. UCB adds a bonus c √(ln t / N) to rarely pulled arms and has logarithmic regret.
  5. Thompson sampling draws from Beta beliefs and pulls the best draw. It beats fixed A/B tests on reward during the test.
  6. Contextual bandits such as LinUCB choose per context. Full RL adds states that actions change.
