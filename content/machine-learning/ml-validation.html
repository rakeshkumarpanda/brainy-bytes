Lesson 1 of 10

Train, validation and test sets

Source: The Elements of Statistical Learning chapter 7, An Introduction to Statistical Learning chapter 5, Kohavi (1995), Kaufman and others, Leakage in Data Mining (2012), Cawley and Talbot (2010), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide sections 3.1 and 3.5.

What it is
Split the data into three parts with three jobs. The training set fits the model. The validation set compares models and settings. The test set is touched once, at the very end, to report how the chosen model will do on new data.
Why we need it
Every time you look at a score and change something, that data starts to shape the model. A test set used for choices is no longer an honest estimate, and the reported score will be too good. Keeping one set locked away is the only way to know how the model will really perform.
How it works
Use train_test_split twice: first split off the test set (often 20%), then split the rest into training and validation. Stratify by the label for classification. After choosing, retrain on training plus validation and score the test set once.

In the exam
Three different values of k tie at 0.982 on just 114 validation rows, and the rule "take the first best" picks k = 1, the most fragile choice. A single small validation set is too noisy to choose reliably. Cross validation, next, uses every row for validation once.
Real world scenario: a driving test

A learner driver practises on many roads (training), takes mock tests with an instructor to decide whether they are ready (validation), and then sits the real test on a route they have never driven (test). If the learner practised on the exact test route, passing it would prove very little.

Lesson 2 of 10

K fold cross validation

What it is
K fold cross validation splits the data into k equal folds. Each fold takes one turn as the validation set while the other k − 1 folds train the model. The k scores are averaged.
Why we need it
One split gives one noisy number that depends on which rows happened to land in the test set. Cross validation uses every row for validation once, so the mean is more stable, and the spread of the fold scores shows how much to trust it.
How it works
cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5) does it in one line. Use 5 or 10 folds. For classifiers an integer cv uses StratifiedKFold automatically. Shuffle with KFold(shuffle=True, random_state=0) when rows are sorted.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 split_luck.py.
Remember
The same decision tree scores anywhere from 0.904 to 0.956 depending only on the random split. Reporting one of those numbers could flatter or punish the model by 5 points. Ten fold cross validation gives 0.917 with a standard deviation of 0.033, an honest picture of both the level and the uncertainty.

Lesson 3 of 10

Stratified, grouped and time ordered splits

What it is
The right splitter depends on how the data was collected. StratifiedKFold keeps the class balance in every fold. GroupKFold keeps all rows of one group (a patient, a user, a device) in the same fold. TimeSeriesSplit always trains on the past and validates on the future.
Why we need it
Cross validation must copy the situation the model will face. A new patient has never been seen, so no row of theirs may be in training. Tomorrow’s data comes after today’s, so validating on the past with a model trained on the future is cheating.
How it works
Pick the splitter by asking: will new data contain new groups? Is there an order in time? Is one class rare? Then pass it as cv=, and pass groups= for group splitters.

Lesson 4 of 10

Data leakage: the silent killer

What it is
Data leakage happens when information that will not be available at prediction time, or information from the validation rows, sneaks into training. The scores look great and the model fails in real use.
Why we need it
Leakage is the most common reason a model that scored well in the lab fails in production. Typical causes: scaling or selecting features on all the data before splitting, a feature computed from the target, duplicates across splits, and future information in time series.
How it works
Put every step that learns from data (imputation, scaling, encoding, feature selection, resampling) inside a Pipeline, and cross validate the whole pipeline. Then each fold learns those steps from its own training rows only.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 leakage.py.
In the exam
The data is pure noise with random labels, so the honest answer is about 0.5. Choosing the 20 best of 5,000 features on all rows, and only then cross validating, reports 0.85. Doing the same selection inside a pipeline gives 0.48. This exact error is the "wrong way" to do cross validation in The Elements of Statistical Learning, section 7.10.2.

Lesson 5 of 10

Learning curves: will more data help?

What it is
A learning curve plots training and validation scores against the number of training rows.
Why we need it
Collecting data is expensive. The learning curve tells you whether it is worth it. If both curves have flattened at a poor score (high bias), more data will not help, but a more flexible model will. If there is a big gap that is still closing (high variance), more data or more regularisation will help.
How it works
sklearn.model_selection.learning_curve(model, X, y, train_sizes=..., cv=5) returns the sizes and the train and validation scores for each fold.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 learning_curve.py.
Remember
Naive Bayes stops improving at about 0.81 on validation and its training score falls to 0.87: the curves meet at a low level, a high bias model, so more digits will not help. The RBF SVM still gains from 0.961 to 0.972 as the data doubles, with training at 1.0: more data keeps paying off.

The maths, all in one place

K fold estimateExam favourite
CVk = (1/k) Σi Ei
Ei is the score on fold i.
Standard error of the meanExam favourite
SE = s / √k
Rough, since folds share training rows.
Fold sizeExam favourite
n / k rows validate, n(k − 1)/k train
5 fold: 80% train, 20% validate.
Fits for a grid searchExam favourite
k × (number of settings) + 1 refit
Grows fast with every new parameter.
Worked example: mean and spread of five folds
  1. Fold accuracies: 0.90, 0.80, 0.85, 0.95, 0.90.
  2. Mean = 4.40 / 5 = 0.88.
  3. Deviations: 0.02, −0.08, −0.03, 0.07, 0.02. Squares add to 0.013.
  4. standard deviation = √(0.013 / 5) = 0.051
  5. Report 0.88 ± 0.05. A rival at 0.89 is not clearly better.
Going deeper: The bias and variance of cross validation itself

Each fold model trains on only (k − 1)/k of the data, so cross validation slightly underestimates the performance of the final model trained on everything: a pessimistic bias that shrinks as k grows. Leave one out (k = n) has almost no bias, but its n training sets overlap almost completely, so the fold errors are highly correlated and the estimate has high variance (Kohavi, 1995, recommends stratified 10 fold). There is no unbiased estimator of the variance of k fold cross validation (Bengio and Grandvalet, 2004), so the ± from the fold standard deviation is only a guide. Finally, if you pick the best of many models by their cross validation scores, the winning score is optimistically biased, the winner’s curse described by Cawley and Talbot (2010). Nested cross validation, in the next chapter, fixes that.

Chapter summary

  1. Train fits, validation chooses, test reports once at the end.
  2. One split is noisy. K fold cross validation averages k scores and shows their spread.
  3. Pick the splitter from the data: StratifiedKFold for classes, GroupKFold for repeated groups, TimeSeriesSplit for time.
  4. Leakage makes scores too good. Fit every preprocessing step inside a Pipeline.
  5. Learning curves tell whether more data (high variance) or a better model (high bias) will help.
  6. Choosing the best of many models by CV score is itself optimistic. Nested CV fixes it.
