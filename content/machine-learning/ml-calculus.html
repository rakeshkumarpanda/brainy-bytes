Lesson 1 of 12

Derivatives: how fast something changes

Source: Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning chapters 5 and 7, Goodfellow and others, Deep Learning chapter 4, and Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization chapter 3.

What it is
The derivative of a function at a point is its slope there: how much the output changes when the input moves a tiny bit. We write it f′(x) or df/dx.
Why we need it
Training a model means making the loss smaller. The derivative of the loss tells us, for each parameter, whether to increase or decrease it and how strongly it matters. Without derivatives we would have to guess blindly.
How it works
Take two points on the curve that are h apart, compute rise over run, and let h shrink towards 0. In practice we use rules (power, sum, product, chain) to write the derivative as a formula, and libraries compute it automatically.

Real world scenario: the speedometer in a car

Your car’s trip meter shows distance. The speedometer shows how fast that distance is changing right now. Speed is the derivative of distance. If you drive 60 km in one hour, the average speed is 60 km per hour, but the speedometer shows the speed at this exact moment: the limit over a tiny time step.

Lesson 3 of 12

The chain rule: slopes multiply

What it is
The chain rule gives the derivative of a function inside another function: if y = f(g(x)), then dy/dx = f′(g(x)) × g′(x). The slopes of the steps multiply.
Why we need it
Every model is a chain of simple steps: multiply by weights, add, squash with a sigmoid, compute a loss. To know how the loss depends on a weight deep inside, we chain the slopes of each step. This is exactly what backpropagation does in neural networks (Deep Learning 1.2).
How it works
Write the computation as small steps. Find the slope of each step on its own. Multiply them along the path from the weight to the output.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 chain_rule.py.
Worked example: chain rule by hand
  1. y = (3x + 1)2. Inner g(x) = 3x + 1, outer f(u) = u2.
  2. Slope of the outer step: f′(u) = 2u = 2(3x + 1).
  3. Slope of the inner step: g′(x) = 3.
  4. dy/dx = 2(3x + 1) × 3 = 6(3x + 1)
  5. At x = 1: dy/dx = 6 × 4 = 24. A tiny nudge of x by 0.001 changes y by about 0.024.
Lesson 4 of 12

Partial derivatives and the gradient

What it is
When a function has many inputs, a partial derivative is its slope along one input while the others stay fixed. The gradient is the list of all the partial derivatives, written ∇f.
Why we need it
A model has many weights. The gradient of the loss says, for every weight at once, which way and how strongly to change it. It points in the direction where the loss grows fastest.
How it works
Compute each partial derivative by treating the other variables as constants. Then step against the gradient: wnew = w − η ∇L(w). Each weight moves by its own partial derivative.

Real world scenario: walking down a hill in fog

Imagine you are on a hill in thick fog and want to reach the valley. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. You take a step downhill, feel again, and repeat. Big steps are fast but you might jump over the valley. Tiny steps are safe but slow. That is gradient descent, and the step size is the learning rate.

Lesson 7 of 12

Valleys, convexity and local minima

Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 local_minima.py.
What it is
A function is convex when it is shaped like a bowl: any line between two points on the curve lies above it. A convex function has only one valley, so gradient descent cannot get stuck in a wrong one.
Why we need it
Linear regression with squared error and logistic regression have convex losses, so they always reach the best answer. Neural networks do not, so where training starts and how it moves matters.
How it works
A local minimum is a point lower than everything nearby but not the lowest overall (the global minimum). A saddle point has slope 0 but goes down in some direction. Random starts, momentum and noise from mini-batches help in non convex problems.
Convex test (one variable)Exam favourite
f′′(x) ≥ 0 everywhere
The second derivative (curvature) is never negative.
At a minimum
f′(x) = 0 and f′′(x) > 0
Flat and curving up.
Second order step (Newton)
x ← x − f′(x) / f′′(x)
Uses curvature to pick the step size. Fast but costly with many parameters.
Common mistake
Gradient descent finds a point where the gradient is near 0. For a convex loss this is the best answer. For a non convex loss it may be a local minimum or a saddle point.

The maths, all in one place

DerivativeExam favourite
f′(x) = limh→0 (f(x + h) − f(x)) / h
The slope at a single point.
Chain ruleExam favourite
d/dx f(g(x)) = f′(g(x)) g′(x)
Slopes along a chain multiply.
Gradient descentExam favourite
wt+1 = wt − η ∇L(wt)
One step for every parameter at once.
Steps on f(x) = a x2
xt+1 = (1 − 2 a η) xt
Converges when |1 − 2aη| < 1, so η < 1/a.
Central difference
f′(x) ≈ (f(x + h) − f(x − h)) / 2h
Error shrinks like h2. Used for gradient checks.
Worked example: two steps of gradient descent by hand
  1. Minimise L(w) = (w − 5)2, start w = 1, learning rate 0.25.
  2. L′(w) = 2(w − 5). At w = 1: slope = −8. New w = 1 − 0.25 × (−8) = 3.
  3. At w = 3: slope = −4. New w = 3 − 0.25 × (−4) = 4.
  4. Each step halves the distance to 5: 4, 2, 1, 0.5, ...
  5. Distance factor = 1 − 2 × 1 × 0.25 = 0.5, as the formula above predicts.
Going deeper: Why gradient descent slows down in long narrow valleys

If the loss curves strongly in one direction and gently in another (a large condition number, the ratio of the largest to the smallest curvature), a learning rate small enough for the steep direction is far too small for the flat one, and the path zig zags. Feature scaling makes the valley round, which is why it speeds up training. Momentum and Adam help too. Convergence for a convex quadratic takes about κ log(1/ε) steps, where κ is the condition number.

Lesson 10 of 12

From beginner to expert

One idea, four depths

A derivative is a slope. Gradient descent looks at the slope of the error and takes a small step downhill, again and again, until the error is as small as it can get.

Use the power, sum, product and chain rules, compute partial derivatives and the gradient, apply w ← w − η∇L, pick a learning rate, and know batch, stochastic and mini-batch versions and the idea of an epoch.

Convex losses (least squares, logistic) have one minimum. Non convex losses have local minima and saddle points. The Hessian measures curvature, its condition number controls speed, and feature scaling and momentum improve conditioning.

For L-smooth convex functions, gradient descent with η = 1/L converges at rate O(1/t), and Nesterov acceleration reaches the optimal O(1/t2). SGD with decaying steps converges in expectation (Robbins and Monro, 1951). In deep networks most critical points in high dimensions are saddles rather than bad local minima (Dauphin and others, 2014).

Intermediate

Expert

Chapter summary

  1. The derivative is the slope at a point: the limit of rise over run as the run shrinks to 0.
  2. Rules: power n xn−1, sum, product, ex stays ex, ln x gives 1/x, sigmoid gives σ(1 − σ).
  3. The chain rule multiplies the slopes of the steps. It is the base of backpropagation.
  4. The gradient lists all partial derivatives and points uphill. Gradient descent steps against it: w ← w − η∇L.
  5. The learning rate must be neither too small (slow) nor too big (diverges). On a x2, η must be below 1/a.
  6. Batch uses all rows per step, SGD one row, mini-batch a small group. One epoch is one pass over the data.
  7. Convex losses have one valley. Non convex ones have local minima and saddle points.
