Lesson 1 of 10

Manifolds, and distance along the data

Source: Tenenbaum, de Silva and Langford, Isomap (Science 2000), Roweis and Saul, Locally Linear Embedding (Science 2000), Belkin and Niyogi, Laplacian Eigenmaps (2003), and the scikit-learn 1.8 user guide section 2.2.

What it is
A manifold is a low dimensional surface curled up inside a high dimensional space, like a sheet of paper rolled into a tube. Manifold learning finds coordinates on that surface. Isomap measures distance along the surface (the geodesic distance) as the shortest path through a nearest neighbour graph, then lays the points out so those distances are kept.
Why we need it
Real data often has few true degrees of freedom: photos of a face turning have thousands of pixels but are controlled by a couple of angles. PCA only finds flat directions, so it cannot unroll curved structure. Manifold methods can, which makes them strong for pictures of complex data.
How it works
Build a k nearest neighbour graph. Isomap computes shortest paths between all pairs and runs classical MDS on them. LLE rebuilds each point from its neighbours and keeps those weights in low dimensions. Spectral embedding (Laplacian eigenmaps) uses eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian so that linked points stay close.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 swiss_roll.py.
In the exam
The swiss roll is a flat sheet rolled up in 3D. PCA squashes it flat and mixes the layers, so its best axis follows the position along the roll with a rank correlation of only 0.19. Isomap unrolls it almost perfectly (1.0), spectral embedding nearly so (0.998), and LLE reaches 0.916. All three need a sensible number of neighbours.
Real world scenario: walking around a mountain lake

Two villages sit on opposite shores of a lake. As the crow flies they are 2 km apart, but by the road around the shore it is 15 km. If you can only walk, the road distance is the one that matters. Isomap measures the road distance through the data instead of the straight line.

Lesson 2 of 10

Multidimensional scaling: a map from distances

What it is
Multidimensional scaling (MDS) takes only a table of distances between items and finds positions in 2D (or any dimension) whose distances match the table as closely as possible. Classical MDS solves this exactly with an eigen decomposition, and metric MDS minimises the stress, the sum of squared differences between the given and the map distances.
Why we need it
Sometimes you have distances but no features: how similar two products are, travel times between towns, or how often two words appear together. MDS turns those into a picture. Classical MDS on Euclidean distances gives exactly the same result as PCA.
How it works
MDS(n_components=2, metric="precomputed", metric_mds=True, init="classical_mds") in scikit-learn 1.8, then fit_transform(D) on a square distance matrix. The map can come out rotated or mirrored, because distances do not change under those moves.
Download this program (.py)Save it, open it in any editor and run it with python3 mds_map.py.
Remember
From the 10 distances alone, MDS rebuilds the five towns so well that no distance is off by even 0.001, and A to E is 6.32 both ways. The rebuilt map may be turned or flipped compared with the true one, since nothing in a distance table fixes north.

Lesson 3 of 10

t-SNE: maps that keep neighbours together

Source: van der Maaten and Hinton, Visualizing Data using t-SNE (JMLR 2008), van der Maaten, Accelerating t-SNE using Tree-Based Algorithms (JMLR 2014), and the scikit-learn 1.8 TSNE documentation.

What it is
t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding) makes 2D or 3D maps in which points that are neighbours in the original space stay neighbours. It turns distances into neighbour probabilities, with a Gaussian in high dimensions and a heavy tailed Student t curve in the map, and moves the map points to make the two sets of probabilities match.
Why we need it
t-SNE gives the clearest pictures of clusters in images, text embeddings, gene data and neural network features. It is a tool for looking and exploring, not for feeding a model.
How it works
For each point, choose a Gaussian width so that the perplexity (the effective number of neighbours) equals the setting, often 5 to 50. Place the map points at random or from PCA, then run gradient descent on the KL divergence. Early steps exaggerate the pull between neighbours so clusters form first.

Reading t-SNE maps without being fooled

Source: Wattenberg, Viegas and Johnson, How to Use t-SNE Effectively (Distill 2016).

What it is
t-SNE keeps neighbours but deliberately distorts everything else. Cluster sizes and distances between clusters in the map carry little meaning. Different perplexities and random starts give different looking maps, and random noise can show apparent clusters at low perplexity.
Why we need it
People often read a t-SNE map like a scatter plot of real coordinates and draw wrong conclusions, such as "this group is more varied" or "these two groups are related". Knowing what is and is not kept avoids that.
How it works
Try several perplexities, use init="pca" for more stable global layout, run more than one seed, check clusters with a method that works in the original space, and never use t-SNE coordinates as features for a model. t-SNE has no transform for new points.

Remember
On these 400 digits, neighbour accuracy stays between 0.985 and 0.992 for every perplexity from 2 to 100, and trustworthiness between 0.981 and 0.992. The local structure is robust. What changes is the look of the map: the size, shape and spacing of the clusters.

Lesson 5 of 10

UMAP and choosing a method

Source: McInnes, Healy and Melville, UMAP: Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction (2018), and the umap-learn documentation.

What it is
UMAP also builds a k nearest neighbour graph with fuzzy edge weights and then finds a low dimensional layout whose graph matches it, by minimising a cross entropy with stochastic gradient descent. Its two main settings are n_neighbors (default 15, local against global balance) and min_dist (default 0.1, how tightly points may pack).
Why we need it
UMAP is usually much faster than t-SNE on large data, often keeps more of the global arrangement of clusters, and can transform new points after fitting. It has become the default for large single cell biology and embedding datasets.
How it works
In Python it comes from the separate umap-learn package (umap.UMAP(n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.1).fit_transform(X)). It is not part of scikit-learn and does not run in this browser page, so the programs here use t-SNE, Isomap and MDS. The same reading rules apply: trust neighbours, be careful with sizes and gaps.

Worked example: classical MDS for two points
  1. Two points 2 apart: squared distance matrix D(2) = [[0, 4], [4, 0]].
  2. Double centring gives B = −½ J D(2) J = [[1, −1], [−1, 1]].
  3. The eigenvalues of B are 2 and 0. The top eigenvector is (1, −1) / √2.
  4. Coordinates = √2 × (1, −1)/√2 = (1, −1)
  5. The two points sit at 1 and −1, exactly 2 apart, centred at 0.
Going deeper: Why t-SNE uses a Student t in the map

In 10 dimensions a point can have many neighbours all at about the same distance, but in 2D there is not enough room to place them all equally close: this is the crowding problem. With a Gaussian in both spaces, moderately distant points get squashed together in the middle of the map. The Student t curve with one degree of freedom has heavy tails, so a moderate high dimensional distance can become a large map distance at little cost. That opens gaps between clusters. KL(P | Q) is asymmetric: placing true neighbours far apart (large p, small q) costs a lot, while placing non neighbours close (small p, large q) costs little. That is exactly why t-SNE keeps local structure and is loose with global structure. Barnes-Hut t-SNE approximates the repulsion with a quadtree and runs in O(n log n) instead of O(n2).

Manifold learning makes a flat map of data that is curled up in many dimensions, keeping points that are close together close on the map.

Chapter summary

  1. A manifold is a low dimensional surface inside a high dimensional space. PCA cannot unroll curved ones.
  2. Isomap uses shortest paths in a k neighbour graph (geodesic distances) and then classical MDS. LLE and spectral embedding keep local neighbourhoods.
  3. MDS makes a map from a distance table. Classical MDS on Euclidean distances equals PCA.
  4. t-SNE matches Gaussian neighbour probabilities with Student t map probabilities by minimising KL. Perplexity sets the effective number of neighbours.
  5. In t-SNE maps trust neighbours, not cluster sizes or gaps. Try several perplexities and seeds. There is no transform for new points.
  6. UMAP is faster, scales to millions and can transform new points, but lives in the separate umap-learn package.
