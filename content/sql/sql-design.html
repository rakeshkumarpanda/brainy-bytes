Lesson 1 of 5

From a story to tables

Good schemas start with words, not SQL. Read how the business talks about its work, then turn the nouns into tables and the verbs into relationships.

Real world scenario: the neighbourhood library

"Members borrow copies of books. A book can have several authors, and an author writes many books. The library may own several copies of a popular book, each on a shelf. A loan records who borrowed which copy, when, when it is due, and when it came back."

Five design steps
  1. Entities: underline the nouns that have their own facts: member, book, author, copy, loan.
  2. Attributes: list the facts of each: a member has a name and phone, a copy has a shelf.
  3. Relationships: a member borrows copies (through loans), a book has copies, books and authors are many to many.
  4. Keys: give every table a primary key, and turn each relationship into foreign keys or a junction table.
  5. Rules: write NOT NULL, UNIQUE and CHECK for every fact the business promises, such as due date after loan date.
Word in the storyBecomes
member, book, author, copytables
name, phone, title, shelfcolumns
"has several authors" and "writes many books"a junction table book_authors
"a copy of a book"copies.book_id references books
"who borrowed which copy, when"a loans table with two foreign keys and dates
Remember
A loan is an event, and events usually become their own table with a date. Do not squeeze it into members as columns like book1, book2, book3, which breaks 1NF.

ER diagrams and the three kinds of relationship

An entity relationship diagram (Peter Chen, 1976) draws entities as boxes and relationships as lines. Most tools today use crow’s foot notation: the three pronged end means "many", a bar means "one", and a circle means "optional".

RelationshipExampleTables
One to oneperson and passportforeign key with UNIQUE on one side

Habits that keep a schema healthy

These are widely used conventions, not laws. Pick a style and use it everywhere in one database.

HabitExampleWhy
Plural or singular table names, but always the samecustomers, orders, productsnobody has to guess
snake_case, no spaces, no reserved wordsorder_date, not "Order Date" or dateno quoting needed
A surrogate id plus UNIQUE on the natural keyid INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, email UNIQUEids never change, emails can
Foreign key named after the tablecustomer_id references customers(id)joins read naturally
Created and updated timescreated_at, updated_atdebugging and audits
Money as exact numbersprice_paise INTEGER or DECIMAL(10,2)no rounding errors

Lesson 5 of 5

From intuition to research

One idea, four depths

Turn nouns into tables and verbs into links. Give everything a key, and write the rules down in the table.

Draw an ER diagram, map each relationship to foreign keys or a junction table, normalize to 3NF, and add constraints for every business rule.

Conceptual, logical and physical models are separate stages: the ER model, the relational schema, then types, indexes and storage. Subtypes (a member who is a student or staff) can map to one table with a type column, one table per subtype, or a shared parent table.

Schema evolution, keeping years of data valid while the design changes, is an active research and tooling area. So is modelling for distributed and document databases, where data is often shaped around the queries rather than the entities.

Going deeper: Modelling inheritance

Suppose some members are students with a roll number and some are staff with an employee code. Option 1: one members table with nullable roll_no and emp_code plus a kind column. Option 2: separate student_members and staff_members tables. Option 3: a members parent table plus child tables sharing its id. Option 3 keeps NOT NULL rules honest and is the usual relational choice.
