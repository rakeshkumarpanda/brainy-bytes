Lesson 1 of 6

Video and optical flow

The lecture opens this part with one sentence: a video adds one more piece of information that a still image does not have, time. A video is a stack of frames, so it is 2D plus time, an image plus motion.

Optical flow is the pattern of apparent motion of objects, surfaces and edges in a scene, caused by the relative motion between the camera and the scene. Compare frame T with frame T + 1, and give every point a flow vector (u, v): how far it moved across and how far it moved up or down.

Flow magnitudeExam favourite
|(u, v)| = √u² + v²
How far the point moved between two frames, in pixels per frame.
Flow direction
θ = atan2(v, u)
The angle of the motion. 0° is right, 90° is up (with up taken as positive).
SpeedExam favourite
speed = magnitude × fps
Pixels per frame times frames per second gives pixels per second.

Sparse or dense

TypeWhat it computesWhen to use it
SparseMotion for a selected set of informative points, usually cornersLimited computing power, or a uniform still background where only a few objects need tracking
DenseMotion for almost every pixelCrowded scenes such as pedestrians in a busy street, where motion matters everywhere. It needs much more computation.
The lecture problem
  1. A pixel moves by (u, v) = (3, 4) pixels between two frames.
  2. Magnitude: √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5 pixels per frame.
  3. At 30 fps each frame lasts 1/30 of a second, so speed = 5 × 30 = 150 pixels per second.
  4. Direction: atan2(4, 3) ≈ 53.13°, between right and up. With 8 bins of 45°, the nearest is up-right.
In the exam
In flow pictures, colour shows direction and the strength of the colour shows speed. Learn to read the colour wheel, because exam figures often use it.
Lesson 2 of 6

How flow is measured: brightness constancy

A computer cannot see motion directly. It sees two grids of numbers. The classic trick is to assume that a point keeps its brightness as it moves. That assumption turns into one equation per pixel.

Brightness constancy
I(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t)
The same point has the same brightness in the next frame, just somewhere else.
Optical flow constraintExam favourite
Ix u + Iy v + It = 0
From a first order Taylor expansion of the line above. Ix, Iy are the image gradients from chapter 1.2 and It is the change over time.

One equation, two unknowns. On a straight edge you can only measure the motion across the edge, never the motion along it. This is the aperture problem, and you can watch it below.

Two classic fixes (both from 1981)

MethodExtra assumptionResult
Lucas and KanadeAll pixels in a small window share one (u, v). Solve the window equations together by least squares.Fast and local. Works at corners, fails on plain edges and flat areas. Used for sparse tracking.
Horn and SchunckThe flow field is smooth over the whole image. Minimise the constraint error plus a smoothness penalty.A dense field everywhere, but it blurs across object boundaries.
Going deeper: the Lucas and Kanade equations

Stack the constraint for the n pixels of a window: A [u, v]T = −b, where row i of A is (Ix, Iy) at pixel i and b holds the It values. The least squares answer solves (ATA) [u, v]T = −ATb. The 2 × 2 matrix ATA is the same structure tensor used by the Harris corner detector. It can be inverted only when both of its eigenvalues are large, which happens at corners. That is the mathematical reason sparse trackers follow corners. Large motions break the Taylor approximation, so real systems run the method on an image pyramid, coarse to fine.

Real world scenario: your phone camera

Video stabilisation, slow motion and the night mode that merges several frames all need to know how pixels moved between frames. Modern phones estimate flow with small neural networks descended from FlowNet (2015) and RAFT (2020), but the target they estimate is the same (u, v) field defined here.

Lesson 3 of 6

HOF and MBH: describing motion

Before deep learning, action recognition used hand made motion descriptors: compact lists of numbers that summarise how a video moves. The pipeline is video frames → optical flow → HOF or MBH → action clue.

HOF: which way is the motion happening

The histogram of oriented optical flow counts motion directions. The lecture gives four steps.

  1. Take two consecutive frames, T and T + 1.
  2. Compute optical flow, a motion vector for each pixel or region.
  3. Group the vectors by direction into bins: right, left, up, down and the diagonals.
  4. Build the histogram. The tallest bin is the dominant direction. In fuller versions each vote is weighted by the flow magnitude.
HOF bin weightExam favourite
H[b] = Σpixels with direction in bin b √u² + v²
With 8 bins of 45°, the bin of a vector is round(θ ÷ 45°) mod 8.

MBH: where motion changes

The motion boundary histogram does not look at the motion itself. It looks at where the motion changes. It takes the spatial gradient of the flow components u and v, exactly as HOG takes the gradient of the intensity, and builds orientation histograms of those gradients.

MBH inputExam favourite
∇u = (∂u/∂x, ∂u/∂y),   ∇v = (∂v/∂x, ∂v/∂y)
If the camera adds the same vector to every pixel, every gradient stays the same. That is why MBH ignores camera motion.
HOGHOFMBH
QuestionWhat does it look like?Which way is it moving?Where does motion differ from its surroundings?
SignalGradient of intensityFlow directionGradient of the flow
Good forShape of a person or objectClear directional motion with a still cameraCamera motion, separating object from background
WeaknessIgnores motionCan be swamped by camera motionLess direct for a pure direction summary
Remember
HOF is how things move. MBH is where motion differs. HOG is what it looks like. All three are the same operation, a local histogram of orientations, applied to three different signals.

Which descriptor? The lecture scenarios

The lecturer asked a series of which descriptor questions across both sessions. They are exactly the kind of reasoning exams test, so here they are with answers. Use the animation in the previous lesson to check each one.

ScenarioAnswerWhy
Two people walk in opposite directions. Are their HOF features the same?DifferentHOF is direction sensitive. Their tallest bins are on opposite sides. Over the whole frame you would see two peaks.
A person walks while the camera pans right. Which descriptor isolates the person?MBHThe pan adds uniform motion to the background, which swamps HOF. MBH suppresses uniform motion and keeps the person boundaries.
A fixed camera films a ball moving up in front of a still wall.HOFNo competing motion. HOF shows the upward bin directly and MBH adds little.
A person cycles to the right.Both tell different thingsHOF says the dominant direction is right. MBH highlights the pedalling legs, whose motion differs from the steady torso.
A drone flies beside a running cheetah, keeping it centred. You want the leg motion.MBHThe grass streaks past everywhere. MBH ignores that uniform motion and highlights the legs moving relative to the body.

In the exam
The rule from the lecture: whenever camera motion is present and could contaminate HOF, prefer MBH. When nothing competes with the motion you care about, HOF alone is sufficient.

Two questions the lecture answered briefly

Can HOF or MBH describe colour or appearance? No. They are built only from flow direction and size. MBH gives at most a rough outline of a moving blob, enough to say human shaped, not enough to tell a fox from a dog.

Is MBH still used? Yes, where deep models cannot run: small edge devices with no GPU and little memory. Elsewhere, learned motion features have replaced it.

Lesson 5 of 6

The traditional action recognition pipeline

Here is how the descriptors were combined into a full action classifier before deep learning. Every step was designed and tuned by hand.

  1. Find space time interest points, places where motion or appearance changes, such as moving joints and limbs.
  2. Cut space time patches around each point: small windows in space and time, at several scales.
  3. Describe each patch with HOG for appearance, HOF for motion or MBH for motion boundaries.
  4. Cluster into a dictionary of visual words. k-means groups similar descriptors from many training videos. Each cluster centre is one word.
  5. Build a histogram of visual words for each video: how often each word occurs. Every video becomes a vector of the same length.
  6. Classify with an SVM (support vector machine), which predicts the action label.
k-means objectiveExam favourite
J = Σi ‖xi − μc(i)‖²
Alternate two steps: assign each descriptor to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the mean of its descriptors. J never increases.

This is the bag of words model from language processing, applied to video. A document is a count of words from a fixed dictionary, ignoring order. A video is a count of visual words, ignoring where and when each occurred.

What deep learning replaced

Hand made stepLearned replacement
Interest points, patches, HOG and HOF3D CNNs that convolve over height, width and time together (for example C3D, I3D)
Separate appearance and motion descriptorsTwo stream networks: one stream sees RGB frames (like HOG), one sees stacked optical flow (like HOF), Simonyan and Zisserman 2014
Visual word dictionary and SVMVideo transformers that attend across space and time, trained end to end
Going deeper: why the two stream split mirrors HOG and HOF

The two stream network makes the same split the hand made pipeline made: appearance from single frames and motion from flow. Its flow stream takes a stack of about 10 flow fields (20 channels for u and v) as input, so its first layer learns filters over flow, which is what HOF and MBH were designed by hand to summarise. Later work (I3D, 2017) showed that adding the flow stream still helped even strong 3D CNNs, which is evidence that explicit motion carries information that RGB networks do not easily learn alone.

Modern video tasks

The lecture ended this part with a quick tour of current video problems. The point is breadth: know what each task is and why it is hard.

TaskWhat it meansNotes from the lecture
Frame interpolationPredict an unknown frame between two known framesUsed for slow motion (for example Super SloMo). Smooth interpolation in a generative model latent space shows the model learned a continuous space rather than memorising examples.
StabilisationRemove unwanted camera shakeA phone feature and a preprocessing step, since shake corrupts flow, HOF and MBH. Gaps created by the correction can be filled like inpainting.
Video denoisingRemove noise using several framesImage denoising from chapter 2.3, extended over time.
Person re-identificationFind every appearance of one person across a video or camerasDespite changes in pose, clothing colour or light. Used to follow one ID through a campus from entry gate to exit gate.
Object propagationShare detections over timeIf an object was found confidently in frame T, use that to find it cheaply in frame T + 1 instead of starting again.
Context and relationsUnderstand interactions, not single actionsGraph neural networks link people and objects. Example: a character interaction map and event flow built from a film.
Video question answering and RAGAnswer questions about a long videoSummarise video into text first, then retrieve the relevant part and answer from text plus video. Example: insurance claims after a car accident.
Common mistake
Interpolation was defined in class only after several attempts. It is not generating new images and not editing pixels. It is predicting the missing frame between two frames.
Real world scenario: a university campus

Cameras at the main gate see a visitor enter. Re-identification gives that person an ID and matches them as they appear on other cameras, even when lighting and viewing angle change. Object propagation keeps tracking cheap between frames. At the exit gate the system confirms the same ID left. Each piece uses ideas from this chapter: motion between frames, descriptors that survive change, and sharing information over time.

One idea, four depths

A video is a flip book. Optical flow is a set of arrows that show where each bit of the picture moved from one page to the next. HOF counts which way the arrows point. MBH looks for places where the arrows suddenly change, like at the edge of a moving car.

Flow (u, v) has magnitude √(u² + v²) and speed magnitude × fps. Brightness constancy gives Ixu + Iyv + It = 0, one equation for two unknowns. HOF bins flow directions, MBH bins gradients of the flow, and a bag of visual words with an SVM classifies the action.

Lucas and Kanade solve the constraint by least squares in a window, with the structure tensor deciding whether the answer is stable. Horn and Schunck add a global smoothness term and solve a variational problem. Dense trajectories (2011) tracked points through dense flow for 15 frames and described each track with HOG, HOF and MBH, which made MBH the strongest hand made motion feature of its time.

Learned flow estimators such as RAFT build a 4D correlation volume between all pixel pairs and refine the flow with a recurrent update. Self supervised methods train flow by warping one frame onto the next and penalising the photometric error, which is brightness constancy turned into a loss. Video foundation models now learn motion implicitly from masked frame prediction, and long video understanding is moving toward retrieval over text and visual memories.
