Lesson 1 of 5

Four kinds of noise

No camera records a perfect copy of the scene. The lecture calls noise the unwanted guests in an image: random changes caused by the sensor, the electronics, poor light, the weather or errors during transmission. Each kind of noise has its own pattern, and each pattern calls for a different filter. Using the wrong filter either leaves the noise or destroys real detail.

Additive noiseExam favourite
g(x, y) = f(x, y) + n(x, y)
f is the true image, g the one you record. The noise does not depend on the brightness.
Multiplicative (speckle) noiseExam favourite
g(x, y) = f(x, y) · (1 + n(x, y))
The noise is scaled by the signal, so bright regions get noisier than dark ones.
NoiseWhat it looks likeTypical sourceBest filter
Gaussian (white)Many small random changes over the whole image, a grainy lookSensor and electronic noiseGaussian or averaging filter
Impulse (salt and pepper)A few pixels jump to pure black (0) or pure white (255)Transmission or sensor errorsMedian filter
SpeckleGranular texture whose strength grows with local brightnessUltrasound and SAR (synthetic aperture radar)Adaptive or median type filters
Poisson (shot)Grain that is worst in low lightToo few photons reach the sensorDenoising with variance stabilisation

The lecture made one more point that is easy to miss. High frequency is not a type of noise. It only means the intensity changes quickly from pixel to pixel. Gaussian and impulse noise both contain strong high frequencies, but so do real edges and fine texture. A filter that simply removes all high frequencies therefore blurs away detail along with the noise.

In the exam
Salt and pepper pushes a few pixels straight to an extreme by a sudden jump. Speckle changes values gradually by multiplying them. The mechanisms differ, which is why they need different filters.
Lesson 2 of 5

Convolution: sliding a kernel

Spatial filtering means applying a small grid of weights, called a kernel or mask, to the neighbourhood of every pixel. Lay the kernel over the image, multiply each weight by the pixel beneath it, add up the products, and write the total into the output. Then slide one step and repeat. The same idea, with the weights learned instead of chosen, is the building block of every convolutional neural network later in the course.

Filtering (correlation)Exam favourite
g(x, y) = Σi=−aa Σj=−bb h(i, j) · f(x + i, y + j)
A (2a + 1) × (2b + 1) kernel h. This is what cv2.filter2D and CNN layers compute.
True convolution
(h ∗ f)(x, y) = Σi Σj h(i, j) · f(x − i, y − j)
The same, with the kernel flipped in both directions. For symmetric kernels such as box and Gaussian the two are identical.
Output sizeExam favourite
O = ⌊W − k + 2PS⌋ + 1
W input width, k kernel size, P padding on each side, S stride.

What happens at the border

At the edge of the image part of the kernel hangs over empty space. There are three common choices. Zero padding pretends the outside is black and keeps the size. Replicate copies the edge pixel outward, which avoids a dark frame. Valid (no padding) only computes where the kernel fits, so the output shrinks by k − 1. Turn the padding dial in the animation and watch the corner values change.

Going deeper: why convolution is linear and shift invariant

If you filter af1 + bf2 you get a(h ∗ f1) + b(h ∗ f2), because each output is a weighted sum. Shifting the input shifts the output by the same amount, because the same weights are used everywhere. Every operation that is both linear and shift invariant is a convolution with some kernel, namely its response to a single bright pixel (the impulse response). Try the spike input in the animation: the output is the kernel itself, flipped. This is also why a CNN layer can detect a pattern wherever it appears in the image.

Lesson 3 of 5

Smoothing: mean, Gaussian and median

Smoothing filters are low pass filters. They keep slow changes and remove quick ones, which makes the image look softer and quieter.

Mean (box) filterExam favourite
h = 19111111111
Each output is the plain average of the 3 × 3 neighbourhood. The weights add up to 1, so flat areas keep their brightness.
Gaussian kernelExam favourite
G(x, y) = 12πσ² exp(−x² + y²2σ²)
Near pixels count more than far ones. A common 3 × 3 version is (1/16)[[1, 2, 1], [2, 4, 2], [1, 2, 1]].
OpenCV default σ
σ = 0.3 × ((k − 1) × 0.5 − 1) + 0.8
Used when you pass σ = 0, as the notebook does with cv2.GaussianBlur(image, (9, 9), 0). For k = 9 this gives σ = 1.7.
Median filter
g(x, y) = median{{ f(s, t) : (s, t) in the window }}
Not a weighted sum at all. Sort the neighbours and take the middle one.
The lecture problem: mean versus median
  1. Patch: [[10, 20, 10], [100, 20, 10], [10, 10, 20]]. The 100 is almost certainly a noisy pixel.
  2. Mean: 10 + 20 + 10 + 100 + 20 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 20 = 210, and 210 ÷ 9 ≈ 23.33. Without the outlier the average would be near 14.4, so one bad pixel pulled it up a lot.
  3. Median: sort the nine values to 10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 20, 20, 20, 100 and take the 5th. The answer is 10.
  4. The median is far closer to the true local value, because the outlier sorts to the end of the list and never reaches the middle.

Why the median keeps edges sharp

Averaging is linear, so every neighbour, including a badly wrong one, pulls the result toward itself. For an outlier to change the median, more than half the window would have to be outliers too. And the median is always a value that really occurred in the window, while the mean can invent a blended value that was never there. At an edge the mean produces a ramp of new in between values, but the median picks a value from one side or the other, so the edge stays crisp.

Going deeper: separable filters save work

A kernel is separable when it equals a column vector times a row vector. The box and Gaussian kernels are separable, for example (1/16)[[1, 2, 1], [2, 4, 2], [1, 2, 1]] is (1/4)[1, 2, 1] down the columns times (1/4)[1, 2, 1] across the rows. Filtering with a k × k kernel costs k² multiplications per pixel, but filtering rows and then columns costs only 2k. For k = 9 that is 81 against 18. The median is not separable, and fast versions use running histograms instead.

Lesson 4 of 5

Sharpening and the Laplacian

Sharpening is the opposite job. It makes edges and fine detail stand out by boosting quick changes. Two kernels appear in this course, and they are closely related.

Lecture high pass kernelExam favourite
h = 19-1-1-1-18-1-1-1-1
The weights add up to 0. In a flat region every neighbour cancels the centre and the output is 0. At an edge the output is large.
Notebook sharpening kernelExam favourite
h = 0-10-15-10-10
The weights add up to 1, so flat regions keep their brightness while edges get extra contrast. The project used this kernel in Stage 3.
Unsharp masking
sharpened = f + k (f − Gσ ∗ f)
f − G ∗ f is the detail that a blur removes. Add k times it back.

Both kernels come from the same recipe. The lecture kernel is the identity minus the box average: the centre weight is 1 − 1/9 = 8/9 and the others are −1/9. So it equals original minus low pass, which is the high pass part. The notebook kernel equals the identity minus the 4 neighbour Laplacian [[0, 1, 0], [1, −4, 1], [0, 1, 0]], which is original plus high pass. The Laplacian is a discrete second derivative, and it is exactly what the lecture means by highlights edges and fine details.

Sharpening one pixel with the notebook kernel
  1. A pixel of value 100 has four neighbours (up, down, left, right) of value 80.
  2. Multiply: 5 × 100 = 500, and each neighbour gives −1 × 80 = −80.
  3. Add: 500 − 4 × 80 = 500 − 320 = 180.
  4. The pixel was 20 brighter than its neighbours and is now 100 brighter. In a flat region of 80s the output would be 5 × 80 − 320 = 80, unchanged.
Common mistake
Sharpening cannot bring back detail that blur destroyed. It boosts whatever high frequencies are left, and that includes noise. In the project, sharpening the blurred COCO128 images raised mAP50 from 0.6176 to 0.6277 but it stayed below the original 0.6706. You will meet those numbers again in the project lab.
Real world scenario: a phone camera

Almost every phone applies some sharpening before it saves a photo, because the tiny lens and sensor blur fine detail. Push it too far and you see bright halos around dark edges and crunchy noise in the sky. Those halos are the overshoots in the curve animation above. Photo editors expose the same three dials as unsharp masking: amount (k), radius (σ) and a threshold that skips small, noisy differences.

The frequency view and learning from noise

Filtering can be done in two places. In the spatial domain you slide a kernel over the pixels, as above. In the frequency domain you first turn the image into its frequencies, the same idea as the DCT behind JPEG in chapter 2.1, then multiply each frequency by a gain and turn it back.

Convolution theoremExam favourite
g = h ∗ f   ⟺   G(ω1, ω2) = H(ω1, ω2) F(ω1, ω2)
Convolving in space is the same as multiplying in frequency. Capital letters are Fourier transforms.
FilterKeepsRemovesLooks like
Low pass (mean, Gaussian)Slow changes, large shapesFine texture, noise, sharp edgesBlur
High pass (Laplacian, lecture 1/9 kernel)Edges and fine detailFlat areas and smooth shadingA dark image with bright outlines
Sharpen = original + high passEverything, with extra edgesNothingCrisper edges, more visible noise

For large kernels the frequency route is faster, because a fast Fourier transform costs about N log N operations, while direct convolution costs N × k². For the small kernels used here, direct filtering wins.

Noise is also added on purpose

The lecturer described a review of a medical imaging paper. The reviewers said the test images were too clean to represent real clinics. The fix was to add different kinds and combinations of noise to the test set and show the model still worked. Noise is therefore not only something to remove. It is a tool for testing robustness.

A whole family of models learns by removing noise. A denoising autoencoder (Vincent and colleagues, 2008) is trained to rebuild a clean image from a deliberately corrupted copy. Diffusion models, behind most current image generators, add Gaussian noise to real images over many small steps and train a network to undo one step at a time. To generate a picture they start from pure noise and run the learned denoising process repeatedly.

One idea, four depths

Noise is random speckle that the camera adds. Blurring hides it by mixing each dot with its neighbours. Sharpening does the opposite and makes edges pop. The median filter is like asking nine neighbours and going with the middle answer, so one person shouting a wild number cannot change the result.

A linear filter is a convolution with a kernel. Low pass kernels have weights that add to 1, high pass kernels add to 0. The output size is ⌊(W − k + 2P) ÷ S⌋ + 1. The median is a non linear order statistic that removes impulse noise and keeps edges. PSNR = 10 log10(255² ÷ MSE) measures quality against a clean reference.

By the convolution theorem, filtering multiplies the spectrum by H. The Gaussian is the only kernel that is both separable and rotationally symmetric, and its Fourier transform is again a Gaussian, so it removes high frequencies smoothly without ringing. The box filter has a sinc shaped spectrum with side lobes, which lets some high frequencies through. Wiener filtering chooses H = |F|² ÷ (|F|² + |N|²) to minimise mean squared error when the signal and noise spectra are known.

Modern denoisers are learned. Noise2Noise showed a network can be trained on pairs of independent noisy images with no clean target, because the expected error points to the same answer. Score based diffusion models learn the gradient of the log density of noisy images at every noise level. PSNR rewards blurry averages, so research also reports SSIM and learned perceptual metrics such as LPIPS.
