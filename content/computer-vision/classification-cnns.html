Lesson 1 of 6

Image classification and its datasets

Image classification answers one question about the whole picture: what is the main thing in it. The model does not return just a word. It returns a probability for every class, and the highest one becomes the prediction.

ClassPredicted probability
Dog0.82
Cat0.10
Wolf0.05
Other0.03

The datasets that drove progress

Caltech 101, PASCAL VOC and ImageNet gave researchers many images, fixed categories, agreed train and test splits and common metrics, so models could be compared fairly. The lecture singles out ImageNet: about 22,000 categories and about 15 million images in total. Its yearly challenge (ILSVRC) used a controlled subset of 1,000 classes with about 1.28 million training images, or about 1.43 million counting the validation and test images.

The central lesson from the lecture: more data often needs a more powerful architecture to learn from it. A small model on ImageNet underfits however long you train it.

In the exam
Classification gives one label for the whole image. Detection adds a box for each of a variable number of objects. Segmentation labels every pixel. Know all three outputs.
Lesson 2 of 6

Convolutional neural networks

In chapter 2.3 you chose the kernel numbers yourself: a box for blur, Sobel for edges. A convolutional neural network (CNN) uses exactly the same sliding operation, but it learns the kernel numbers from labelled examples. Each layer has many filters, and each filter produces one feature map.

One convolution layerExam favourite
y = ReLU(W ∗ x + b),   ReLU(t) = max(0, t)
The filters W and biases b are learned. ReLU keeps positive responses and sets the rest to 0.
Parameters of a layerExam favourite
params = k × k × Cin × Cout + Cout
Each of the Cout filters spans all Cin input channels and has one bias.
Output size
O = ⌊W − k + 2PS⌋ + 1
The same formula as chapter 2.3. The number of output channels is the number of filters you choose.

Pooling

Pooling shrinks a feature map. Max pooling keeps the largest value in each 2 × 2 block, average pooling keeps the mean. Pooling makes the network a little tolerant to small shifts, reduces computation, and lets later layers see a larger part of the image.

A hierarchy of features

The lecture describes how CNNs learn in layers. Early layers respond to simple edges and intensity patterns, much like Sobel and HOG. Middle layers combine these into textures, corners and parts. Deep layers respond to whole objects and meaningful concepts. This is the same climb from pixels to meaning that Marr described in chapter 1.2, but learned from data rather than designed.

Lesson 3 of 6

SoftMax, sigmoid and cross entropy

The last layer of a classifier outputs one raw number per class, called a logit. These numbers can be any size and sign, so they are turned into probabilities.

SoftMaxExam favourite
pi = eziΣj ezj
All probabilities are positive and add up to 1, so the classes compete.
SigmoidExam favourite
σ(z) = 11 + e−z
Squashes one score into (0, 1). Independent sigmoids do not add up to 1.
Cross entropy lossExam favourite
L = −Σj yj ln pj = −ln ptrue
y is one hot: 1 for the true class, 0 elsewhere. A confident correct answer gives a loss near 0.
Gradient
∂L ÷ ∂zk = pk − yk
Push the true logit up and the others down, each in proportion to its error.
The lecture example by hand
  1. Logits z = [2.0, 1.0, 0.1].
  2. Exponentiate: e2.0 = 7.389, e1.0 = 2.718, e0.1 = 1.105.
  3. Sum: 7.389 + 2.718 + 1.105 = 11.213.
  4. Divide: p = [0.659, 0.242, 0.099], which adds up to 1.
  5. If class 1 is correct, the loss is −ln 0.659 ≈ 0.417 and the gradient is [−0.341, 0.242, 0.099].

SoftMax or sigmoid

The lecturer's rule: sigmoid for two classes, SoftMax for many, although SoftMax also works for two. The precise picture: use SoftMax when exactly one class is correct, and one sigmoid per class when several labels can be true at once (a photo tagged both beach and sunset). With two classes they are the same thing, because ez1 ÷ (ez1 + ez2) = σ(z1 − z2).

Remember
Computers overflow when they raise e to a large number. Since SoftMax(z) = SoftMax(z − max z), real code subtracts the largest logit first. The answer does not change.
Lesson 4 of 6

Confidence, inductive bias and calibration

When a model says dog with probability 0.80, that number is called its confidence. The class answer in the lecture was correct: most often it is the SoftMax output, though entropy based measures are also used.

Inductive bias

The lecture asked what inductive bias is. The answer: the assumptions a model makes so that it can generalise. The example was the sequence 2, 4, 6. Most people say the next number is 8, assuming add 2 each time. But add the last two numbers would give 10, and that also fits. Every model must assume something to fill the gap between the examples it saw and new ones, and its confidence is shaped by those assumptions.

Real world scenario: ducks on a lake

A model is trained only on photos of ducks swimming on blue lakes. It learns that blue background means duck, and then says not a duck for a duck standing on brown sand. The data was consistent with two rules, duck shape or blue background, and the model picked the wrong one, just as 8 was only one valid answer for 2, 4, 6. This is called background bias or a spurious correlation, and it is why diverse training data matters.

Calibration

CalibrationExam favourite
Among all predictions made with confidence c, the fraction that is correct should be about c
A model that says 0.80 but is right only half the time is overconfident.

The lecturer stressed safety: if a model is used to save a life and acts on a prediction it is only 10% sure of, the cost of a mistake is severe. A trustworthy model must be accurate and also well calibrated. Modern deep networks tend to be overconfident. A simple fix, temperature scaling, divides the logits by one number T chosen on validation data (try the temperature dial in the SoftMax animation).

Going deeper: measuring calibration

Sort predictions into bins by confidence, for example 0.0 to 0.1, 0.1 to 0.2 and so on. In each bin compare the average confidence with the accuracy. The expected calibration error is the weighted average gap: ECE = Σb (nb ÷ N) |acc(b) − conf(b)|. A reliability diagram plots accuracy against confidence, and a perfect model lies on the diagonal. Guo and colleagues (2017) showed that modern networks such as ResNets are often overconfident, and that temperature scaling fixes much of it without changing which class is predicted.

Lesson 5 of 6

From AlexNet to Vision Transformers

The lecture traces the architectures that pushed ImageNet error down year after year.

ModelYearKey idea
AlexNet2012Deep CNN trained on GPUs with ReLU, dropout regularisation and data augmentation. A large jump over hand made features.
VGG2014Only small 3 × 3 filters, stacked deep. Repeated small filters reach as far as large ones with fewer weights and more non linearity.
GoogLeNet (Inception)2014Filters of several sizes side by side, because objects contain patterns of different sizes. 1 × 1 convolutions keep it cheap.
ResNet2015Shortcut connections, so each block learns a correction F(x) = H(x) − x. Made networks of 100 and more layers trainable.
Vision Transformer (ViT)2020Cut the image into patches and treat them as a sequence of tokens. Attention links distant parts directly.
Residual learningExam favourite
H(x) = F(x) + x
The shortcut passes x straight through. The layers only learn the difference F(x).

Why does this help? Making a plain network deeper does not automatically make it better. Very deep plain networks suffer from vanishing gradients and become hard to optimise, and even their training accuracy can get worse, which the ResNet authors called the degradation problem. A shortcut lets information and gradients flow straight through, so a block that has nothing useful to add can simply learn F(x) = 0.

The 1 × 1 convolution

The lecture set this as homework: an 80 × 80 × 128 feature map goes through a 1 × 1 convolution. What comes out? One student guessed 160 × 160 with twice the channels, which is not right. A 1 × 1 convolution with stride 1 keeps the height and width and mixes the channels at each pixel. The number of output channels equals the number of filters you choose. With 256 filters the output is 80 × 80 × 256, with 1 × 1 × 128 × 256 + 256 = 33,024 parameters. It was introduced in Network in Network (2013) and made popular by GoogLeNet as a cheap way to change the channel count.

ViT tokens
number of tokens = (HP) × (WP)
For a 224 × 224 image and 16 × 16 patches: 14 × 14 = 196 tokens.
Common mistake
Some models now report lower error than the estimated human error on ImageNet. That does not mean human level vision. Models still fail under unusual conditions, distribution shift, adversarial changes, occlusion, new categories and ambiguous scenes. Benchmark wins are specific to the benchmark.
Lesson 6 of 6

Designing a model: architecture, loss, optimisation

The lecturer asked what three dimensions every deep learning design has. A student guessed width and height. The answer: architecture, loss function and optimisation. The lecturer added that only about 1% of people work on optimisation, which needs a strong mathematical background, while most work goes into architectures and losses.

AxisQuestion it answersExamples
ArchitectureWhich functions can the model represent?Convolutions, residual blocks, attention, feature pyramids
LossWhat do we ask it to do?Cross entropy, Huber, focal loss (chapter 5.2)
OptimisationHow do we search for good weights?SGD, momentum, Adam, learning rate schedules
Gradient descentExam favourite
w ← w − η ∂L∂w
η is the learning rate. Too large and training jumps around, too small and it crawls.

Regularisation keeps a model from overfitting. A weight decay term lives inside the loss, as the lecture said. Dropout, data augmentation and early stopping also regularise, but they do it without any loss term.

One idea, four depths

A CNN looks at a picture through many tiny windows. Each window checks for a simple pattern such as an edge. Later layers combine the patterns into eyes, wheels and leaves, and the last layer turns all of it into a score for each possible answer.

A conv layer computes ReLU(W ∗ x + b) with k × k × Cin × Cout + Cout parameters. Pooling and stride shrink the maps and grow the receptive field. SoftMax turns logits into probabilities, cross entropy −ln ptrue is the loss, and its gradient p − y drives gradient descent.

Convolution builds in two inductive biases: locality and translation equivariance, with weights shared across positions. Residual connections make the network an ensemble of paths of many depths and keep the Jacobian close to the identity. ViTs drop most of the convolutional bias, so they need more data or strong augmentation, which is why hybrid designs and pre training matter.

Current research studies scaling laws relating data, parameters and compute, self supervised pre training (contrastive and masked image modelling), robustness to distribution shift, and calibration and uncertainty, including conformal prediction that gives sets of classes with a guaranteed error rate.
