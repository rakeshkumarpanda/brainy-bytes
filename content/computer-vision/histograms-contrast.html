Lesson 1 of 5

The image histogram

A histogram counts. For an 8 bit grey image it has 256 bars, one for each value from 0 to 255, and the height of bar i is the number of pixels with value i. Divide by the number of pixels and you get a probability distribution over intensities.

Histogram
h(i) = number of pixels with value i
i = 0, 1, …, 255. The bars add up to N = w × h.
Normalised histogram
p(i) = h(i)N
The values add up to 1.
Cumulative distributionExam favourite
CDF(r) = Σi ≤ r p(i)
A running total that climbs from 0 to 1.

How many, never where

A histogram throws away every position. Very different pictures can have exactly the same histogram, as the animation shows. This is the main limitation the lecture stresses.

Reading exposure from the histogram

Lesson 2 of 5

Brightness, contrast and dynamic range

The lecture gives a sentence worth memorising: brightness decides where the intensity distribution sits, contrast decides how widely it is spread, and dynamic range is how many distinct levels are used.

BrightnessExam favourite
B(I) = 1wh Σv=1h Σu=1w I(u, v)
The average intensity of all pixels.
RMS contrast
σ = √1wh Σ Σ (I(u, v) − B)²
The standard deviation of the intensities, the spread of the histogram.
Michelson contrast
C = Imax − IminImax + Imin
A change in luminance relative to the average luminance, from 0 to 1.
Linear contrast and brightness
g = αf + β, then clip to [0, 255]
α above 1 stretches the histogram, β shifts it.

Contrast is about the spread of the histogram, not the height of its peak. A narrow central hump means low contrast. A wide spread means good contrast. Piling values at both ends (0 and 255) gives very high contrast but loses detail, and the lecture warns it can start to look like noise.

Dynamic range counts the distinct levels an image uses. More levels give smoother gradations, which is why TVs advertise HDR (high dynamic range). An image with few levels looks posterised.

One idea, four depths

Brightness is how much light the whole picture gives off. Contrast is how different the light and dark parts are. A foggy day has low contrast: everything is a similar grey.

Brightness is the mean of the pixel values and contrast is often measured by their standard deviation. Adding a constant changes the mean but not the standard deviation. Multiplying by α changes both the mean and the standard deviation by a factor α (before clipping).

Perceived contrast is not linear in intensity. Weber's law says the smallest visible change is roughly proportional to the background level, which is why gamma and log curves, which expand dark values, often look more natural than linear stretching. Sensor noise also grows with signal, so stretching dark regions amplifies noise.

HDR imaging merges several exposures into a radiance map with far more range than a display can show, then uses tone mapping (global curves or local operators) to compress it. Learned tone mapping and exposure correction networks are an active research area, along with evaluating them without a ground truth image.

Lesson 3 of 5

Point operations: negative, log, gamma and slicing

A point operation changes each pixel using only its own value. It is a function s = T(r) from input level r to output level s, so it can be drawn as a curve. Point operations are the simplest form of image processing, and they are very fast because each pixel is handled once.

NegativeExam favourite
g(x, y) = L − f(x, y)
L is the maximum value, 255 for 8 bits. In OpenCV: img_neg = 255 − img.
Log transformExam favourite
s = c · log(1 + |r|)
Compresses large values more than small ones. Choose c = 255 ÷ log(256) so 255 maps to 255.
Gamma (power law)
s = 255 (r255)γ
γ below 1 brightens dark areas, γ above 1 darkens.
Grey level slicing
s = high if A ≤ r ≤ B, otherwise low (or unchanged)
Highlights one band of intensities.

When to use which

OperationUse it whenExample
NegativeDetail sits in dark areas and is easier to read invertedReading X ray films, mammograms
Linear stretchThe histogram is narrow but its shape is fineA foggy photo
LogA few very bright pixels hide everything elseAstronomy, the magnitude of a Fourier spectrum, satellite and medical images
GammaYou need a smooth, adjustable brighten or darkenDisplay correction, under or overexposed photos
SlicingOnly one intensity band mattersTissue in a scan, water in a satellite image, a material in inspection
Real world scenario: a radiologist looking for a small lesion

A large dark region in a scan can dominate an AI model, which then learns mostly about that region and misses small important structures. The lecture describes grey level slicing as a fix: keep only the intensity band of the tissue of interest and set everything else to a flat constant. A flat region has no pattern, so the model cannot be biased by it, and the lesion stands out, just as it does for a radiologist when a region is pre highlighted.

Lesson 4 of 5

Histogram equalisation

Histogram equalisation is a point operation whose curve is chosen automatically from the image itself. It spreads crowded values over the full 0 to 255 range, so neighbouring intensities that were hard to tell apart become clearly different. The lecture adds an honest caveat: it does not make the histogram perfectly flat, it makes better use of the available range.

Equalisation mappingExam favourite
s = round[ CDF(r) − CDFmin1 − CDFmin × 255 ]
CDFmin is the smallest non zero CDF value, the CDF of the darkest level present. In general replace 255 by L − 1.
Worked example from the lecture
  1. The image is four rows of 80, 100, 120 and 140. It uses only the range 80 to 140, so it looks grey.
  2. Histogram: each level has 4 of the 16 pixels, so each probability is 4 ÷ 16 = 0.25.
  3. CDF: 80 → 0.25, 100 → 0.50, 120 → 0.75, 140 → 1.00. So CDFmin = 0.25.
  4. Map 100: (0.50 − 0.25) ÷ (1 − 0.25) × 255 = 0.333 × 255 = 85. In the same way 80 → 0, 120 → 170, 140 → 255.
  5. The output uses the full range: black, dark grey, light grey and white.
Going deeper: why the CDF flattens the histogram

Treat the intensity as a random variable R with cumulative distribution F. Define S = F(R). For any t between 0 and 1, P(S ≤ t) = P(R ≤ F−1(t)) = F(F−1(t)) = t. A variable whose probability of being below t equals t is uniform on [0, 1]. This is the probability integral transform. Images are discrete, so all pixels of one value must move together and the result is only roughly flat. That is the caveat in the notes.

Remember
Global equalisation can over boost noise in flat regions and wash out areas that were already fine. CLAHE (contrast limited adaptive histogram equalisation) equalises small tiles separately and limits how steep the curve can get. It is the version most used in medical imaging and as a preprocessing step.
Lesson 5 of 5

Why this matters for AI models

It is tempting to think a deep network will cope with any input. The lecture asked what happens if a very dark, low contrast image goes straight into a model. The class answers were all correct: objects cannot be identified, features are not extracted, and the dominant dark region biases what the model learns.

  • Edges and gradients are the raw material of HOG, SIFT and the first layer of a CNN. In a squashed histogram, neighbouring surfaces get almost the same value, so their edges are weak or vanish.
  • A model trained on well exposed photos sees a dark photo as a different distribution. Normalising contrast is a cheap way to reduce that shift.
  • In medicine, astronomy and remote sensing, the important detail is often in a small part of a huge dynamic range. Log transforms and slicing make it visible to people and to models.

Narrow hump, good shapeLinear stretchSpreads values, keeps their order and spacing

Common mistake
These methods change the input distribution. Apply exactly the same preprocessing at training time and at deployment, or the model sees two different worlds.
