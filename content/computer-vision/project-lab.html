The project brief

This chapter rebuilds your mini project from start to finish and explains every number in it. The question was simple: if the pictures get blurry, how much worse does a detector become, and does sharpening fix it?

ItemChoice in the notebook
DatasetCOCO128: 128 images, 929 labelled objects, 80 classes, 2 images with no objects
ModelPretrained YOLO11n (fused): 100 layers, 2,616,248 parameters, 6.5 GFLOPs

The design is a controlled experiment. Only one thing changes between stages, the pixels. The model, the labels, the image size and the metric code stay fixed, so any change in the scores must come from the image processing.

The four stages
  1. Baseline. Write a dataset YAML pointing at the original images and run model.val.
  2. Blur. Read every image, blur it, save it under the same file name, copy the labels unchanged, evaluate again.
  3. Sharpen. Read every blurred image, sharpen it, save, copy labels, evaluate again.
  4. Analyse. Put the twelve numbers in one table and explain the changes.

One function builds a processed copy of the dataset from any image operation, so Stage 2 and Stage 3 differ only in the function passed in. The labels are copied, not recomputed, because blur and sharpening do not move objects. YOLO labels store each box as class, centre x, centre y, width and height, all as fractions of the image size, and the image size does not change.

Remember
The labels are copied unchanged on purpose. If you resized or cropped the images, the boxes would have to change too. Blur and sharpening keep every pixel in place.

What the four numbers mean

PrecisionOf the boxes the detector reported, what share were right?

Chapter 5.4 derives these in full. The point to keep here: mAP50-95 rewards tight boxes, so anything that blurs object edges should hurt it the most.

Lesson 2 of 5

Stage 2: the Gaussian blur

cv2.GaussianBlur(image, (9, 9), 0) replaces every pixel by a weighted average of the 9 × 9 square around it. Pixels near the centre count most, following the bell shaped Gaussian curve. Passing 0 for σ lets OpenCV choose σ from the kernel size.

Gaussian weightExam favourite
G(x, y) ∝ e−(x² + y²) ÷ (2σ²)
Normalised so the 81 weights add to 1. A flat area keeps its brightness.
σ chosen by OpenCVExam favourite
σ = 0.3 ((k − 1) ÷ 2 − 1) + 0.8
k = 9 gives σ = 0.3 × 3 + 0.8 = 1.7. For odd sizes up to 7 OpenCV uses fixed binomial weights instead.
The 9 weights along one axis
  1. σ = 1.7, positions −4 to 4.
  2. Unnormalised weights e−x² ÷ 5.78: 0.063, 0.211, 0.500, 0.841, 1, 0.841, 0.500, 0.211, 0.063.
  3. Divide by their sum 4.23: 0.0148, 0.0498, 0.1183, 0.1988, 0.2364, 0.1988, 0.1183, 0.0498, 0.0148.
  4. The 2D kernel is this row times this column, because the Gaussian is separable.

Separable means the 2D blur can be done as a horizontal pass followed by a vertical pass. That costs 9 + 9 = 18 multiplications per pixel instead of 81, and OpenCV does it this way.

What blur does to a detector

Blur is a low pass filter. It keeps slow changes such as large shapes and colour regions and removes fast changes such as edges, thin lines and fine texture. A detector's early layers are edge and texture finders, so blur weakens the evidence they pass on. Small, thin and distant objects suffer most, because nearly all of their evidence is fine detail. A bus filling half the frame survives a 9 × 9 blur. A tennis racket 20 pixels wide does not.

In the exam
Your analysis found exactly this. In 000000000531.jpg detections fell from 10 to 4 and the tennis racket and distant players disappeared. In 000000000260.jpg detections fell from 8 to 3. Blur also caused class confusion: in 000000000049.jpg a horse was labelled as a dog with score 0.30.

Stage 3: the sharpening kernel

The notebook applies cv2.filter2D(blurred, −1, K) with

The project kernelExam favourite
K = 0−10−15−10−10 = 000010000 − 0101−41010
The identity minus the 4 neighbour Laplacian. The weights add to 5 − 4 = 1, so flat areas are unchanged.

Written out for one pixel B(x, y) of the blurred image:

Sharpened pixel
S(x, y) = 5B(x, y) − B(x + 1, y) − B(x − 1, y) − B(x, y + 1) − B(x, y − 1)
Then rounded and clipped to 0 to 255, because ddepth = −1 keeps the 8 bit image type.

Three ways to read the same kernel

  • Image minus Laplacian. The Laplacian measures how much a pixel differs from its neighbours, which is large at edges. Subtracting it pushes edges further apart in brightness.
  • Unsharp masking. S = B + 4 (B − average of the 4 neighbours). The average is a tiny blur, B minus that blur is the detail, and the kernel adds 4 times the detail back. The general form S = B + a (B − blur) gives this kernel at a = 4.
  • A high frequency boost. Along one axis the gain is 3 − 2 cos ω: 1 for flat areas (ω = 0), 3 at half the finest frequency, 5 at the finest.
Sharpening by hand
  1. Flat patch, every pixel 100: 5 × 100 − 4 × 100 = 100. Unchanged.
  2. A linear ramp 60, 100, 140 along a row: 3 × 100 − 60 − 140 = 100. Unchanged, the kernel only reacts to curvature.
  3. The dark side of an edge, centre 50 with neighbours 50, 50, 50, 200: 250 − 350 = −100, clipped to 0. That is the dark halo.
  4. The bright side, centre 200 with neighbours 200, 200, 200, 50: 1,000 − 650 = 350, clipped to 255. That is the bright halo.

Why sharpening cannot undo the blur

A linear filter multiplies each frequency by its gain. The 9 × 9 blur keeps only about 3 percent of the wave at half the finest frequency and about 0.5 percent of the finest. The kernel multiplies those by 3 and 5, giving about 9 and 3 percent. The detail is not hidden somewhere waiting to be revealed. Most of it is gone, and 8 bit rounding after the blur removed even more. Meanwhile any noise or JPEG artefact at those frequencies is boosted just as strongly as real detail.

Common mistake
filter2D computes correlation, not convolution, so the kernel is not flipped. For symmetric kernels such as this one the two are identical. The border default reflects the image without repeating the edge pixel (BORDER_REFLECT_101).
Going deeper: Could anything restore more?

If the blur kernel is known, deconvolution divides by the blur gain in the frequency domain. Plain division explodes where the gain is near zero, so the Wiener filter divides by gain² plus a noise to signal ratio, boosting only where the signal is still above the noise. Learned deblurring networks go further by filling in plausible detail from training data, with the same caution as super-resolution in chapter 7.1: plausible is not the same as true. The most reliable fix for a detector is usually to train it on blurred images, so it learns features that survive blur.

The precision puzzle

Precision went up under blur. That looks like the detector got better, but it reported fewer boxes, and the ones it kept were the confident, easy ones. Blur also removes fine texture, which is a common trigger for false alarms. Fewer false alarms in the denominator can lift precision even while real objects are missed.

Lesson 5 of 5

How sure can we be, and what to try next

A good analysis also says what the experiment cannot show. Five points matter here.

  1. The baseline saw these images in training. COCO128 is the first 128 images of COCO train2017, and the pretrained YOLO11n was trained on COCO train2017. The baseline scores are therefore optimistic. A fair test would use images from val2017.
  2. 128 images is a small sample. A difference of 0.01 in mAP may be within the variation you would see from a different set of 128 images. Bootstrapping, re-scoring many random resamples of the images, gives a confidence interval.
  3. P and R come from one confidence. Ultralytics reports mean precision and recall at the confidence where the mean F1 over classes is highest. That point moves between stages, so P and R compare different operating points. mAP does not have this problem.
  4. One blur level, one sharpening setting. A 9 × 9 blur is strong. The conclusion might differ for a 3 × 3 blur or for motion blur. The sharpening strength was not tuned.
  5. Validation settings. model.val uses a very low confidence threshold (0.001) so it can trace the full precision and recall curve. Example pictures from model.predict used 0.25, so they show fewer boxes than were scored.
Real world scenario: A city traffic camera

A council runs a vehicle and pedestrian detector on roadside cameras. On foggy mornings and with dirty lenses the images blur, and the count of pedestrians drops. Someone proposes adding a sharpening step. Following this project: measure mAP on a set of foggy frames with and without the step, check that false alarms from sensor noise do not rise, compare against simply fine tuning the detector on blurred frames, and apply the step only when a blur detector (for example a low variance of the Laplacian) says the frame is soft.

Your conclusion, to sharpen only images known to be blurred, matches the evidence: a cheap step with consistent small gains in recall and mAP on blurred input, at some cost in precision, and not something to apply to sharp images where it only adds noise.

One idea, four depths

We made photos blurry on purpose and asked a computer to find things in them. It found fewer things, especially small ones. Sharpening the blurry photos helped a little, but could not bring back details that were already smudged away.

A 9 × 9 Gaussian blur (σ = 1.7) cut recall from 0.589 to 0.536 and mAP50-95 from 0.503 to 0.443. The kernel [[0, −1, 0], [−1, 5, −1], [0, −1, 0]] won back about a fifth of each loss and lowered precision by amplifying noise.

Blur is a low pass filter whose gain at high frequencies is near zero, and the sharpening gain 3 − 2 cos ω cannot restore what is gone. mAP50-95 falls most because strict IoU thresholds expose uncertain box edges. P and R are reported at the max F1 confidence, so they compare shifting operating points.

Robustness to image corruptions is studied with benchmarks such as COCO-C and ImageNet-C, which apply blur, noise and weather at several strengths. The usual findings match this project: preprocessing gives small gains, while training with corruption augmentation or learned restoration jointly optimised with the detector gives larger ones. Evaluations should report confidence intervals and avoid train and test overlap.
