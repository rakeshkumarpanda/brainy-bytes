Lesson 1 of 6

Marr's stages of vision

David Marr, a British neuroscientist working at MIT, described vision as a chain of representations rather than one big jump from pixels to meaning. His book Vision (published in 1982) still shapes how the subject is taught. The idea is simple: each stage removes some ambiguity and hands a cleaner description to the next.

StageWhat it containsQuestion it answers
1. Input imagePixel intensities, colour, local contrastWhat signal did the camera capture?
2. Primal sketchEdges, bars, blobs, ends, zero crossings, groups, boundariesWhere does structure begin and end?
3. 2½-D sketchDepth and surface orientation of the visible surfaces, discontinuitiesHow are the visible surfaces arranged relative to me?
4. 3D modelVolumes and parts in an object centred frameWhat is the object and what is its full shape?

Why "two and a half" D

The 2½-D sketch knows more than a flat picture, because it has depth and the tilt of each visible surface. It knows less than a full 3D model, because it only describes what the camera can see from where it stands. Look at a basketball: you can tell the near surface is curved and which parts are closer, but you cannot see the back. That viewpoint dependence is the half.

In the exam
Depth is not volume. A depth map gives the distance to each visible surface. A 3D model describes the whole object, including the hidden back. The exam asks this as "why is it called 2½-D".

An edge is evidence, not an answer

An edge in the primal sketch can be an object boundary, a change in surface direction, a change in material, a shadow or a change in lighting. The edge only says "something changes here". Later stages decide what.

One idea, four depths

When you draw a cat, you first draw outlines, then shade to show which parts stick out, and only then does it look like a real cat. Marr said computers should see in the same order: outlines first, then shape, then the whole object.

Each stage is a different data structure. The image is a 2D array of intensities. The primal sketch is a set of tokens (edge segments with position, orientation and contrast). The 2½-D sketch is a map giving depth and a surface normal for every visible point. The 3D model is a hierarchy of parts, each a simple volume such as a generalised cylinder.

Marr also proposed three levels for explaining any information processing system: the computational level (what problem is solved and why), the algorithmic level (which representation and procedure), and the implementational level (the hardware, neurons or silicon). This is useful for analysing any vision method: first state what it computes, then how, then on what.

Deep networks do not build explicit Marr stages, yet probing studies find intermediate layers that encode edges, then surface properties such as depth and normals, then object identity. Current work on monocular depth estimation and 3D aware generative models can be read as learning the 2½-D and 3D stages end to end.

What makes a good feature

A feature is a distinctive, useful property of an image region: a corner, an edge, a texture, a colour change, a blob. Pixels alone are usually not enough. A feature is computed from pixels to carry information that helps with matching, tracking, alignment, reconstruction or recognition.

Three properties of a good feature point

PropertyMeaningBad exampleGood example
DistinctiveIts neighbourhood looks unlike most other placesA patch of plain blue skyThe corner of a window
Stable (repeatable)It is found again in a second photo of the same sceneA reflection that moves when you moveA painted letter on a sign
InvariantIts description stays the same under scale, rotation and lighting changesRaw pixel valuesA normalised histogram of gradient directions

Flat, edge and corner

Imagine sliding a small window over the image. In a flat region the window looks the same wherever you move it, so you cannot tell where you are. On an edge it changes when you move across the edge but not along it, so you only know your position in one direction. At a corner it changes whichever way you move, so the position is pinned down. This is why corner detectors (Harris, FAST) and blob detectors (SIFT) are the classic keypoint finders.

In the exam
Feature detection finds where the interesting points are. Feature description turns the neighbourhood of each point into a vector of numbers so points can be compared. SIFT does both. HOG is mainly a descriptor.
Real world scenario: stitching a phone panorama

When you sweep your phone for a panorama, the app finds keypoints in each frame, describes them, and matches descriptors between neighbouring frames. Three or four good matches fix how one frame lines up with the next. If you sweep across a white wall, there are no distinctive points, the matches fail, and the panorama tears. That is the "flat region" problem in real life.

Lesson 3 of 6

Image gradients

A gradient measures how fast brightness changes and in which direction. It is the basic signal behind edges, HOG, SIFT, optical flow and even the first layer of a CNN.

For a function of one variable the derivative is the slope. An image has two directions, so it has two partial derivatives. Pixels are discrete, so we replace the derivative by a difference of neighbours.

Central difference
Gx ≈ I(x + 1, y) − I(x − 1, y)
Often divided by 2. The kernel is [−1, 0, 1].
Gradient magnitudeExam favourite
|∇I| = √Gx² + Gy²
How strong the edge is.
Gradient directionExam favourite
θ = atan2(Gy, Gx)
Points across the edge, from dark to bright.
Sobel kernels
Kx = −101−202−101   Ky = −1−2−1000121
Sobel is a central difference in one direction combined with a small smoothing [1, 2, 1] in the other direction, which makes it less sensitive to noise. Prewitt uses [1, 1, 1] instead.
Worked example: Sobel on a vertical edge
  1. Take the 3 × 3 neighbourhood 101050101050101050.
  2. Multiply by Kx value by value and add: (−1)(10) + 0 + (1)(50) + (−2)(10) + 0 + (2)(50) + (−1)(10) + 0 + (1)(50) = 160.
  3. Multiply by Ky: the top row and bottom row are the same, so they cancel and Gy = 0.
  4. Magnitude √(160² + 0²) = 160. Direction atan2(0, 160) = 0°, so the arrow points right, from the dark side to the bright side.
Common mistake
With image coordinates the y axis points down. An angle of 90° therefore means "brightness increases downwards". Many textbooks draw y upwards, so check which convention a question uses.
Lesson 4 of 6

SIFT: keypoints that survive zoom and rotation

SIFT (Scale Invariant Feature Transform, David Lowe, 2004) finds distinctive points and describes them so the same point can be matched between two photos, even if one is zoomed, rotated or lit differently. The notes stress that it is invariant, or at least robust, to scale and rotation. It does not promise that every point matches after any change.

The four steps

  1. Scale space search. Blur the image with Gaussians of growing width σ and subtract neighbouring blurs. This difference of Gaussians (DoG) responds strongly to blobs whose size matches σ. Keypoints are the maxima and minima across position and scale.
  2. Keypoint refinement. Throw away points with low contrast and points that lie along edges, because they are unstable.
  3. Orientation. Build a histogram of gradient directions around the point and take the strongest one. All later measurements are made relative to this angle, which is what makes SIFT rotation invariant.
  4. Descriptor. Split the neighbourhood into a 4 × 4 grid of cells, and in each cell make an 8 bin histogram of gradient directions. That gives 4 × 4 × 8 = 128 numbers, normalised to reduce the effect of lighting.
Gaussian blur
L(x, y, σ) = G(x, y, σ) ∗ I(x, y)
G is a Gaussian of width σ and ∗ is convolution.
Difference of Gaussians
DoG = L(x, y, kσ) − L(x, y, σ)
Close to (k − 1) σ² ∇²G ∗ I, the scale normalised Laplacian.
Descriptor lengthExam favourite
4 × 4 cells × 8 bins = 128
Each cell is a small histogram of gradient directions.
Lowe ratio test
d1d2 < 0.8
d1 and d2 are the distances to the nearest and second nearest descriptor. Accept the match only if the best is clearly better than the runner up.
Going deeper: why the peak tells you the size

For a bright disc of radius r, the scale normalised Laplacian of Gaussian at the centre of the disc is largest when σ = r ÷ √2. If the photo is zoomed by a factor s, the disc has radius sr and the peak moves to sσ. So the detector reports a size that grows with the zoom, and a descriptor measured over a region proportional to that size sees the same content in both photos. This is the precise meaning of scale invariance.

Lesson 5 of 6

HOG: describing shape with edge directions

HOG (Histogram of Oriented Gradients, Dalal and Triggs, 2005) describes the shape of an object through the directions of its edges. It was built for detecting pedestrians and worked far better than earlier features on that task.

How HOG is built

  1. Compute Gx and Gy for every pixel, then the magnitude and direction.
  2. Cut the detection window (64 × 128 pixels for a standing person) into cells of 8 × 8 pixels.
  3. In each cell, make a histogram with 9 bins covering 0° to 180°. Each pixel votes for its direction with a weight equal to its magnitude, split between the two nearest bins.
  4. Group cells into overlapping blocks of 2 × 2 cells and normalise each block, so a change in lighting or contrast does not change the numbers much.
  5. Join all block vectors into one long descriptor and feed it to a classifier, usually a linear support vector machine.
Bin width
width = 180°number of bins
9 bins give 20° per bin, with centres at 10°, 30°, 50° and so on.
Vote split
share to the upper bin = θ − clowerbin width
The rest goes to the lower bin. Both shares are multiplied by the magnitude.
Descriptor lengthExam favourite
(nx − 1)(ny − 1) × 4 × B
For 64 × 128 with 8 × 8 cells: 7 × 15 = 105 blocks, each 4 × 9 = 36 numbers, so 3,780 in total.
Block normalisation (L2)
v ← v√‖v‖² + ε²
ε is a small number that avoids dividing by zero.

Why it works and where it fails

HOG looks at edge directions, not colours, so a person in a red coat and a person in a blue coat give similar descriptors. The cells allow a little movement of each body part without changing the histogram much. It struggles when contrast is very low, the object bends or changes pose (sitting, lying down), the object is partly hidden, the background is very cluttered, or the object has few strong edges.

Lesson 6 of 6

Why classical features still matter

CNNs and transformers are more accurate on most benchmarks, so why learn SIFT and HOG? The notes give four reasons.

ReasonWhat it meansExample
EfficiencyLittle memory, little power, no training data neededA doorbell camera, a microcontroller, a drone
InterpretabilityYou can say why the system firedA HOG glyph shows the shoulder edges that made the detector fire
Domain knowledgeIf you know the key pattern, you can measure it directlyFinding a logo, a QR code, the straight edges of a document
Working with deep modelsThey can prepare or support a learned modelKeypoints for camera tracking in AR, matching in 3D reconstruction

Classical keypoints are still used inside modern systems. Building 3D models from internet photo collections, which the second lecture shows with the city of Rome, relies on matching keypoints across thousands of photos and then triangulating the matched points.

One idea, four depths

Some jobs need a big powerful machine and some need a pocket knife. Hand made features are the pocket knife: small, quick and easy to understand.

A HOG plus SVM detector runs in real time on a CPU and needs a few thousand labelled windows. A CNN detector is more accurate but needs a GPU and tens of thousands of labelled images. Choose by accuracy needs, hardware and data.

Learned descriptors (such as SuperPoint and the matchers built on it) now outperform SIFT in many matching benchmarks, but SIFT remains a strong baseline because it is training free and behaves predictably on new domains such as medical or aerial images.

An open research theme is to combine the inductive biases of classical features, such as explicit scale and rotation handling, with learned representations, through equivariant networks or differentiable versions of classical pipelines.
