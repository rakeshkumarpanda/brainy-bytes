Lesson 1 of 5

An image is a discrete signal

A digital image is a grid of samples of the light that reached the camera. Each sample is a pixel, and each pixel stores numbers. For an 8 bit grey image, 0 is black, 255 is white and everything in between is a shade of grey.

Image as a function
I : {0, …, H − 1} × {0, …, W − 1} → {0, …, 255}C
Each position (row, column) maps to C numbers.
Stored valuesExam favourite
H × W × C
For the lecture example 16 × 16 × 3 = 768.

Low level and high level vision

The lecture separates two layers of work. Low level vision deals with the signal: brightness, contrast, colour, noise, edges, blur, sharpness, resolution. High level vision deals with meaning: classification, detection, scenes, activities, captions, answering questions. Information flows upwards: pixel changes give edges, edges give shapes, shapes give objects, and objects with their relationships give scenes. That is why low level choices such as compression and colour handling can change high level accuracy.

Before an image reaches a model

The lecture lists five things to check every time: channel order, pixel range, data type, normalisation and layout. A model trained on RGB values scaled to 0 to 1, laid out as channels first, will give nonsense if it receives BGR values from 0 to 255 laid out channels last.

Scale to 0 to 1
x′ = x255
Turns 8 bit integers into floats.
Standardise per channel
x″ = x′ − μcσc
μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of channel c over the training data.
Common mistake
Compression and normalisation are different things. Compression makes files smaller for storage and transfer. Normalisation changes the scale of numbers so a model can learn. The notes list this as a common confusion.

Colour models: RGB, CMYK, HSL and YCbCr

A computer does not know the word "orange". A colour model says which numbers describe a colour and how they combine.

ModelChannelsHow it worksWhere it is used
RGBRed, green, blueAdditive: start from black and add light. All three at full give white.Screens, cameras, most image files
CMYKCyan, magenta, yellow, key (black)Subtractive: start from white paper and add ink that absorbs light.Printing
HSLHue, saturation, lightnessSeparates which colour, how pure, and how light.Photo editing, colour based filtering
YCbCrLuma Y, blue difference Cb, red difference CrSeparates brightness from colour.JPEG and video codecs
BGRBlue, green, redSame as RGB with the order reversed.OpenCV in memory
Grey from RGB (BT.601)Exam favourite
Y = 0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B
Green gets the biggest weight because the eye is most sensitive to it. OpenCV uses these weights.
RGB to CMYK
K = 1 − max(R′, G′, B′),   C = 1 − R′ − K1 − K
R′ = R ÷ 255. M and Y use G′ and B′ the same way.
HSL lightness and saturation
L = max + min2,   S = max − min1 − |2L − 1|
max and min of R′, G′, B′. Hue is the angle on the colour wheel.
Colour difference channels (JPEG)
Cb = 128 − 0.1687 R − 0.3313 G + 0.5 B
Cr = 128 + 0.5 R − 0.4187 G − 0.0813 B
A pure grey has Cb = Cr = 128.
Real world scenario: picking ripe tomatoes on a conveyor belt

A sorting machine must separate red tomatoes from green ones under factory lights that change through the day. In RGB, a darker red tomato has smaller numbers in every channel, so a fixed threshold on R fails when the lights dim. In HSL the hue of a red tomato stays near 0° whether the light is bright or dim. Thresholding on hue and ignoring lightness makes the sorter far more robust. Choosing the right colour model is often the cheapest fix in a real project.

Lesson 3 of 5

Why and how images are compressed

Raw images and video are huge, as the first chapter showed. Compression reduces storage, bandwidth, server cost and transfer time, and makes streaming and mobile apps possible. It works by removing redundancy: neighbouring pixels are similar, colour channels are correlated, smooth regions repeat the same values, and the eye does not notice some details.

Compression ratioExam favourite
ratio = original sizecompressed size
A ratio of 15 means the file is 15 times smaller.
Bits per pixel
bpp = 8 × file bytesH × W
Raw colour is 24 bpp. A good JPEG photo is often 1 to 2 bpp.
Lossless (PNG)Lossy (JPEG)
RecoveryExact, every pixel comes backApproximate, some information is gone for ever
File sizeLargerMuch smaller
Best forText, diagrams, screenshots, transparency, medical dataPhotographs
Repeated savingNo changeQuality can drop each time
Typical damageNoneBlocks, blur, ringing near edges, lost fine texture
  • Spatial redundancy is inside one picture: a smooth sky, a repeated texture.
  • Temporal redundancy is between frames of a video: a still background, a slowly moving person.
Common mistake
For medical, legal and industrial images, use lossless or very light compression. The fine edges that JPEG throws away can be the tumour boundary, the digit on a number plate or the crack in a weld.
Lesson 4 of 5

The JPEG pipeline and the DCT

The lectures describe JPEG in five steps. Decompression runs them backwards.

  1. Colour transform. RGB becomes YCbCr, separating brightness (Y) from colour (Cb, Cr).
  2. Downsampling. Cb and Cr are stored at lower resolution, because the eye is far more sensitive to brightness than to colour. In the common 4:2:0 scheme each colour channel keeps one value per 2 × 2 pixels.
  3. Discrete cosine transform (DCT). Each 8 × 8 block is written as a mix of 64 cosine patterns, from flat (low frequency) to fine checkerboards (high frequency).
  4. Quantisation. Each coefficient is divided by a number from a table and rounded. Low frequencies get small divisors and keep their detail. High frequencies get large divisors and often become zero. This is the only step that loses information.
  5. Encoding. The coefficients are read in a zig zag order so the zeros bunch together, then run length and Huffman coding store them compactly.
2D DCT of an 8 × 8 blockExam favourite
F(u, v) = ¼ C(u) C(v) Σx=07 Σy=07 f(x, y) cos (2x + 1)uπ16 cos (2y + 1)vπ16
C(0) = 1 ÷ √2 and C(k) = 1 otherwise. f is the pixel value minus 128.
QuantisationExam favourite
Fq(u, v) = round(F(u, v)Q(u, v))
Decoding multiplies back by Q. The rounding error is the loss.
The DC coefficient
F(0, 0) = 18 Σ Σ f(x, y) = 8 × mean
The top left coefficient is just 8 times the block average after subtracting 128.
Chroma subsampling 4:2:0
data kept = 1 + ¼ + ¼3 = ½
Y keeps every pixel, Cb and Cr keep a quarter each.
Worked example: a flat block of value 140
  1. Subtract 128: every value becomes 12.
  2. A flat block has no variation, so every coefficient except the DC one is 0.
  3. DC = 8 × mean = 8 × 12 = 96.
  4. The standard table has Q(0, 0) = 16, so the stored value is round(96 ÷ 16) = 6. One small number replaces 64 pixels.
Going deeper: why cosines and not some other pattern

For natural images, neighbouring pixels are strongly correlated. For such signals the DCT comes close to the Karhunen Loeve transform, the transform that packs the most energy into the fewest coefficients. Cosines also avoid the jumps at the block edges that a plain Fourier transform would create, because the DCT implicitly mirrors the block. The price of working on separate 8 × 8 blocks is the blocking artefact at high compression, which is one reason newer formats use larger and overlapping transforms.

Video compression: I, P and B frames

A video is a sequence of frames, and neighbouring frames are usually almost the same. Codecs (H.264, H.265, AV1 and earlier H.261 to H.263) store some full frames and predict the rest.

FrameHow it is codedStrengthWeakness
I (intra)On its own, like a JPEGCan be decoded alone, good for seeking and recovering from errorsLargest
P (predicted)From an earlier frame, using motion vectors and the leftover differenceMuch smaller than IErrors carry forward until the next I frame
B (bi predicted)From an earlier and a later frameSmallestNeeds a future frame, which adds delay and memory
Extra delay from B frames
delay ≈ longest run of B framesfps seconds
Each B frame in a row makes the decoder wait one more frame time for its future reference.

Why not use only B frames

Because a B frame needs the future, the encoder must look ahead and the decoder must buffer frames before it can show anything. That is fine for a film on a streaming service, where the whole file exists. It is not fine for a video call, a live broadcast, controlling a drone or a car's perception system, where every millisecond of delay matters. Live systems use I and P frames with few or no B frames.

Real world scenario: a city with a thousand surveillance cameras

Sending every frame from every camera to the cloud needs huge bandwidth and storage and raises privacy risks. The lecture suggests processing near the camera (edge processing): detect people, vehicles or unusual motion locally, send only the important frames or just the metadata, and compress empty scenes heavily. The risk is that aggressive compression can destroy evidence, such as the face of a fast moving person. So the compression level should depend on motion speed, event importance, the evidence quality the law needs, resolution, storage budget and real time limits.

One idea, four depths

A flip book where most pages are almost the same can be saved by drawing the first page fully and then only writing down what moved on each new page.

P frames store motion vectors for blocks plus a residual image. The residual is compressed like a JPEG. B frames average a forward and a backward prediction, which handles objects that appear from behind something.

The encoder balances rate and distortion. It picks motion vectors, block sizes and quantisation to minimise D + λR, where D is the distortion, R is the bit rate and λ sets the trade off. The GOP structure trades compression against random access, error resilience and latency.

Learned video codecs replace hand designed motion estimation and transforms with neural networks trained end to end on the rate distortion objective. Research also studies compression for machines: codecs tuned so that a detector, not a human, gets the best accuracy per bit.
