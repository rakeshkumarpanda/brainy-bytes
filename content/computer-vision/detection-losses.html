Lesson 1 of 5

Giving every anchor a target

Detection is supervised learning. Annotators drew the true boxes and classes, but there are only a few of them, while the network makes a prediction for ten thousand or more anchors. Target assignment decides which true box, if any, each anchor must learn.

  • Match each anchor to the true box it overlaps most, measured by IoU.
  • If that IoU is high enough the anchor is positive: it gets the object's class and a box target.
  • If it is low the anchor is background (class 0) and gets no box target.
  • Anchors in between are ignored, so borderline cases do not confuse training.
DetectorPositiveBackground
RetinaNetIoU ≥ 0.5IoU < 0.4
Faster R-CNN region proposalsIoU > 0.7 (or the best anchor for an object)IoU < 0.3

A student asked whether Manhattan distance could replace Euclidean distance for matching. The lecturer said any distance can be used if it suits the problem. In practice most detectors use IoU because it is scale invariant: a 5 pixel centre error is huge for a 20 pixel box and tiny for a 500 pixel box, and IoU sees that difference while a plain distance does not.

The training objective

Per-anchor lossExam favourite
L = 1N Σanchors [ α Lcls + β δ(positive) Lloc ]
δ is 1 for a positive anchor and 0 otherwise, so background anchors only pay the class loss. N is often the number of positive anchors.

Two losses, one for what and one for where, added together. The rest of this chapter is about choosing each one well: a box loss that is not thrown by outliers, and a class loss that is not swamped by background.

Lesson 2 of 5

Box loss: MSE, MAE and Huber

Mean squared error
MSE = 1n Σ (ŷi − yi)²
Squares the error, so large errors are punished far more: 0.1² = 0.01 but 10² = 100.
Mean absolute error
MAE = 1n Σ |ŷi − yi|
Grows linearly, so outliers matter less. Its best constant guess is the median, MSE's is the mean.
Huber (Smooth L1)Exam favourite
Huber(e) = ½e² if |e| ≤ 1,   |e| − ½ otherwise
e is prediction minus target, not Euler's number. The general form uses a threshold δ.
Huber slope
∂Huber ÷ ∂e = clip(e, −1, 1)
Proportional for small errors, capped for large ones.

Why not plain MSE for boxes?

The class gave the right instinct: large outliers should not dominate training. The lecturer explained why that matters early on. At the start every box is a rough guess, so MSE hands out huge penalties and the model does not learn smoothly. Her comparison: giving a full exam in the second class, before any introduction. Feedback should be smooth, hence the name smooth L1.

The deeper reason is the gradient. With MSE the push on each box is proportional to its error, so one box with error 10 pushes harder than all the good boxes together, and training becomes unstable. Huber caps the push at 1.

One bad box among four (the slide example)
  1. Errors 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 10.
  2. Squared: 0.04, 0.09, 0.16 and 100. The good boxes total 0.29, so the bad one contributes 100 ÷ 0.29 ≈ 345 times more.
  3. Huber: 0.02, 0.045, 0.08 and 10 − 0.5 = 9.5.
  4. Gradients: with ½e² they are 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 10, so the outlier is 11 times the rest. With Huber they are 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 1, only 1.1 times.
Going deeper: Huber is smooth and convex

At |e| = 1 the two pieces meet: ½ × 1² = 0.5 and 1 − ½ = 0.5. Their slopes also meet: e = 1 and sign(e) = 1. So the loss has no kink, unlike MAE at 0. Its slope clip(e, −1, 1) never decreases, which makes the loss convex with a single minimum at e = 0. A student asked if 0 was a saddle point. It is not: a saddle needs directions going both up and down, and a convex function has none. MSE is the maximum likelihood loss for Gaussian noise, MAE for Laplacian (heavier tailed) noise, and Huber sits between them.

Why normalise

The lecture example: age runs from 20 to 30 while income runs into crores. Without scaling, income dominates every distance. Min-max scaling maps each feature to [0, 1]: x′ = (x − min) ÷ (max − min). For boxes the same idea appears as anchor relative encoding from the previous chapter: dividing by the anchor size makes a target mean the same thing for tiny and huge objects.

Lesson 3 of 5

Class imbalance and cross entropy

Picture a beach photo: one third sky, sand at the bottom, two people. Almost every anchor lands on sky, sky, sand, background. Each of these easy negatives has a tiny loss, but there are tens of thousands of them, and together they overwhelm the few anchors that sit on real objects.

How easy negatives swamp the loss
  1. 100,000 easy background anchors, each classified correctly with pt = 0.99. 10 hard object anchors with pt = 0.10.
  2. Cross entropy per easy anchor: −ln 0.99 = 0.01005. Total 100,000 × 0.01005 ≈ 1,005.
  3. Per hard anchor: −ln 0.10 = 2.303. Total 10 × 2.303 ≈ 23.
  4. The easy background owns 1,005 ÷ 1,028 ≈ 97.8% of the loss. Training mostly learns to keep saying background.

Four standard remedies

RemedyWhat it doesExample
Subsample negativesUse only some background anchorsSSD keeps 3 negatives per positive, the Faster R-CNN RPN samples 256 anchors per image
Down-weight negativesKeep all, but give background a smaller weightα-balanced cross entropy
Online hard example miningCompute every loss, keep only the hardest negativesOHEM (Shrivastava and colleagues, 2016)
Focal lossReshape the loss so easy examples contribute almost nothingRetinaNet (Lin and colleagues, 2017)

A student asked whether focal loss and hard mining are the same. The lecturer's answer: the idea is the same, the process differs. Mining is a selection that keeps or drops each example. Focal loss is a loss function that gives every example a continuous weight. She also compared it with token pruning in large language models, where unimportant tokens are removed to save computation. Pruning deletes, focal loss re-weights.

Entropy and cross entropy

Entropy
H(p) = −Σk pk log pk
Average surprise. Highest when all outcomes are equally likely, zero when the outcome is certain. The lecturer's picture: a messy table has high entropy, a tidy one low.
Binary cross entropyExam favourite
BCE = −[ y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p) ]
If y = 1 only the first term is active, if y = 0 only the second.
pt notation
pt = p if y = 1,   1 − p if y = 0,   so   BCE = −ln pt
pt is the probability given to the correct answer.
Going deeper: cross entropy is maximum likelihood

Model a label as Bernoulli(p): P(y | p) = py(1 − p)1−y. Its negative log is exactly BCE, so minimising BCE is maximum likelihood estimation. For distributions, H(p, q) = −Σ p log q = H(p) + KL(p ‖ q). The data fixes H(p), so minimising cross entropy minimises the KL divergence, which is zero only when the model matches the data.

Focal loss and the RetinaNet bias trick

Focal lossExam favourite
FL(pt) = −αt (1 − pt)γ ln pt
The factor (1 − pt)γ goes to 0 for easy, confident examples and stays near 1 for hard ones. γ = 0 gives cross entropy. RetinaNet used γ = 2 and α = 0.25 for the foreground class.

With γ = 2 a well classified example at pt = 0.9 loses 100 times its cross entropy, while a hard one at 0.1 keeps 81% of it.

Focal loss rescues the beach photo
  1. Same setting: 100,000 easy anchors at 0.99, 10 hard ones at 0.10, γ = 2.
  2. Easy: (1 − 0.99)² × 0.01005 = 0.0001 × 0.01005 ≈ 1.005 × 10−6 each, 0.1005 in total.
  3. Hard: (0.9)² × 2.303 = 0.81 × 2.303 ≈ 1.865 each, 18.65 in total.
  4. The background now owns 0.1005 ÷ 18.75 ≈ 0.54% of the loss, down from 97.8%. The objects drive training.

Starting with the right bias

A fresh sigmoid classifier outputs p = 0.5 for every anchor. With 100,000 background anchors the first loss is enormous and training can blow up. RetinaNet sets the bias of the last class layer so that every anchor starts out predicting a small prior probability π of being an object.

Prior biasExam favourite
σ(b) = π   ⟹   b = ln π1 − π
With π = 0.01: b = −ln 99 ≈ −4.595. Every anchor begins by saying probably background.
Real world scenario: a traffic camera

A fixed camera at a junction sees mostly road, sky and buildings, with a few cars and cyclists. A detector trained with plain cross entropy learns that saying background is almost always right and starts missing cyclists, the rare and important class. Focal loss and a sensible bias start let the rare objects shape the model.

Lesson 5 of 5

Architecture or loss: the GAN story

The lecturer used generative adversarial networks to show how a field moves forward along the design axes from the CNN chapter: architecture, loss and optimisation. A GAN (Goodfellow and colleagues, 2014) trains a generator against a discriminator.

StepWhat changedAxis
GAN (2014)The discriminator is a binary classifier: real or fake, sigmoid and cross entropyOriginal
DCGAN (2015)Convolutional layers, batch normalisation, no fully connected layers, which made GANs train stablyArchitecture
WGAN (2017)The loss becomes the Wasserstein (earth mover) distance. The critic outputs a real number, not a probabilityLoss
Earth mover distance in 1D
W1 = Σi |FP(i) − FQ(i)|
For unit spacing, using the cumulative sums F. It is the least total work to move one pile of earth into the shape of the other.
Earth mover distance by hand
  1. P = [0.5, 0.5, 0] and Q = [0, 0.5, 0.5] over positions 0, 1, 2.
  2. Cumulative sums: FP = [0.5, 1.0, 1.0], FQ = [0, 0.5, 1.0].
  3. W1 = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0 = 1.0.
  4. Check by moving earth: 0.5 from position 0 to 1 costs 0.5, and 0.5 from 1 to 2 costs 0.5. Total 1.0.

Detection followed the same pattern: architecture upgrades such as feature pyramids, and loss upgrades such as Huber and focal loss. The lecturer's advice for a thesis: think architecture first, then loss, and choose optimisation only if you enjoy the mathematics.

One idea, four depths

To learn, a detector needs to know how wrong it was. For the box it measures the distance from the right answer, but stops a single crazy guess from shouting over everything else. For the label it learns to ignore the endless easy background and focus on the few real objects.

Anchors are labelled by IoU thresholds. The loss is α Lcls + β δ Lloc per anchor. Huber gives quadratic loss near zero and linear beyond, capping gradients at 1. Focal loss −(1 − pt)γ ln pt with γ = 2 cuts the background share in the worked example from 97.8% to 0.54%.

Cross entropy is the Bernoulli negative log likelihood and equals H(p) + KL(p‖q). Huber is the M-estimator between Gaussian and Laplacian noise. The prior bias b = ln(π ÷ (1 − π)) sets the starting loss scale. WGAN replaces Jensen-Shannon divergence by W1, which stays informative when distributions do not overlap, and needs a 1-Lipschitz critic.

Box losses moved from coordinates to overlap itself: IoU, GIoU, DIoU and CIoU losses optimise the evaluation measure directly. Assignment became learned or dynamic (ATSS, OTA, the Hungarian matching of DETR), and quality focal and varifocal losses join the class score with localisation quality.
