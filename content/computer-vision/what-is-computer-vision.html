Lesson 1 of 6

What computer vision is

Computer vision is the part of computer science that teaches machines to understand pictures and videos. A camera gives the computer only a grid of numbers. Computer vision is everything that happens between those numbers and an answer such as "a child is crossing the road in front of the car".

The course notes use a careful definition: computer vision uses algorithms and representations to compute properties of the physical world from visual data. Notice the words "physical world". The goal is not the picture itself. The goal is the real scene that the picture came from.

Three jobs computer vision does

JobThe question it answersEveryday example
MeasurementHow big, how far, what shape, how fast?A phone measuring a room, a car estimating the distance to the car in front
Perception and interpretationWhat is in the scene and what is happening?Face unlock, a doctor's tool marking a tumour, a robot finding a cup
Search, organisation and interactionWhich pictures match, and what do they mean?Searching your gallery for "beach", reverse image search, answering a question about a photo

You see meaning, the computer sees numbers

When you look at a chair your brain does a lot of work without telling you. The eyes record light and dark. The brain finds edges, joins edges into a shape, guesses depth and the direction of each surface, decides it is a chair and even knows you can sit on it. A computer has to build every one of those steps itself. The animation below shows what it starts with.

One idea, four depths

A photo on a computer is like a giant sheet of graph paper. Every little square holds a number that says how bright it is. Computer vision is teaching the computer to look at all those numbers and say "that is a cat".

A grey image is a matrix I with H rows and W columns. Each entry I(r, c) is a whole number from 0 to 255. A colour image stacks three such matrices, one each for red, green and blue, giving an array of shape H × W × 3. Vision algorithms are functions that map this array to useful outputs such as a label, a box or a depth map.

A camera performs a projection from the 3D world to a 2D image plane, so depth is lost and many different scenes can produce the same image. Vision is therefore an inverse problem: from the image I we want the scene S with I = f(S), where f includes geometry, lighting, the lens and the sensor. The inverse is not unique, so every method adds assumptions, either hand written (smooth surfaces, rigid objects) or learned from data.

Modern research frames vision as learning representations: functions that map pixels to vectors in which the things we care about (identity, pose, depth, relations) are easy to read out and the nuisances (lighting, viewpoint, background) are suppressed. Open questions include robustness under distribution shift, learning from few labels, linking vision with language, and understanding 3D structure and physics from video.

Remember
A picture is only a measurement of the world. The same object can produce very different numbers, and different objects can produce similar numbers. Every hard part of vision comes from this.

What recognition involves

"Recognise the picture" sounds like one task. In practice it is a family of tasks, and each one gives a different kind of answer. Knowing which task you are solving is the first step of any vision project.

TaskQuestionOutput
Image classificationWhat is this picture mainly of?One label, for example "dog"
Object detectionWhat objects are present, and where?Boxes with a label and a confidence each
CategorisationWhich general class is this object?Person, vehicle, chair
Instance recognitionWhich exact object is this?"This is the Eiffel Tower", "this is your car"
Scene recognitionWhat kind of place is this?Kitchen, highway, market, forest
Attribute recognitionWhat is it like?Red, metal, wet, broken, open
Activity recognitionWhat is happening over time?Walking, falling, crossing the road
Relationship recognitionHow do the objects relate?"A person is holding a bag", "a car is approaching a cyclist"
SegmentationWhich pixels belong to which thing?A label for every pixel

Detection is really two jobs at once: classification (what it is) and localisation (where it is). Category recognition says "this is a building". Instance recognition says "this is the Eiffel Tower". Relationship recognition is a big step beyond detection, because two correctly found boxes say nothing about whether the person is holding the bag or just standing near it.

Context changes the answer

The same small rectangle can be a book in a classroom, a traffic sign by a road and a tray in a kitchen. Recognition uses the surroundings, not just the pixels inside the object. This is why scene recognition often helps object recognition.

Real world scenario: a self driving car at a junction

A car approaching a junction needs almost every task in the table. Detection finds the pedestrian, the cyclist and the traffic light. Attribute recognition reads the light as red. Activity recognition notices the pedestrian has started walking. Relationship recognition notices the cyclist is moving into the car's lane. Measurement estimates that the pedestrian is 14 metres away. A mistake in any one of them can lead to a wrong decision, which is why the notes stress that recognition errors become action errors for autonomous agents.

In the exam
Examiners like the pair detection versus recognition. Detection finds and localises objects. Recognition decides what they are and what they mean. Categorisation gives a class, instance recognition names one particular object.

Why vision is hard

People find vision easy, so it is surprising how hard it is for machines. The reason is that the numbers in an image change a lot when the world changes only a little. The notes list the main troublemakers.

ChallengeWhat happensExample
IlluminationThe same object gives different numbers under different lightNight, glare, shadows, a face lit from one side
Viewpoint and poseThe shape on the image changes with the camera angleA car from the front and from the side
ScaleObjects far away are only a few pixelsA pedestrian 100 metres away
OcclusionPart of the object is hiddenA child standing behind an adult
ClutterA busy background hides boundariesA crowded market, a supermarket shelf
Intra class variationMembers of one class look differentOffice chair, stool, wheelchair
Inter class similarityDifferent classes look alikeWolf and husky, bus and truck
Limited field of viewImportant things are outside the frameA car coming from behind the camera
WeatherContrast and visibility changeRain, fog, dust, snow
Irregular environmentsThe world is messier than the datasetPotholes, missing lane markings

The animation shows why the simplest idea, "store a picture of the object and compare pixels", breaks so quickly. Some changes can be undone with maths. Others cannot.

Normalised cross correlation (NCC)
NCC = Σ (T − T̄)(I − Ī)√( Σ (T − T̄)² · Σ (I − Ī)² )
T is the template, I is the image patch, and a bar means the average. The score runs from −1 to 1. If I = a·T + b with a > 0, the score is exactly 1, so brightness and contrast changes do not matter.

Robustness, generalisation and imbalance

Three words from the notes that are easy to mix up:

  • Robustness is staying correct under the variations you expect, such as different lighting or a slightly different pose.
  • Generalisation is staying correct on new data that differs from the training data, for example a model trained on clean city roads used on rural roads with potholes. The training data and the deployment data come from different distributions. This is called distribution shift.
  • Data imbalance is when some conditions or classes are rare in the training data, for example many summer images and very few snowy ones. It is a common cause of poor generalisation for the rare cases.
One idea, four depths

If you only ever practised football on a sunny day, you might play badly in the rain. Computers are the same. They do well on what they practised and can fail on things they rarely saw.

A classifier learns from training examples. If test images look like training images it does well. Changes in lighting, angle or background move the test images away from what it learned, and accuracy falls. More varied training data and augmentation (random changes during training) are the usual fixes.

Formally the model is trained to minimise expected loss under the training distribution Ptrain(x, y), but it is judged under Ptest. Covariate shift changes P(x) and keeps P(y | x). Label shift changes P(y). Imbalance means some regions of x have few samples, so the empirical risk there has high variance. Reweighting, targeted data collection and domain adaptation each address a different part of this.

Research on robustness studies invariance and equivariance built into architectures, test time adaptation, benchmarks with natural shifts (weather, geography, sensors), and the link between robustness and shortcut learning, where a model uses a background cue that happens to correlate with the label.

Common mistake
A high score on the test set does not prove the model will work after deployment. If the deployment conditions are missing from the data, the test set cannot measure them.

Images, video and their cost

Every pixel is a number the computer has to store and process. Pictures get big very fast, and video multiplies the size by the number of frames.

Numbers in an imageExam favourite
N = H × W × C
H rows, W columns, C channels (1 for grey, 3 for colour).
Bytes in an image
bytes = H × W × C × b8
b is the bits per number. With 8 bits each number is one byte.
Levels per number
levels = 2b
8 bits give 256 levels, from 0 to 255.
Raw video data rateExam favourite
rate = H × W × C × b8 × fps
Bytes per second before compression. fps is frames per second.
Worked example: why the lecture compared 32 × 32 with 2048 × 2048
  1. A 32 × 32 image has 32 × 32 = 1,024 pixels.
  2. A 2048 × 2048 image has 2048 × 2048 = 4,194,304 pixels.
  3. The ratio is 4,194,304 ÷ 1,024 = 4,096. The large image has 4,096 times as many pixels.
  4. Any step that touches every pixel once, such as a 3 × 3 filter, now does 4,096 times the work. This is why resolution is a cost, not just a quality setting.
Worked example: one second of Full HD video
  1. Full HD is 1920 × 1080 with 3 channels and 8 bits per number.
  2. One frame is 1920 × 1080 × 3 = 6,220,800 bytes, about 6.2 MB.
  3. At 30 frames per second that is 6,220,800 × 30 = 186,624,000 bytes, about 187 MB every second.
  4. This is why video is almost always compressed, and why a vision system must decide which frames to process.

Three costs every vision system pays

CostQuestion to askWhy it matters
TimeCan each frame be processed before the next one arrives?A car at 72 km/h moves 2 metres in 100 ms
MemoryCan the frames and the model fit on the device?Phones, drones and cameras have little memory
EnergyHow much battery or power does it use?Edge devices run on small batteries

The notes stress the trade off between accuracy, speed, memory and energy. A very accurate model can be too slow for real time use, and a very fast model can miss things. Video adds a fourth difficulty: the system must keep track of the same object across frames, decide whether movement is real or caused by the camera moving, and keep its answers consistent over time.

Limited supervision

Supervised learning needs labelled examples, and labels are expensive. In some areas the data is rare by nature: rare diseases, rare natural disasters, unusual factory faults. Medical data is also private and needs experts to label it. The usual tools are transfer learning, self supervised learning, few shot learning, semi supervised learning, careful augmentation and domain adaptation. None of them removes the need for good, representative data.

Where computer vision sits in AI

Computer vision uses ideas from all of artificial intelligence. The notes give a simple nesting: artificial intelligence contains machine learning, machine learning contains deep learning, and deep learning includes convolutional networks (CNNs), recurrent networks (RNNs), transformers and other designs.

EraHow features were madeExample
Classical visionPeople design the features by hand, then a simple classifier decidesSIFT or HOG features with a support vector machine
Deep learningThe network learns the features from labelled dataCNNs such as AlexNet and ResNet
Foundation modelsVery large models learn from huge, loosely labelled data, often with textCLIP, SAM, vision transformers

What a CNN learns, layer by layer

CNNs tend to learn a hierarchy. Early layers respond to edges and simple light and dark patterns. Middle layers respond to textures, corners and parts. Deeper layers respond to whole objects and concepts. This mirrors the staged view of vision in the next chapter, where edges come first and meaning comes last.

The brain and AI

The notes put it this way: the brain inspires AI, and AI helps us understand the brain. Studies of the visual cortex found neurons that respond to edges at particular orientations, which inspired layered feature detectors. Today, neural networks are used as models to compare with brain recordings. The notes also warn against taking the analogy literally. An artificial neuron is not a biological neuron, and supervised learning is not how children learn.

One idea, four depths

Old computer vision was like giving a robot a checklist: "a face has two eyes and a mouth". New computer vision is like showing a robot thousands of faces and letting it work out the checklist itself.

In a classical pipeline you compute a feature vector by hand (for example a histogram of edge directions) and train a classifier on it. In deep learning, the feature extractor is a stack of learned layers trained end to end with the classifier, using gradient descent on a loss function.

Learned features win when labelled data and compute are plentiful, because the network can discover features no person would design. Classical features still win when data is scarce, compute is tight, or you need to explain each decision. Many real systems mix both, for example classical keypoints for camera tracking and a CNN for recognition.

Representation learning asks what makes a good feature space. Self supervised methods learn from unlabelled images by predicting missing parts or matching two views of the same image. Multimodal models align images with text so that a single space supports classification, retrieval and question answering without task specific labels.

Common mistake
Do not say that classical features such as HOG are needed "because CNNs overfit". The notes correct this. Overfitting depends on data size, model size, regularisation and augmentation. Classical features are useful because they are efficient, simple and easy to interpret.

Common mistake
OpenCV loads colour images in BGR order, not RGB. Most other libraries (Matplotlib, PIL, PyTorch models) expect RGB. Forgetting to convert is one of the most common bugs in vision code.

Two indexing rules to keep straight: an image is indexed row first, then column, so img[r][c] means row r (the y position) and column c (the x position). And slices do not include their end, so img[1:3] gives rows 1 and 2.
