Lesson 1 of 5

Intersection over union

A student first called it interaction of union. It is intersection over union: the area two boxes share divided by the area they cover together. It is the single most used number in detection: for matching anchors in training, for removing duplicates, and for marking predictions right or wrong in evaluation.

IoUExam favourite
IoU(A, B) = |A ∩ B||A ∪ B| = |A ∩ B||A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|
Between 0 and 1. The overlap rectangle runs from the larger of the left edges to the smaller of the right edges, and the same for top and bottom.
The lecture example
  1. A = (50, 50, 150, 150) and B = (80, 70, 180, 170), each 100 × 100 = 10,000.
  2. Overlap x: max(50, 80) = 80 to min(150, 180) = 150, width 70. Overlap y: 70 to 150, height 80. Area 5,600.
  3. Union: 10,000 + 10,000 − 5,600 = 14,400.
  4. IoU = 5,600 ÷ 14,400 ≈ 0.389. A miss at the usual 0.5 threshold. The box (60, 55, 160, 155) gives 8,550 ÷ 11,450 ≈ 0.747, a hit.

Useful facts

  • IoU = 0 exactly when the boxes do not overlap, and IoU = 1 exactly when they are identical.
  • It is symmetric, IoU(A, B) = IoU(B, A), and unchanged by scaling the whole image.
  • For two equal squares shifted sideways by a fraction s of their width, IoU = (1 − s) ÷ (1 + s). A 20% shift already drops it to 0.667, and a 50% shift to 0.333.
Lesson 2 of 5

Removing duplicates with NMS

Nothing stops several neighbouring anchors from all detecting the same person, each with a slightly different box and score. The lecturer's recipe: every box has a confidence score, so keep the highest one and remove the lower scoring boxes that overlap it too much. This is non-maximum suppression (NMS).

  1. Sort the detections by score, highest first.
  2. Take the best remaining box and keep it.
  3. Remove every remaining box of the same class whose IoU with it is above the threshold.
  4. Repeat until no boxes remain.
The slide example, threshold 0.5
  1. Scores: A 0.95, B 0.89, D 0.81, C 0.75. IoU with A: B 0.82, C 0.71, D 0.10.
  2. Keep A. B (0.82) and C (0.71) overlap it by more than 0.5, so they are removed. D (0.10) stays.
  3. Keep D, the next best. Nothing is left.
  4. Result {A, D}: A is the person, B and C were duplicates, and D is the dog, a different object.

Weak spots

ProblemWhat happens
CrowdsTwo real people who overlap by more than the threshold: the lower scoring one is deleted.
Per class onlyA dog box and a cat box on the same animal both survive.
One fixed thresholdToo low removes real neighbours (lower recall), too high keeps duplicates (lower precision).
Soft-NMS
si ← si · e−IoU(M, bi)² ÷ σ
Instead of deleting, decay the score of each box by how much it overlaps the kept box M. Near duplicates sink, slightly overlapping neighbours survive.
Common mistake
The lecturer mentioned comparing with the ground truth. At test time there is no ground truth, so NMS uses only scores and box to box IoU. Ground truth is used in training (target assignment) and in evaluation.

Newer designs go further. DETR (2020) predicts a fixed set of boxes with a transformer and matches them one to one with the objects during training, so it needs no NMS at all.

Lesson 3 of 5

One stage or two stage

The lecturer opened this part with a challenge: anyone can call YOLO from a library and run it in an hour, for example YOLO("yolov8n.pt")("image.jpg") with the ultralytics package. But what is happening behind it? Detectors are built from a classification backbone (VGG, Inception, ResNet) plus a detection part, in one of two patterns called meta-architectures.

One stageTwo stage
IdeaImage straight to boxes and classesPropose likely regions first, then refine each one
ExamplesSSD, YOLO, RetinaNetR-CNN, Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN
StructureBackbone with a class head and a box headBackbone, region proposals, crop each region, then classify and refine
StrengthSimple operations, easy to run fastPer object features, easy to add masks or keypoints

The old rule was one stage is fast, two stage is accurate. The slides call that outdated: modern systems blur the line. The lecturer's answer to which is better: it depends on your domain, so measure accuracy and speed on your own data.

Class agnostic proposals

The first stage of a two stage detector only answers is there an object here, and refines the box. It never says which object. That is what class agnostic means. The lecturer also introduced task agnostic, a model that uses no task identity, as opposed to task aware.

Training several stages

The lecturer's analogy: semesters. How you do in semester 1 affects semester 2, so the stages depend on each other.

ModeHowAssumes
Stage wiseTrain stage 1 with its loss, feed its output to stage 2 and train that separatelyThe stages are independent
Joint (end to end)Add all the losses and back-propagate from the last stage to the firstThe stages depend on each other
HybridStage wise first, then joint fine tuning, or freeze stage 1 and train stage 2Stability first, then end to end tuning

Faster R-CNN is trained jointly with four losses: proposal classification and proposal box in stage 1, object class and object box in stage 2. This works because the crop and resize step is differentiable, as the last lesson shows.

Lesson 4 of 5

RetinaNet and the feature pyramid

RetinaNet (Lin and colleagues, 2017) is the single stage case study. Its three ideas: a multi-resolution feature extractor (ResNet with a feature pyramid network), focal loss from the last chapter, and the small starting bias.

The hierarchical assumption

The lecturer asked for the basic assumption of a CNN. Students offered locality and translation invariance. Her answer: an image is processed hierarchically, fine details first, then broader features, so the resolution keeps reducing through the network. Both views are right at different levels: locality and weight sharing are the built in biases, and stacking layers with downsampling gives the hierarchy.

Early layersDeep layers
Spatial resolutionHigh, for example 80 × 80Low, for example 20 × 20
Semantic meaningLow: edges, colours, blobsHigh: parts and whole objects
Receptive fieldSmallLarge, sees context

Several students guessed that early layers carry more meaning because they hold more numbers. The lecturer corrected this: meaning develops as layers go deeper. That is the problem for small objects. They need high resolution maps, which come from early layers, which have little meaning.

FPN fixes the trade off

One FPN mergeExam favourite
Pl = Conv1×1(Cl) + Upsample×2(Pl+1)
The 1 × 1 convolution matches the channel count (to 256), the upsampling matches the size, then the two maps are added. A 3 × 3 convolution then smooths each result.
Anchors scored
Σlevels (cells) × A
For 640 × 640: (6,400 + 1,600 + 400 + 100 + 25) × 9 = 76,725 anchors.

The same class head and box head (four 3 × 3 layers of 256 channels each) run on every level. Sharing works because FPN has made every level equally rich in meaning. The lecturer's summary: the large map gains meaning from the small one, and the small one gains detail from the large one.

Lesson 5 of 5

Faster R-CNN, interpolation and RoIAlign

Faster R-CNN is the two stage case study. Stage 1 is a region proposal network, itself a small single stage detector that deliberately over-predicts class agnostic boxes. Stage 2 crops each proposal from the shared feature map, resizes it to a fixed size such as 7 × 7, and classifies and refines it. Cropping features rather than pixels is cheap, because the features already exist.

How do you shrink an image without a network?

The lecturer asked how to turn a 256 × 256 image into 32 × 32 without any neural network or pooling. The answer is interpolation: compute new pixel values at new positions from their neighbours. Nearest neighbour copies the closest pixel, bilinear blends the 4 nearest, bicubic uses 16. Quantisation is a different thing: it keeps the size and reduces the bits per pixel. A student asked whether information is lost. Yes, 32 × 32 is low resolution, but the layout and relationships between objects are kept.

Bilinear interpolationExam favourite
f(x, y) = (1 − u)(1 − v) f00 + u(1 − v) f10 + (1 − u)v f01 + uv f11
u and v are the fractional parts of x and y. Each corner's weight is the area of the rectangle opposite it. The weights add to 1.
The lecture green dot at (2.3, 4.6)
  1. Corners: f(2, 4) = 10, f(3, 4) = 20, f(2, 5) = 30, f(3, 5) = 50. Here u = 0.3 and v = 0.6.
  2. Weights: (0.7)(0.4) = 0.28, (0.3)(0.4) = 0.12, (0.7)(0.6) = 0.42, (0.3)(0.6) = 0.18. They add to 1.
  3. f = 10(0.28) + 20(0.12) + 30(0.42) + 50(0.18) = 2.8 + 2.4 + 12.6 + 9.0 = 26.8.
  4. The answer lies between 10 and 50, closest to the corner nearest the point.

RoIAlign

A proposal rarely lines up with the feature grid. RoIAlign (from Mask R-CNN) divides the proposal into a fixed grid of bins and samples each bin centre with bilinear interpolation. The older RoI pooling rounded the proposal to whole cells, misaligning features by up to half a cell, which hurt masks. Because bilinear interpolation is smooth, gradients flow back through the crop, and the whole detector trains end to end.

RoIAlign with a 2 × 2 output
  1. Feature map f(x, y) = 10y + x. Proposal (0.8, 1.2, 4.4, 4.8).
  2. Bin size: (4.4 − 0.8) ÷ 2 = 1.8 wide and (4.8 − 1.2) ÷ 2 = 1.8 tall.
  3. Bin centres: x = 1.7 and 3.5, y = 2.1 and 3.9.
  4. Sampling gives [[22.7, 24.5], [40.7, 42.5]], a fixed size output whatever the proposal size. Each equals 10y + x, because bilinear interpolation reproduces such a formula exactly.
One idea, four depths

A detector often draws several boxes around the same thing. NMS keeps the most confident box and throws away the copies that overlap it a lot. Some detectors find objects in one go, others first guess where objects might be and then look closer.

IoU = |A ∩ B| ÷ |A ∪ B|. NMS sorts by score and removes same class boxes with IoU above a threshold. One stage detectors (SSD, YOLO, RetinaNet) predict directly, two stage ones (Faster R-CNN) propose, crop with RoIAlign and refine. FPN gives every pyramid level deep features.

FPN merges by 1 × 1 lateral convs, nearest upsampling and addition, then 3 × 3 smoothing, and heads are shared across levels. RoIAlign uses bilinear sampling, which is piecewise linear, so gradients reach both features and box coordinates. Soft-NMS uses a Gaussian decay e−IoU² ÷ σ.

Set prediction (DETR and its faster variants) removes NMS by bipartite matching. Real time detectors such as recent YOLO versions train with one to one assignment to become NMS free. BiFPN in EfficientDet learns weighted bidirectional fusion across levels, and transformer backbones replace ResNet in many current detectors.
