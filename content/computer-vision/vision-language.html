Lesson 1 of 6

From pixels to meaning to words

The lecturer split vision into two levels. Low level vision works on the signal: brightness, contrast, colour, noise, edges, blur, sharpness and resolution. High level vision works on meaning: classification, detection, scene recognition, activity recognition, captioning and visual question answering.

The processing ladderExam favourite
pixels → edges and textures → shapes → objects → scenes and activities
Pixel changes reveal edges, edges form shapes, shapes reveal objects, and objects with their relations describe the scene.

Everything up to now used a fixed list of classes: 20 for VOC, 80 for COCO, 19 for Cityscapes. Adding a class meant collecting and labelling new data. Language removes that limit. If a model understands that a picture matches the words a photo of a zebra, any class that can be described becomes available.

ModelQuestion it answersOutput
CLIPWhich text best matches this image?Similarity scores, zero-shot labels
DALL-EWhat image should be created from this text?A new image
SAMWhich pixels belong to the object I point at?Masks, without class names
Language-guided segmentationWhich pixels match this phrase?A mask for the phrase
Common mistake
Some models now beat the estimated human error on the ImageNet benchmark. The lecturer's caution: that is task specific. The same models can still fail under unusual conditions, distribution shift, adversarial changes, occlusion and new categories. Benchmark superiority is not general intelligence.
Lesson 2 of 6

CLIP: one space for images and text

CLIP (Radford and colleagues, OpenAI, 2021) learns from about 400 million image and caption pairs collected from the web. It has two encoders: an image encoder (a ResNet or a vision transformer) and a text encoder (a transformer). Each turns its input into an embedding, a vector that represents meaning. Both land in one shared space where an image of a dog lies close to the text a photo of a dog and far from a red motorcycle.

Cosine similarityExam favourite
cos(x, y) = x · y‖x‖ ‖y‖
Between −1 and 1. It ignores length and compares direction only.
Cosine by hand
  1. Image embedding x = (1, 2, 2) and text embedding y = (2, 1, 2).
  2. Dot product: 2 + 2 + 4 = 8. Lengths: √9 = 3 and √9 = 3.
  3. cos = 8 ÷ 9 ≈ 0.889, a close match.

Contrastive learning

A batch holds N images and their N captions. Every image is compared with every caption, giving an N × N grid of similarities. The N matching pairs on the diagonal are positives, the other N² − N cells are negatives. Training pulls the diagonal up and pushes everything else down.

Symmetric contrastive lossExam favourite
L = ½ [ CErows(S ÷ τ) + CEcolumns(S ÷ τ) ]
Each row is a SoftMax over captions whose correct answer is the diagonal, and each column the same over images. τ is a learned temperature. CLIP used batches of 32,768 pairs, so each image had 32,767 negatives.

Zero-shot classification

At test time, write one prompt per class: a photo of a dog, a photo of a cat, a photo of a horse. Embed the prompts once, embed the image, and pick the prompt with the highest similarity. No labelled training set for these classes is needed, which is what zero-shot means here.

TrainingInference
Image and caption pairs go inOne image and a list of class prompts go in
Both encoders produce embeddingsPrompts are embedded once and reused
All pairwise similarities in the batchSimilarity of the image with each prompt
The contrastive loss updates both encodersThe highest similarity gives the class

Wording matters. A photo of a dog, an image of a dog and a close-up picture of a dog give slightly different embeddings, so the prompt is part of the classifier. Averaging several wordings per class, called prompt ensembling, usually helps.

In the exam
CLIP and SAM both take prompts but answer different questions. CLIP says what an image corresponds to and outputs scores. SAM says which pixels belong to the object and outputs masks.
Lesson 3 of 6

Transformers for dense prediction

The vision transformer (ViT, Dosovitskiy and colleagues, 2020) was introduced in chapter 4.1. A student asked how it differs from the transformer used for text. The lecturer: in NLP each word is a token, in vision the image is cut into patches and each patch is one token. The transformer block itself, self-attention plus a small MLP, is the same.

Why use it for segmentation? Pixel labels often depend on long range context: road against wall, river against shadow. Self-attention lets every position exchange information with every other one in a single layer. A CNN needs many layers or dilation to see that far. The price is cost.

TokensExam favourite
N = (n ÷ p)²
n image side, p patch side. A 224 image with 16 × 16 patches gives 14² = 196 tokens of 16 · 16 · 3 = 768 numbers each.
Attention pairs
global: N²,   windowed: (N ÷ w²) · (w²)²
w tokens per window side.
How quickly attention grows
  1. 224 × 224, patch 16: 196 tokens, 196² = 38,416 pairs per head.
  2. 512 × 512: 32² = 1,024 tokens and 1,048,576 pairs, 27.3 times more for 5.2 times more pixels.
  3. Patch 4 on 224 gives 56 × 56 = 3,136 tokens and 9,834,496 global pairs.
  4. Swin style 7 × 7 windows: 64 windows × 49² = 153,664 pairs, 64 times fewer.

Two common confusions

  • Is a patch a vocabulary word? No. A text vocabulary is a fixed table of IDs. A patch is a continuous vector of pixel values mapped to an embedding by a learned linear layer. Patch equals token is right, patch equals vocabulary word is not.
  • How does positional encoding work? In the original ViT it is a learned table, one vector per patch position, added to the tokens and trained with everything else. Swin uses relative position bias, and SegFormer drops explicit encodings and lets a 3 × 3 convolution inside the feed forward layer supply position.

SegFormer and mask classification

SegFormer (2021) is a simple modern design: a hierarchical transformer encoder with four stages at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32, the transformer version of a CNN pyramid, plus a light all-MLP decoder that upsamples every stage to 1/4 size, concatenates and predicts.

Mask classification (MaskFormer 2021, Mask2Former 2022) changes the question. Instead of H × W × C logits, N learned queries each produce one mask and one class, including no object. Training matches predictions to true segments one to one with the Hungarian algorithm, as in DETR. Because the output is a set of (mask, class) pairs, one model handles semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation.

Lesson 4 of 6

Promptable and language-guided segmentation

The lecturer: after DeepLab, backbones moved to transformers, then to foundation models where the prompt is an input, for example segment only the pedestrian in this image.

Segment Anything (SAM)

Released by Meta AI in 2023. Three parts: a large ViT image encoder that runs once per image, a prompt encoder for points, boxes or rough masks, and a light mask decoder that returns several candidate masks with quality scores when a prompt is ambiguous. It was trained on SA-1B, about 11 million images and 1.1 billion masks, built with a data engine in which the model itself helped annotate. That is about 100 masks per image.

Common mistake
SAM is class agnostic. It tells you where an object is, not what it is. Pair it with a detector or CLIP when you need names.

A student asked what promptable means. The lecturer compared it with ChatGPT: whatever you write is the prompt. Here the prompt tells the model which object to segment, as a click, a box or a phrase. One frozen model serves many tasks without retraining, which is what foundation model means.

Open vocabulary and language-guided masks

Classic segmentation has a fixed class list. Language-guided segmentation takes a query such as segment the red car and returns the matching region. Labels can come from text, models move beyond fixed datasets, and masks connect to captions, grounding, question answering and agents. The slide called this the bridge from the vision half of the course to the language half.

Building blockWhat it does
CLIPAligns whole images with text
LSeg, OpenSegAlign per pixel embeddings with the text embedding of a class name
CLIPSegSegments from a text or image prompt
Grounded-SAMA text driven detector (Grounding DINO) finds boxes, SAM turns them into masks
Going deeper: Conditioning, guidance, grounding and VQA

The lecturer explained language conditioning with an example: make my face a smiling face. The text is a condition on what the model produces. Language guided stresses a query at inference time that steers the output. Mechanically both feed text embeddings into the network, by concatenation, cross-attention or feature scaling, and the terms overlap in practice.

Segment the red car is strictly referring expression segmentation, a grounding task whose answer is a mask. Visual question answering answers with text, such as what colour is the car. Both need the same image and text alignment, which is why the slide links them.

Generating and restoring images

DALL-E (OpenAI, 2021) creates images from text, such as an armchair shaped like an avocado. A text to image model must read the prompt, find the objects and attributes, understand how they relate, and compose a plausible scene. In a red motorcycle standing in a garage it must keep the object (motorcycle), the colour (red), the state (standing) and the place (garage).

Restoration

Restoration tries to recover the true image from a degraded one: noise, blur, motion, compression, sensor limits, poor light, low resolution. It is not the same as making the picture look nice. Its history runs through space and medicine: enhancing lunar images from the Ranger 7 mission in the 1960s and tomographic reconstruction in the 1970s.

The lecturer's research example: expensive MRI scanners give clean images slowly, faster or cheaper scans give noisier ones, and a model trained on pairs maps low quality to high quality. That could cut exam time and cost, but raises a safety question: how do we know the restored image is medically correct? Use separate training, validation and test sets, clinical benchmarks, expert review against the original, and clinical measures such as whether lesions and boundaries are preserved.

Super-resolution

A student asked how a model can produce a high resolution image when none exists at deployment. During training it sees pairs and learns f(low resolution) ≈ high resolution. At deployment it applies f to a new image. That is inference, not recovery: the model fills in plausible details that may not match the true scene.

Common mistake
A visually attractive restored image can still contain wrong information. In medicine, legal evidence and inspection, invented detail is dangerous, so validate restoration on the task it serves, not on how it looks.
Lesson 6 of 6

3D from many photographs

A camera flattens the 3D world into 2D. 3D reconstruction recovers the shapes, positions and scene geometry, and the camera viewpoints, from several images. The lecture showed Building Rome in a Day (Agarwal and colleagues, ICCV 2009), which reconstructed city landmarks from ordinary internet photos taken by different people, with different cameras, resolutions and lighting.

  1. Match features. Find the same physical points in several photos: corners, textures, architectural details. This is where SIFT style keypoints from chapter 1.2 earn their place.
  2. Estimate camera poses. Where each camera stood and which way it faced.
  3. Triangulate. Each match gives one viewing ray per camera. The rays meet at the 3D point.
Stereo depthExam favourite
Z = f Bd
f focal length in pixels, B baseline between the cameras, d disparity: how far the point shifts between the two images.
Depth error
ΔZ ≈ Z²f B Δd
Error grows with the square of distance and falls with a wider baseline.
Depth from disparity
  1. f = 700 pixels, B = 0.12 m, and the point shifts by d = 42 pixels.
  2. Z = 700 × 0.12 ÷ 42 = 2 m.
  3. A 1 pixel mistake: Z² ÷ (fB) = 4 ÷ 84 ≈ 0.048 m, about 5 cm.
  4. At 8 m the disparity is only 10.5 pixels and the same mistake costs about 0.8 m.

Good reconstructions are used in digital maps, heritage preservation, architecture, robotics, augmented and virtual reality, urban planning, film and navigation. They are not perfect copies. Errors come from wrong matches, repeated textures, moving objects, poor light, occlusion and weak camera geometry.

One idea, four depths

Some models now understand pictures and words together. They can sort photos into categories you just type in, cut out whatever you click on, or draw a new picture from a sentence. Two cameras can also tell how far away something is, the same way your two eyes do.

CLIP maps images and text into one space and is trained to pull matching pairs together. Zero-shot classification picks the prompt with the highest cosine similarity. SAM returns masks for points or boxes. Stereo depth is Z = fB ÷ d.

CLIP uses a symmetric cross-entropy over an N × N similarity matrix with a learned temperature. ViT attention costs N² in the number of tokens, so dense models use hierarchy and windows. Mask classification predicts (mask, class) sets with Hungarian matching. Depth error grows as Z² ÷ (fB).

Current work extends these ideas: open vocabulary detectors and segmenters, vision language models that answer questions and follow instructions, diffusion models that edit images under text control, and neural scene representations (NeRF, Gaussian splatting) that replace classic multi-view stereo for novel view synthesis.
