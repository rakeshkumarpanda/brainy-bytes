How computer vision is examined

This chapter is for the week before an exam. It does not teach new material. It shows the shapes questions take at each level, with worked answers, and points back to the chapter where each idea is taught. The questions are original practice questions written in the style of each tier, not copies of real past papers.

Lesson 2 of 6

Tier 1: school and first year

Questions at this level check that you can describe an image as numbers and do small calculations on it.

Image size in bytesExam favourite
width × height × channels × bytes per value
An 8 bit value takes 1 byte.
Colour to greyExam favourite
Y = 0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B
Green counts most because the eye is most sensitive to it.
Negative and brightness
s = 255 − r,   s = clip(r + b, 0, 255)
Results outside 0 to 255 are clipped.
Question 1. How much memory does a Full HD colour photo take, uncompressed?
  1. Full HD is 1920 × 1080 pixels with 3 channels of 1 byte.
  2. 1920 × 1080 × 3 = 6,220,800 bytes.
  3. Divide by 1,048,576 (220) to get about 5.93 MiB, or 6.22 MB in powers of ten. State which one you used.
Question 2. Convert the pixel (R, G, B) = (200, 100, 50) to grey.
  1. Y = 0.299 × 200 + 0.587 × 100 + 0.114 × 50.
  2. = 59.8 + 58.7 + 5.7 = 124.2.
  3. Round to the nearest whole number: 124.
Question 3. How many different colours can a 24 bit image show?
  1. 8 bits per channel gives 28 = 256 levels.
  2. Three channels: 256 × 256 × 256.
  3. = 224 = 16,777,216 colours.

Short answer favourites: name three tasks of computer vision (classification, detection, segmentation), explain why the same object can look very different (viewpoint, light, occlusion, scale, deformation, clutter, from chapter 1.1), and explain why a photo file is smaller than width × height × 3 (compression, chapter 2.1).

Common mistake
A common slip is mixing bits and bytes. 24 bits per pixel is 3 bytes per pixel.
Lesson 3 of 6

Tier 2: university semester and IIT course exams

Here the examiner gives a small matrix or a pair of boxes and asks you to work by hand. Show every step.

Question 4. Apply the Sobel x kernel to the patch [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6], [7, 8, 9]].
  1. Sobel x = [[−1, 0, 1], [−2, 0, 2], [−1, 0, 1]]. Multiply matching entries and add.
  2. Top row: −1 + 3 = 2. Middle row: −8 + 12 = 4. Bottom row: −7 + 9 = 2.
  3. Gx = 2 + 4 + 2 = 8. A positive value means brightness increases to the right.
Question 5. Gx = 3 and Gy = 4. Find the gradient magnitude and direction.
  1. Magnitude = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5.
  2. Direction θ = atan2(4, 3).
  3. θ ≈ 53.13°. The edge itself runs at right angles to this direction.
Question 6. The Harris matrix is M = [[4, 0], [0, 4]] and k = 0.04. Find R and classify the point.
  1. det M = 16, trace M = 8.
  2. R = det M − k (trace M)² = 16 − 0.04 × 64.
  3. R = 13.44, large and positive, so the point is a corner. Negative R means an edge, small |R| a flat area.
Question 7. Boxes A = (0, 0, 4, 4) and B = (2, 2, 6, 6) as (x1, y1, x2, y2). Find IoU.
  1. Overlap is from (2, 2) to (4, 4), area 4.
  2. Union = 16 + 16 − 4 = 28.
  3. IoU = 4 ÷ 28 = 1 ÷ 7 ≈ 0.143. Under the usual 0.5 rule these are different objects.
Question 8. A conv layer has 3 × 3 kernels, 64 input and 128 output channels, with bias. How many parameters?
  1. Weights per filter: 3 × 3 × 64 = 576.
  2. 128 filters: 576 × 128 = 73,728.
  3. Add 128 biases: 73,856.
Question 9. A detector finds 10 boxes, 8 correct, and 12 objects exist. Find P, R and F1.
  1. TP = 8, FP = 2, FN = 12 − 8 = 4.
  2. P = 8 ÷ 10 = 0.8, R = 8 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.667.
  3. F1 = 2PR ÷ (P + R) = 1.0667 ÷ 1.4667 ≈ 0.727.

Short notes and compare questions at this level: median against Gaussian filter for salt and pepper noise (chapter 2.3), one stage against two stage detectors (chapter 5.3), semantic against instance against panoptic segmentation (chapter 6.1), FCN against U-Net (chapter 6.2), dense against sparse optical flow (chapter 3.1).

Common mistake
Write the box format before computing IoU. (x1, y1, x2, y2) and (x, y, w, h) give very different answers from the same four numbers.

Tier 3: multiple choice and numerical answer papers

NPTEL course exams and GATE style papers use multiple choice, multiple select and numerical answer questions with no partial credit. Speed matters, so learn the shortcuts and the trap options.

Question 10. Logits (2, 1, 0), true class first. Find the cross entropy loss to three decimals.
  1. e² = 7.389, e¹ = 2.718, e⁰ = 1. Sum 11.107.
  2. p = 7.389 ÷ 11.107 ≈ 0.665.
  3. Loss = −ln 0.665 ≈ 0.408.
Question 11. A confusion matrix for two classes has rows (50, 10) and (5, 35), rows true and columns predicted. Find mIoU.
  1. Class 0: TP 50, FN 10, FP 5. IoU = 50 ÷ 65 ≈ 0.769.
  2. Class 1: TP 35, FN 5, FP 10. IoU = 35 ÷ 50 = 0.700.
  3. mIoU = (0.769 + 0.700) ÷ 2 ≈ 0.735.
Question 12. Focal loss with γ = 2 for a well classified example with p = 0.9. How much smaller is it than cross entropy?
  1. Cross entropy = −ln 0.9 ≈ 0.105.
  2. Focal factor (1 − 0.9)² = 0.01.
  3. Focal loss ≈ 0.00105, 100 times smaller. Easy examples stop dominating the total.
In the exam
Trap options to expect: forgetting the bias in a parameter count, using k instead of k − 1 in the receptive field, counting the background class in mIoU when the question excludes it, and rounding in the middle of a calculation.
Lesson 5 of 6

Tier 4: derivations, design and critique

Research university exams in the style of MIT, Stanford and Cambridge reward a complete argument more than a final number. A good answer states assumptions, derives the result, then says when it fails.

Question 13. Show that a 2D Gaussian blur is separable and say why it matters.

Model answer
  1. G(x, y) = (1 ÷ 2πσ²) e−(x² + y²) ÷ 2σ² = [(1 ÷ √(2π) σ) e−x² ÷ 2σ²] × [(1 ÷ √(2π) σ) e−y² ÷ 2σ²] = g(x) g(y).
  2. So G ∗ I = gy ∗ (gx ∗ I): blur every row, then every column.
  3. Cost per pixel falls from k² to 2k multiplications, for k = 15 from 225 to 30, 7.5 times less.
  4. Also, two Gaussian blurs make one Gaussian with σ = √(σ₁² + σ₂²), so σ = 3 then 4 equals one blur with σ = 5.

Question 14. Derive the optical flow constraint and explain the aperture problem.

Model answer
  1. Assume a point keeps its brightness as it moves: I(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t).
  2. A first order Taylor expansion gives Ix u + Iy v + It = 0.
  3. One equation, two unknowns: only the flow along the gradient is fixed. That is the aperture problem.
  4. Lucas and Kanade assume the flow is constant in a small window and solve the stacked equations by least squares. Example: Ix = 2, Iy = 1, It = −4 with v = 0 gives u = 2.

Question 15. Derive how stereo depth error grows with distance.

Model answer
  1. Similar triangles give Z = f B ÷ d.
  2. Differentiate: dZ ÷ dd = −f B ÷ d² = −Z² ÷ (f B).
  3. So ΔZ ≈ Z² Δd ÷ (f B). Error grows with the square of depth and falls with focal length and baseline.
  4. With f = 500 px, B = 0.1 m and d = 25 px, Z = 2 m, and a 1 pixel error costs about 0.08 m.

Design and critique questions

A design question such as build a system that counts people in a crowded station has no single answer. Structure it as: the data and labels you need, the model family and why (detector, density map or segmentation), the metric that matches the goal, the failure cases (occlusion, scale, lighting) and how you would test them. A critique question gives an experiment, such as the blur and sharpening project in chapter 8.1, and asks what it shows. Name the controlled variable, the sample size, any train and test overlap, the operating point, and one experiment that would make the claim stronger.

In the exam
In derivations, name each assumption when you use it: brightness constancy, small motion, rectified cameras, known σ. Examiners often ask what breaks when an assumption fails.
One idea, four depths

At school you are asked what a picture is made of and how to change it. You show you can count and convert carefully.

At university you are given a small matrix or two boxes and asked to apply a formula by hand, showing each step.

In competitive papers you must recognise the right formula quickly, avoid the trap options and round only at the end.

At research universities you derive the formula yourself, state its assumptions, and argue when a method or an experiment fails.

Ten mistakes that cost marks

  1. Rounding in the middle of a long calculation.
  2. Forgetting biases in parameter counts.
  3. Mixing box formats in IoU.
  4. Forgetting to clip pixel values to 0 to 255.
  5. Flipping the kernel for correlation, or not flipping it when the question says true convolution.
  6. Treating mAP as the mean of precision instead of the mean of AP over classes.
  7. Using pixel accuracy on unbalanced classes where mIoU is asked for.
  8. Forgetting that NMS runs per class in most detectors.
  9. Writing degrees when the formula needs radians.
  10. Giving a design answer without a metric and a failure case.
