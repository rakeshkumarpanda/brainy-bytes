Part 1

📋Describing data: centre, spread and outliers

Statistics turns a pile of numbers into a few that tell the story. The first questions are always: where is the centre, and how spread out is the data?

🧮 Mean
x̄ = x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙn
Add everything and share it out equally. Pulled hard by extreme values.
🧮 Median
the middle value once sorted
With an even count, average the two middle values. Barely moved by extreme values.
🧮 Mode
the most common value
The only one that works for categories such as favourite colour.
🧮 Sample variance and SD
s² = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²n − 1    s = √s²
Dividing by n − 1 instead of n corrects for the fact that data sits closer to its own mean than to the true mean (Bessel correction).
🧮 Quartiles and IQR
IQR = Q3 − Q1
Q1 and Q3 are the medians of the lower and upper halves. The middle 50% of the data lies between them.
🧮 Outlier fences (Tukey)
below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
A common rule of thumb for flagging values worth a closer look.
For skewed data such as incomes or house prices, quote the median. The mean is pulled toward the long tail.
News reports usually give the median house price, because a handful of mansions would push the mean far above what a typical home costs.
🔍 Going deeper: type your own data and read its five number summary

The five number summary (minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum) is drawn as a box plot. The box covers the middle half of the data, the line inside is the median, and the whiskers reach out to the furthest values that are not outliers. Type any list of numbers below and press Play to see the summary built one piece at a time.

Skew. When a few values are far out on one side, the mean is dragged towards them and the median is not. So comparing the two tells you the shape without drawing anything.

ComparisonShapeEveryday example
mean ≈ medianroughly symmetricheights of adults of one sex
mean > medianlong tail to the right (right skew)incomes, house prices, time spent on a website
mean < medianlong tail to the left (left skew)marks in an easy exam where most score high
Many machine learning features are right skewed. Taking the log of such a feature (often log(1 + x) so that zeros are allowed) pulls in the long tail, makes the shape closer to symmetric, and usually helps linear models and gradient descent.

🎣Samples, bias and standard error

We rarely measure everyone. The whole group we care about is the population. The part we actually measure is the sample. A number worked out from the sample (such as x̄) is a statistic, and it is our estimate of the population parameter (such as μ).

PopulationSample
sizeN (often huge)n
meanμ (unknown)x̄ (we compute it)
SDσs
proportionpp̂
🧮 Standard error of a mean
SE = s√n
How much x̄ would wobble from sample to sample (Chapter 10).
🧮 Standard error of a proportion
SE = √(p̂ (1 − p̂)n)
540 yes out of 1000: √(0.54 × 0.46 ÷ 1000) ≈ 0.0158.
A bigger sample shrinks random error but does nothing about bias. Polling only people who answer landline phones, or training a face detector only on photos of one group of people, gives a confident answer to the wrong question.
Kind of biasWhat goes wrongExample
selection biasthe sample is not like the populationsurveying gym members about exercise habits
survivorship biasonly the survivors are visiblestudying only successful startups
non-response biasthe people who answer differ from those who do nota long email survey

Training data is a sample. If it does not look like the data the model will meet in use, accuracy measured on it will be too optimistic. This is called distribution shift, and it is a sampling problem before it is a modelling problem.
🔍 Going deeper: watch the sample mean settle, then meet the bootstrap

The population below is lopsided, like incomes: most values are small and a few are large. Press Play to draw many samples of size n and record each sample mean. The histogram of those means is the sampling distribution. However skewed the population, the means pile up into a bell around the true mean, and the bell narrows as n grows, exactly as σ ÷ √n predicts.

In real life you have one sample, not thousands. The bootstrap is a clever way round that. Treat your sample as if it were the population, draw many new samples from it with replacement (so some values appear twice and some not at all), and compute the statistic each time. The spread of those results estimates the standard error, for any statistic, even ones with no neat formula such as the median.

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains many models, each on a different bootstrap sample, and averages them. A random forest is bagging applied to decision trees, plus a random choice of features at each split.
Part 3

📏Confidence intervals

A single estimate such as "18.2 hours" hides its uncertainty. A confidence interval gives a range: estimate ± margin of error. A 95% confidence interval is built by a method that catches the true value in 95% of samples.

🧮 Confidence interval for a mean
x̄ ± z* · SE    with    z* = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.576 (99%)
For small samples with unknown σ, statisticians use a slightly wider t value instead of z*.
"95% confident" does not mean there is a 95% chance that μ is inside this particular interval. μ is fixed, the interval is what varies. It means that 95% of intervals made this way catch μ. The animation shows exactly that: about 1 in 20 intervals miss.
To halve the margin of error, you need four times as many people. That √n again.
Part 4

⚖️Hypothesis tests and p-values

A hypothesis test asks whether the data is surprising enough to doubt a default claim. The default, "nothing special is going on", is the null hypothesis H₀. The claim we are testing for is the alternative H₁.

🧮 Test statistic (z-test for a mean)
z = x̄ − μ₀σ ÷ √n
How many standard errors the sample mean is from the value the null claims.
🧮 p-value
p = P(a result at least this extreme | H₀ is true)
Small p means the data would be rare if H₀ were true. Reject H₀ when p < α, usually α = 0.05.
H₀ really trueH₀ really false
Reject H₀Type I error (false positive), chance αcorrect, chance = power
Keep H₀correctType II error (false negative), chance β
A p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true. And "not significant" does not prove there is no effect. It only says this data was not enough to tell.
If p is low, the null must go. But statistically significant is not the same as important: with a million users, a 0.01% difference can be significant and still not worth anything.
🔍 Going deeper: see the p-value as an area, then the tests you will meet most

A p-value is an area under the null distribution: the share of results at least as extreme as the one you saw. Move the test statistic below and watch the shaded tails grow and shrink. A two sided test counts both tails, because a surprisingly large difference in either direction would count against H₀.

TestQuestion it answersExample
one sample t-testis the mean different from a claimed value, when σ is unknowndo our batteries last 10 hours on average
two sample t-testdo two groups have different meansis model B faster than model A per request
paired t-testdid the same items change between two conditionseach patient’s blood pressure before and after
chi-square testare two categories related, from a table of countsis device type related to whether people buy
two proportion z-testdo two rates differA/B test of click rates (next lesson)

The multiple testing trap. If you run 20 independent tests at α = 0.05 on data where nothing is going on, the chance that at least one comes out "significant" is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.64. Testing many metrics, many segments or many models and reporting only the winner almost guarantees a false discovery. The simplest fix is the Bonferroni correction: with m tests, use α ÷ m for each one.

Part 5

🆎A/B testing

An A/B test is a hypothesis test that companies run every day. Visitors are split at random between the current version A and a new version B, and we compare a rate such as the share who buy.

🧮 Pooled rate
p̂ = buys A + buys Bvisitors A + visitors B
Under H₀ both pages share one true rate, so we pool the data to estimate it.
🧮 Two proportion z-test
z = p̂B − p̂A√(p̂(1 − p̂)(1/nA + 1/nB))
Then the p-value comes from the normal curve, as before.
Decide the sample size before the test and do not stop the moment p dips below 0.05. Peeking every day and stopping at the first "win" makes false positives far more common than 5%.
Comparing two models works the same way. If model B beats model A by one point of accuracy on a test set, a test like this tells you whether that gap is bigger than what random luck in the test set could produce.
🔍 Going deeper: how many visitors does an A/B test need

Before starting a test, decide how small a change you want to be able to detect. A tiny effect needs a huge sample, because it must stand out from random noise. A common rule of thumb for comparing two rates at 5% significance with 80% power is:

🧮 Visitors needed in each group (rule of thumb)
n ≈ 16 × p(1 − p)δ²
p is the current rate and δ is the smallest difference worth detecting. The 16 comes from (1.96 + 0.84)² × 2 ≈ 15.7.
Halve the effect you want to see and you need four times the people. The square in δ² is the same √n rule seen throughout this chapter.
Part 6

🎯Maximum likelihood estimation

Maximum likelihood answers "which parameter value makes the data we saw most probable?" It is the idea behind how most machine learning models are trained.

🧮 Likelihood of coin flips
L(p) = pʰ (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ʰ
h heads out of n flips, if the chance of heads is p.
🧮 Log-likelihood
ℓ(p) = h log p + (n − h) log(1 − p)
Same best p, but sums instead of products: easier calculus, no underflow.
🧮 The answer
p̂ = hn
Set the derivative h/p − (n − h)/(1 − p) to 0 and solve.
ModelWhat maximum likelihood gives
coin (Bernoulli)p̂ = share of successes
normal dataμ̂ = sample mean, σ̂² = average squared deviation (divides by n)
linear regression with normal noiseminimise the sum of squared errors (least squares)
logistic regressionminimise log loss, also called cross entropy (Chapter 12)
Training a classifier by minimising cross entropy loss IS maximum likelihood. Every time you call fit, an optimiser like Adam (Chapter 8) climbs a log-likelihood surface just like the one in the animation, only in millions of dimensions.

📈Regression, R² and the bias and variance trade-off

Linear regression fits the straight line ŷ = a + b x that makes the squared vertical misses (residuals) as small as possible. It is both a statistics tool and the simplest machine learning model.

🧮 Least squares line
b = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)Σ (xᵢ − x̄)²    a = ȳ − b x̄
Slope = covariance ÷ variance of x. The line always passes through the point (x̄, ȳ).
🧮 R² (coefficient of determination)
R² = 1 − SSresSStot = 1 − Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²Σ (yᵢ − ȳ)²
The share of the variation in y that the line explains. For a straight line fit, R² = r².
🧮 Bias and variance decomposition
expected test error = bias² + variance + noise
Bias: errors from a model too simple to capture the pattern. Variance: errors from a model so flexible it chases the random quirks of its training sample.
Too simple (high bias)Just rightToo flexible (high variance)

test errorhighlowhigh

fixmore features, bigger modelkeep itmore data, regularisation, simpler model
Always judge a model on data it has never seen. A high R² on the training data alone says little about the future.
🔍 Going deeper: watch overfitting happen, and fix it with cross validation

Ten noisy points are drawn from a smooth curve. Raise the polynomial degree below and watch two error numbers: the training error, on the ten points the curve can see, and the test error, on fresh points from the same curve. Training error always falls as the degree grows. Test error falls at first and then shoots up as the curve starts threading through the noise.

To choose the degree (or any setting) honestly you need data the model did not train on. With little data, setting aside a single test set wastes a lot of it. k fold cross validation reuses every point.

  1. Split the data into k equal parts, called folds. 5 or 10 is common.
  2. Train on k − 1 folds and measure the error on the one left out.
  3. Repeat k times so that every fold is left out once.
  4. Average the k errors. Pick the setting with the lowest average, then retrain on all the data.
Keep a final test set that is never used during cross validation. If you tune on the same data you report on, the score is too optimistic, a subtle form of overfitting called data leakage.
