Part 1

⛷️Gradient descent: walk downhill

Training a model means finding the weights that make the loss as small as possible. Gradient descent does it the way a skier finds the bottom of a valley in fog: feel the slope under your feet, take a small step downhill, and repeat.

🧮 The gradient descent update
w ← w − η ∇L(w)
w are the weights, ∇L is the gradient of the loss (Chapter 7) and η (eta) is the learning rate, the size of each step.
Gradient descent never needs to see the whole landscape. Like a ball rolling downhill, it only needs the slope where it is standing right now.
Part 2

🎛️The learning rate: not too small, not too big

The learning rate is the most important setting in training. Too small and training crawls. Too big and every step overshoots the valley floor, bouncing from side to side or flying off completely.

Learning rateWhat happensLoss curve
far too smalltiny steps, training takes foreverfalls very slowly
about rightbig steps at first, smaller near the bottomfalls fast, then flattens
a bit too bigovershoots, zig zags across the valleyfalls with wobbles
far too bigevery step lands higher than the lastshoots up, often to NaN
🧮 Stability rule for a bowl L = ½ a w²
w ← (1 − ηa) w    converges when 0 < η < 2a
a is the curvature (second derivative). Steeper bowls need smaller steps. For many weights, a is the largest Hessian eigenvalue.
A common recipe: try learning rates spaced by factors of 10 (0.1, 0.01, 0.001), watch the loss for a few hundred steps, and keep the largest one that still falls smoothly. Adam usually starts at 0.001.
Adjusting a shower: turn the tap a tiny bit and you wait ages for the right temperature. Turn it a lot and you swing between freezing and scalding. A sensible turn gets you there in a couple of goes.
Part 3

🎲Stochastic and mini batch gradient descent

The true gradient averages over every training example. With a million examples, that is a million calculations for one step. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates the gradient from a small random sample instead, and takes many more, cheaper steps.

VersionExamples per stepProsCons
Batch GDall nexact gradient, smooth pathslow, needs all data in memory
Stochastic GD1very cheap steps, noise can escape bad spotsvery noisy path
Mini batch GDtypically 32 to 1024fast on GPUs, mild noiseone more setting to choose
🧮 Mini batch gradient
g = 1B Σi ∈ batch ∇Lᵢ(w)
An unbiased estimate of the full gradient. Its noise shrinks like 1/√B.
🧮 Epoch
updates per epoch = nB
One epoch is one full pass over the data. 50000 images with B = 100 gives 500 updates per epoch.
Shuffle the data every epoch. If the examples always come in the same order, for example all cats then all dogs, the weights get dragged back and forth and training suffers.
Part 4

🚀Momentum, RMSProp and Adam

Plain gradient descent struggles in long narrow valleys. The steep walls make it zig zag while the gentle floor makes it crawl. Smarter optimisers fix this with two tricks: remember past directions (momentum), and give each weight its own step size (adaptive learning rates).

🧮 Momentum
v ← β v + ∇L    w ← w − η v
v is a running sum of gradients, like a heavy ball gathering speed. Zig zags cancel out, steady directions add up. β ≈ 0.9.
🧮 RMSProp
s ← ρ s + (1 − ρ) g²    w ← w − η g ÷ √s
Divide by the typical size of recent gradients, so steep directions get smaller steps and flat ones bigger.
🧮 Adam (momentum plus RMSProp)
m ← β₁m + (1 − β₁)g   v ← β₂v + (1 − β₂)g²
w ← w − η m̂ ÷ (√v̂ + ε)
m̂ = m/(1 − β₁ᵗ) and v̂ = v/(1 − β₂ᵗ) fix the start up bias. Defaults: β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸.
OptimiserTypical use
SGD with momentumimage models such as ResNets, often with the best final accuracy
Adamthe default for most projects, quick to get working
AdamWAdam with weight decay done properly, standard for transformers and large language models
The first Adam step always moves each weight by about η, whatever the size of the gradient, because m̂ ÷ √v̂ = g ÷ |g| = ±1 at t = 1.
Part 5

🗺️Convexity, local minima and saddles

For a convex loss there is one valley, so gradient descent with a sensible learning rate always reaches the best answer. Linear regression, logistic regression and SVMs have convex losses. Neural networks do not: their landscapes have many valleys, plateaus and saddles.

FeatureWhat it isHow training copes
Global minimumthe lowest point of allthe ideal, rarely guaranteed for neural networks
Local minimuma valley that is not the lowestin big networks most are nearly as good as the global one
Saddle pointflat, but downhill in some directionsnoise from SGD and momentum push the weights off
Plateaua wide nearly flat regionAdam helps by enlarging tiny gradients
Cliffa sudden very steep wallgradient clipping caps the step size
🧮 Condition number of the loss
κ = λmax ÷ λmin of the Hessian
Large κ means a long narrow valley. Plain gradient descent needs roughly κ steps to make progress, which is why feature scaling and adaptive optimisers matter.
Good starting weights matter in non convex problems. Schemes like Xavier and He initialisation pick random starting values scaled so that signals neither explode nor vanish through the layers.
Part 6

🔒Optimising with constraints: Lagrange multipliers

Sometimes the answer must obey a rule: a fixed budget, a fixed length of fence, probabilities that add to 1. At the best point on the constraint, you cannot do better by sliding along it, which happens exactly when the contour of the goal just touches the constraint. There, the two gradients are parallel.

🧮 Lagrange condition
∇f = λ ∇g    together with    g(x) = c
f is what you optimise, g(x) = c is the rule. The multiplier λ tells how much the best value of f improves per extra unit of c.
Support vector machines are found with Lagrange multipliers, and the training points with non zero multipliers are the "support vectors". L2 regularisation is the same as asking the weights to stay inside a ball, ‖w‖ ≤ r, and the regularisation strength plays the part of λ.
Part 7

🛠️Training tricks: schedules, clipping and stopping

Real training runs add a few simple tools around the optimiser.

ToolWhat it doesWhy
Learning rate warm upstart with a tiny learning rate and grow it over the first stepsearly gradients are wild while the Adam averages are still empty
Step or cosine decaylower the learning rate as training goes onbig steps to explore, small steps to settle into the valley
Gradient clippingif ‖g‖ > c, scale g down to length cstops one huge gradient from wrecking the weights
Early stoppingstop when the validation loss stops improvingprevents overfitting and saves time
Weight decayshrink every weight a little each step: w ← w − ηλwthe L2 regularisation of Chapter 12
🧮 Cosine decay
η(t) = ηmax × ½ (1 + cos(π t ÷ T))
Starts at the maximum and glides smoothly to 0 at step T.
🧮 Gradient clipping by norm
g ← g × min(1, c‖g‖)
Keeps the direction, caps the length at c.
If the loss suddenly becomes NaN, the usual suspects are a learning rate that is too big, a missing gradient clip, or a log of zero somewhere in the loss.
