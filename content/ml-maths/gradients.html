Gradients and Backpropagation

A model has many weights, so its loss is a surface in many directions. Slice it with partial derivatives, follow gradient arrows downhill on a contour map, meet the Jacobian and Hessian, and run backpropagation through a graph one node at a time.

Part 1

🎚️Partial derivatives: one knob at a time

Real things depend on many inputs. A house price depends on its size and its age. A model loss depends on millions of weights. A partial derivative asks how the output changes when you turn one input and hold every other input still.

🧮 Partial derivative
∂f∂x = slope of f when only x moves
The curly ∂ means "partial". Treat every other letter as a fixed number, then use the ordinary rules from Chapter 6.
A baker finds that cake sales rise by 30 per week for each 1 degree warmer day (with price fixed), and fall by 50 for each extra rupee (with temperature fixed). Those two numbers are partial derivatives of sales.
Part 2

🧗The gradient: the steepest way uphill

Collect all the partial derivatives into one vector and you get the gradient, written ∇f (said "grad f" or "del f"). It is the most important object in machine learning.

🧮 Gradient
∇f = ∂f/∂x₁∂f/∂x₂⋮∂f/∂xₙ
It points in the direction of steepest increase. Its length says how steep that climb is. Minus the gradient is the steepest way down.
Fact about ∇fWhy it matters
points straight uphillso −∇f points straight downhill, the way to reduce a loss
is perpendicular to the contour linescontours are paths of equal height, and uphill is at right angles to them
has length 0 at peaks, valleys and saddlesa zero gradient means training has stopped moving
has one entry per inputa model with 7 billion weights has a gradient with 7 billion entries
Picture standing on a hillside in fog. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground tilts under your feet. That tilt is the gradient, and gradient descent is taking a small step the other way.
Part 3

📐Directional derivatives and linear approximation

The gradient answers "how fast does f change if I step in any direction u?" Just take a dot product. And near a point, every smooth surface looks almost flat, so a tangent plane predicts nearby values.

🧮 Directional derivative
Du f = ∇f · u
u is a unit vector. It is largest when u points along ∇f, equal to ‖∇f‖, and zero along a contour.
🧮 Linear (first order) approximation
f(x + Δ) ≈ f(x) + ∇f(x) · Δ
The tangent plane. Good for small steps, which is why learning rates are kept small.
Every gradient descent step trusts this linear approximation: the loss should drop by about ‖∇L‖² × learning rate. If the learning rate is too large, the step leaves the region where the surface looks flat, and the loss can go up instead.
Part 4

🧮Jacobian and Hessian matrices

Two matrices organise all the derivatives of many inputs at once.

🧮 Jacobian (first derivatives of many outputs)
J = ∂f₁/∂x₁∂f₁/∂x₂∂f₂/∂x₁∂f₂/∂x₂
Row i holds the gradient of output i. For a layer z = Wx + b the Jacobian ∂z/∂x is just W.
🧮 Hessian (second derivatives of one output)
H = ∂²f/∂x²∂²f/∂x∂y∂²f/∂y∂x∂²f/∂y²
It describes the curvature of the surface. It is symmetric, because the order of differentiating does not matter for smooth f.
Hessian at a flat pointShapeTest for 2 inputs
all eigenvalues positivebowl: a minimumdet H > 0 and ∂²f/∂x² > 0
all eigenvalues negativecap: a maximumdet H > 0 and ∂²f/∂x² < 0
mixed signssaddle: up one way, down anotherdet H < 0
In high dimensions most flat points of a neural network loss are saddles, not bad minima. Gradient descent with a little noise slides off them. The eigenvalues of the Hessian also set the largest safe learning rate, roughly 2 ÷ λmax.
Part 5

🔗The chain rule on a computational graph

Any formula can be drawn as a computational graph: input boxes flow through small operations (add, multiply, sigmoid) to the output. The forward pass computes values left to right. The backward pass sends gradients right to left, and each node multiplies by its own local slope. That is the multivariable chain rule.

🧮 Multivariable chain rule
∂L∂x = Σpaths (product of local slopes along the path)
If x affects L through several routes, add up the contributions of every route.
NodeForwardBackward rule
add: q = a + baddspasses the incoming gradient unchanged to both inputs
multiply: q = a × bmultipliessends gradient × b to a, and gradient × a to b (swap)
max: q = max(a, b)picks the biggersends the whole gradient to the winner only, like ReLU
sigmoid: q = σ(a)squashesmultiplies the gradient by q(1 − q)
Add nodes copy, multiply nodes swap, max nodes route, and every node multiplies by its local slope.
Part 6

🧠Backpropagation through a neuron

Now put it all together for one neuron. Inputs x = [1, 2], weights w = [0.5, −0.3], bias b = 0.1, a sigmoid, and the squared error loss against the target y = 1.

Backpropagation costs about the same as two forward passes, no matter how many weights there are. Computing each partial derivative separately with finite differences would need one extra forward pass per weight, millions of times slower. This efficiency is what made deep learning practical.
Part 7

🤖Gradients for whole datasets

A real loss is the average over many examples, so its gradient is the average of the per example gradients. With matrices, the whole batch is done in one line.

🧮 Mean squared error for linear regression
L(w) = 1n ‖Xw − y‖²
X holds one row per example, w the weights, y the targets.
🧮 Its gradient
∇L = 2n Xᵀ(Xw − y)
Errors weighted by each feature, then averaged. Setting it to zero gives the normal equations of Chapter 4.
TermMeaning
Batch gradientaverage gradient over the whole dataset, accurate but slow
Mini batch gradientaverage over 32 to 1024 random examples, the standard choice
Stochastic gradientgradient from one example, very noisy but cheap
Autogradthe library builds the computational graph and runs backprop for you
Shapes must match: the gradient of a loss with respect to a weight matrix always has the same shape as that weight matrix.
