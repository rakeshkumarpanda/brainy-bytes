1.1

Numbers, Algebra and Equations

Open chapter

The school maths underneath every model: kinds of numbers, the order of operations, percentages, equations that stay balanced, powers, the sigma sign and absolute value. Each idea is linked to how a machine learning model uses it.

Key ideas

  • Why machine learning needs maths A machine learning model is a function with knobs . You give it numbers (the size of a house, the pixels of a photo, the words of a message) and it gives back a number (a price, the word "cat", the chance that a message is spam).
  • Kinds of numbers and how computers store them Numbers come in families. Each family is bigger than the one before and contains it.
  • Order of operations, fractions and percentages When an expression mixes operations, everyone must work it out in the same order, or two people would get two different answers.
  • Variables, expressions and equations A variable is a box with a name, such as x, that holds a number we do not know yet. An expression such as 3x + 5 is a recipe that uses the box. An equation such as 3x + 5 = 20 says two things are equal, and solving it means finding what is in the box.
  • Powers, roots and scientific notation A power is repeated multiplication: 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. The small number 5 is the exponent and 2 is the base .
  • Sigma and Pi notation: the maths shorthand Machine learning formulas add up many terms at once. Writing x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ... + xₙ is slow, so mathematicians use the Greek capital letter sigma, Σ, which means "sum".
  • Absolute value, inequalities and distance The absolute value |x| is the distance of x from zero, so it is never negative: |5| = 5 and |−5| = 5. The distance between two numbers a and b is |a − b|, whichever is bigger.

Functions, Graphs, Exponents and Logarithms

Open chapter

Functions are the machines models are made of. Learn straight lines, parabolas, exponential growth, the number e, logarithms and the sigmoid, ReLU and tanh activations, with sliders and graphs you can drag.

Key ideas

  • What a function is A function is a machine with one rule: every input gives exactly one output. A vending machine is a function. Press B4 and you always get the same snack. We write the machine as f and its output for input x as f(x), read "f of x".
  • Straight lines: slope and intercept The simplest useful function is a straight line. Two numbers describe it completely.
  • Quadratics and polynomials A quadratic has an x² term: f(x) = ax² + bx + c. Its graph is a parabola , a U shape when a > 0 and an upside down U when a < 0. The bottom (or top) is called the vertex .
  • Exponential growth and the number e In an exponential function the variable sits in the exponent: f(x) = aˣ. Each step multiplies instead of adding. With a > 1 it grows faster and faster, with 0 < a < 1 it shrinks towards zero but never reaches it.
  • Logarithms: the undo button for powers A logarithm answers the question "what power do I need?". Since 2³ = 8, log₂ 8 = 3. Since 10² = 100, log₁₀ 100 = 2. The natural log, ln, uses base e.
  • The S curve and other functions machine learning uses Some functions show up in machine learning again and again. They are called activation functions because they decide how strongly a neuron "fires".
  • Composing, shifting and inverting functions Composition feeds one function into another: (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)). Work from the inside out. If g(x) = 3x − 1 and f is the sigmoid, then f(g(1)) = σ(2) ≈ 0.88.

2.1

Vectors

Open chapter

Vectors are lists of numbers and arrows at the same time. Add and scale them, measure their length, compare them with the dot product and cosine similarity, cast shadows with projections, and learn what span, independence and a basis mean.

Key ideas

  • What a vector is A vector is an ordered list of numbers. You can picture it in two ways, and both are useful.
  • Adding, subtracting and scaling vectors Vectors are added part by part: [3, 1] + [1, 2] = [4, 3]. As arrows, put the tail of the second at the tip of the first. The sum goes from the very start to the very end. Multiplying by a number (a scalar ) stretches the arrow: 2 × [3, 1] = [6, 2].
  • Length (norm) and distance The length of a vector is called its norm . The usual one comes straight from Pythagoras: [3, 4] has length √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5. There are other useful ways to measure size.
  • The dot product The dot product turns two vectors into one number. Multiply matching parts, then add: [2, 3] · [4, −1] = 2 × 4 + 3 × (−1) = 8 − 3 = 5. It also has a meaning in pictures: it measures how much two arrows point the same way.
  • Cosine similarity: how alike are two things If you divide the dot product by both lengths, you get cos θ alone. This is cosine similarity .
  • Projection: the shadow of a vector Shine a light straight down onto a line. The shadow a vector casts on that line is its projection . It tells you how much of one vector lies along the direction of another.
  • Span, independence and basis Take all the linear combinations of some vectors. Every point you can reach is called their span . With [1, 0] and [0, 1] you can reach any point in the plane: [5, −2] = 5·[1, 0] − 2·[0, 1].

Matrices and Matrix Multiplication

Open chapter

Matrices hold datasets, images and the weights of every layer. Multiply them step by step with an animation you can edit, watch a matrix bend the plane, find determinants and inverses, solve systems with elimination, and fit a regression line with the normal equations.

Key ideas

  • What a matrix is A matrix is a rectangle of numbers arranged in rows and columns. Its shape is written rows × columns, so a matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns is 2 × 3. The entry in row i and column j is written aᵢⱼ.
  • Adding, scaling and transposing Matrices of the same shape add entry by entry. Multiplying by a number scales every entry. The transpose Aᵀ flips a matrix over its diagonal, so rows become columns and a 2 × 3 matrix becomes 3 × 2.
  • Matrix multiplication, step by step To multiply A by B, each entry of the answer is a dot product : row i of A with column j of B. Walk along the row and down the column together, multiply the pairs, and add.
  • A matrix is a transformation Multiplying a vector by a matrix moves it. Every 2 × 2 matrix stretches, squashes, rotates, shears or flips the whole plane, and keeps straight lines straight. That is why it is called a linear transformation .
  • Identity, determinant and inverse The identity matrix I has 1s on its diagonal and 0s elsewhere. It does nothing: AI = IA = A, just like multiplying by 1. The inverse A⁻¹ undoes A: A⁻¹A = I.
  • Solving many equations at once A system of linear equations is one matrix equation Ax = b . The matrix A holds the numbers in front of the unknowns, x is the vector of unknowns, and b is the right hand side.
  • Rank and special matrices The rank of a matrix is the number of truly independent columns (or rows, it is always the same number). It says how many dimensions the matrix really uses. A 1000 × 50 data matrix with rank 30 has 20 columns that are combinations of others.

Eigenvalues, SVD and PCA

Open chapter

Some directions are special for a matrix: it only stretches them. Hunt for these eigenvectors with an animation, find them by hand, watch the SVD turn a circle into an ellipse, and run PCA step by step to squash data into fewer numbers.

Key ideas

  • Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix keeps Chapter 4 showed that a matrix moves every arrow in the plane. Most arrows get turned to a new direction. A few special arrows only get longer, shorter or flipped , and stay on their own line.
  • Finding eigenvalues by hand
  • Rules that save time Eigenvalues come with a handful of rules that let you check your work in seconds and that explain why they are so useful.
  • Power iteration and PageRank Real matrices have thousands or millions of rows, so nobody solves a characteristic equation. A simple and famous method is power iteration : start with any vector, multiply by A, scale it back to length 1, and repeat.
  • Singular value decomposition (SVD) Eigenvectors need a square matrix, and even then they might not exist. The SVD works for every matrix of any shape. It says each matrix is three simple moves in a row: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and another rotation.
  • Principal component analysis (PCA) PCA finds the directions in which your data spreads out the most. Keep the top few and you can describe 100 features with, say, 10 numbers, while keeping most of the information. It is eigenvectors applied to data.
  • Where machine learning uses all this Eigenvalues and the SVD appear wherever a model needs the "main directions" of something. Here is a quick map, which later chapters revisit.

Derivatives and Integrals

Open chapter

A derivative is a speedometer for any function. Watch a secant line become a tangent, learn the rules that make derivatives quick, master the chain rule behind backpropagation, find peaks and valleys, and fill areas with rectangles to meet the integral.

Key ideas

  • Rate of change and slope A derivative answers one question: how fast is something changing right now? A car that travels 120 km in 2 hours has an average speed of 60 km/h, but the speedometer shows the speed at this exact moment. The derivative is the speedometer.
  • Limits and the derivative from first principles A limit says what value a formula approaches as the input gets close to some number, even if you cannot plug that number in. You cannot set h = 0 in (f(a + h) − f(a)) ÷ h because that divides by zero, but you can ask what happens as h shrinks.
  • The rules of differentiation Nobody works from first principles every time. A small set of rules covers almost every function you will meet in machine learning.
  • The chain rule Most functions in machine learning are functions inside functions. A neuron computes z = wx + b, then a = σ(z), then a loss L(a). The chain rule says how a small change at the start ripples through every link.
  • Peaks, valleys and the second derivative Where a smooth curve reaches a peak or the bottom of a valley, it is flat for a moment, so its slope is 0. Setting f′(x) = 0 finds these critical points . The second derivative f″(x), the slope of the slope, tells which kind it is.
  • Integrals: adding up tiny pieces An integral is the opposite job of a derivative. A derivative turns distance into speed. An integral turns speed back into distance, by adding up "speed × tiny time" over and over. In pictures, it is the area under a curve .
  • Why derivatives make machines learn Training a model means making a loss number small. The derivative of the loss with respect to a weight says which way to nudge that weight and how strongly. If ∂L/∂w is positive, increasing w raises the loss, so decrease it. If it is negative, increase w.

Gradients and Backpropagation

Open chapter

A model has many weights, so its loss is a surface in many directions. Slice it with partial derivatives, follow gradient arrows downhill on a contour map, meet the Jacobian and Hessian, and run backpropagation through a graph one node at a time.

Key ideas

  • Partial derivatives: one knob at a time Real things depend on many inputs. A house price depends on its size and its age. A model loss depends on millions of weights. A partial derivative asks how the output changes when you turn one input and hold every other input still.
  • The gradient: the steepest way uphill Collect all the partial derivatives into one vector and you get the gradient , written ∇f (said "grad f" or "del f"). It is the most important object in machine learning.
  • Directional derivatives and linear approximation The gradient answers "how fast does f change if I step in any direction u?" Just take a dot product. And near a point, every smooth surface looks almost flat, so a tangent plane predicts nearby values.
  • Jacobian and Hessian matrices Two matrices organise all the derivatives of many inputs at once.
  • The chain rule on a computational graph Any formula can be drawn as a computational graph : input boxes flow through small operations (add, multiply, sigmoid) to the output. The forward pass computes values left to right.
  • Backpropagation through a neuron Now put it all together for one neuron. Inputs x = [1, 2], weights w = [0.5, −0.3], bias b = 0.1, a sigmoid, and the squared error loss against the target y = 1.
  • Gradients for whole datasets A real loss is the average over many examples, so its gradient is the average of the per example gradients. With matrices, the whole batch is done in one line.

Gradient Descent and Optimisation

Open chapter

Training a model is walking downhill on its loss. Run gradient descent step by step, find the learning rate that is just right, see why mini batches work, race plain gradient descent against momentum and Adam, and solve problems with constraints using Lagrange multipliers.

Key ideas

  • Gradient descent: walk downhill Training a model means finding the weights that make the loss as small as possible. Gradient descent does it the way a skier finds the bottom of a valley in fog: feel the slope under your feet, take a small step downhill, and repeat.
  • The learning rate: not too small, not too big The learning rate is the most important setting in training. Too small and training crawls. Too big and every step overshoots the valley floor, bouncing from side to side or flying off completely.
  • Stochastic and mini batch gradient descent The true gradient averages over every training example. With a million examples, that is a million calculations for one step. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) estimates the gradient from a small random sample instead, and takes many more, cheaper steps.
  • Momentum, RMSProp and Adam Plain gradient descent struggles in long narrow valleys. The steep walls make it zig zag while the gentle floor makes it crawl.
  • Convexity, local minima and saddles For a convex loss there is one valley, so gradient descent with a sensible learning rate always reaches the best answer. Linear regression, logistic regression and SVMs have convex losses.
  • Optimising with constraints: Lagrange multipliers Sometimes the answer must obey a rule: a fixed budget, a fixed length of fence, probabilities that add to 1.
  • Training tricks: schedules, clipping and stopping Real training runs add a few simple tools around the optimiser.

Probability and Bayes' Theorem

Open chapter

Probability is the maths of uncertainty. Roll virtual dice, see the addition and multiplication rules on a grid of outcomes, count with permutations and combinations, read conditional probabilities from a table, and sort 500 people to understand Bayes' theorem before building a naive Bayes spam filter.

Key ideas

  • Chance, outcomes and events Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.5 means as likely as not. The list of everything that could happen is the sample space , and any group of outcomes you care about is an event .
  • AND, OR and independence Two rules combine simple probabilities into bigger ones. OR means "at least one happens". AND means "both happen".
  • Counting: permutations and combinations When outcomes are equally likely, probability is just counting. Three tools do most of the counting.
  • Conditional probability A conditional probability answers "how likely is A, now that I know B happened?" Knowing B shrinks the world down to just the outcomes where B is true.
  • Bayes' theorem: updating beliefs The theorem of Bayes turns P(B | A) around into P(A | B). It is the rule for updating what you believe when new evidence arrives: start with a prior , see evidence, and get a posterior .
  • Naive Bayes: a classifier from counting Naive Bayes uses the theorem of Bayes to classify, for example spam or not spam. It makes one bold (naive) simplification: given the class, every word is independent of the others.
  • Probability inside machine learning Most models do not just say "cat". They say "cat with probability 0.92". Probability is how machine learning expresses uncertainty.

Random Variables and Distributions

Open chapter

A distribution says which values a random quantity is likely to take. Build binomial bars one at a time, shade areas under the bell curve, watch lopsided data turn bell shaped through the central limit theorem, and measure how two variables move together.

Key ideas

  • Random variables, PMF, PDF and CDF A random variable is a number that comes out of a chance event: the face of a die, the number of heads in ten flips, or the time until the next bus. We write it with a capital letter such as X, and a value it can take with a small letter such as x.
  • Expected value and variance The expected value E[X] (also called the mean μ) is the long run average of X if you could repeat the experiment forever.
  • Bernoulli and binomial: counting successes A Bernoulli trial has two results: success (1) with chance p or failure (0) with chance 1 − p. A coin toss, a free throw and "did the user click?" are all Bernoulli trials.
  • Poisson, uniform and exponential The Poisson distribution counts how many events happen in a fixed stretch of time or space when they arrive at random at an average rate λ (lambda). Calls to a help desk per hour, typos per page and goals per match are classic examples.
  • The normal distribution and z-scores The normal (or Gaussian) distribution is the famous bell curve. It is set by two numbers: the mean μ, which places the centre, and the standard deviation σ, which sets the width. Heights, measurement errors and exam scores are often close to normal.
  • The central limit theorem The central limit theorem (CLT) explains why the bell curve is everywhere. Take many independent random values from almost any distribution (it only needs a finite variance), and their average is close to normally distributed.
  • Covariance, correlation and distributions in ML So far each variable lived alone. Covariance asks whether two variables move together. If X is above its mean when Y is above its mean, the covariance is positive. Correlation rescales covariance to a number between −1 and 1 so it is easy to read.

4.3

Statistics and Inference

Open chapter

Statistics turns a sample into honest conclusions. Describe data with the right summaries, watch confidence intervals catch (or miss) the true mean, run a hypothesis test and an A/B test step by step, climb a likelihood curve to its peak, and fit a regression line while keeping an eye on overfitting.

Key ideas

  • Describing data: centre, spread and outliers Statistics turns a pile of numbers into a few that tell the story. The first questions are always: where is the centre, and how spread out is the data?
  • Samples, bias and standard error We rarely measure everyone. The whole group we care about is the population . The part we actually measure is the sample . A number worked out from the sample (such as x̄) is a statistic , and it is our estimate of the population parameter (such as μ).
  • Confidence intervals A single estimate such as "18.2 hours" hides its uncertainty. A confidence interval gives a range: estimate ± margin of error. A 95% confidence interval is built by a method that catches the true value in 95% of samples.
  • Hypothesis tests and p-values A hypothesis test asks whether the data is surprising enough to doubt a default claim. The default, "nothing special is going on", is the null hypothesis H₀ . The claim we are testing for is the alternative H₁ .
  • A/B testing An A/B test is a hypothesis test that companies run every day. Visitors are split at random between the current version A and a new version B, and we compare a rate such as the share who buy.
  • Maximum likelihood estimation Maximum likelihood answers "which parameter value makes the data we saw most probable?" It is the idea behind how most machine learning models are trained.
  • Regression, R² and the bias and variance trade-off Linear regression fits the straight line ŷ = a + b x that makes the squared vertical misses (residuals) as small as possible. It is both a statistics tool and the simplest machine learning model.

5.1

Entropy, Loss Functions and Metrics

Open chapter

This is where the maths meets real models. Measure surprise with entropy, see how cross entropy and softmax train classifiers, compare MSE, MAE and log loss, shrink weights with regularisation, and judge a model with precision, recall and the ROC curve.

Key ideas

  • Information and entropy Some news tells you a lot, some tells you almost nothing. "The sun rose today" is no surprise. "It snowed in the Sahara" is a big surprise. Information theory measures surprise with a number, and entropy is the average surprise of a random event.
  • Cross entropy and KL divergence Suppose reality follows p, but your model believes q. You will be surprised more often than necessary. Cross entropy measures your average surprise, and the extra over the unavoidable entropy is the KL divergence .
  • Softmax, temperature and log-sum-exp A classifier first produces raw scores called logits , which can be any real numbers. Softmax turns them into probabilities: all positive and adding to 1, with the biggest logit getting the biggest share.
  • Loss functions for regression A loss function turns "how wrong was the model?" into one number that training then makes smaller. The choice of loss decides what kind of mistakes the model cares most about.
  • Log loss for classification For a yes or no classifier that outputs a probability p, the standard loss is log loss , also called binary cross entropy. It is the cross entropy from earlier with only two classes.
  • Regularisation: L2 and L1 A flexible model can fit noise (overfitting, Chapter 11). Regularisation adds a penalty for large weights to the loss, so the model only uses big weights when the data really demands them.
  • Classification metrics, ROC and AUC Accuracy alone can fool you. If 99% of card payments are honest, a model that never flags fraud is 99% accurate and completely useless. The confusion matrix shows what really happened.

Key ideas

  • Quadratic forms and positive definite matrices A quadratic form is the matrix version of ax². For a symmetric matrix A and a vector x, the number xᵀAx is a sum of squares and cross terms. In two dimensions it is a surface over the plane, and its shape is decided by A.

  • Matrix norms, trace and the condition number Vectors have lengths (Chapter 3). Matrices need a size too, for regularising weight matrices, for measuring how far apart two matrices are, and for judging how sensitive a problem is. There are a few standard choices.
  • Tensors, shapes and broadcasting A tensor in machine learning code is simply an array with any number of directions, called axes or dimensions. A number is a 0D tensor, a vector is 1D, a matrix is 2D, and so on. The shape lists the size along each axis.

Taylor Series, Newton’s Method and Automatic Differentiation

Open chapter

Approximate functions with Taylor polynomials, solve and minimise with Newton’s method, use matrix calculus rules with a shape check, watch automatic differentiation run forward and backward, understand vanishing and exploding gradients, use convexity and Jensen’s inequality, and keep numbers stable on a real computer.

Key ideas

  • Taylor series: curves as polynomials Computers can only add and multiply. To find e 0.5 or sin 0.3, a calculator uses a polynomial that copies the function very closely near one point. That copy is a Taylor polynomial .
  • Newton’s method for roots and minimums To solve f(x) = 0, Newton’s method draws the tangent line at your current guess and jumps to the point where that line crosses zero. The tangent line is the order 1 Taylor polynomial, so each step solves a simpler, straight line version of the problem.
  • Matrix calculus: gradients without index pain When a function takes a whole vector or matrix as input, we want its gradient as a vector or matrix of the same shape.
  • Automatic differentiation: forward and reverse mode There are three ways a computer can find derivatives. Numerical differences, (f(x + h) − f(x)) ÷ h, are easy but slow and slightly inaccurate. Symbolic differentiation, like a computer algebra system, produces exact formulas that can grow huge.
  • Vanishing and exploding gradients Backpropagation through a deep network multiplies many factors together, one per layer. Each factor is roughly (slope of the activation) × (weight). If those factors are a little below 1, their product shrinks towards 0 and the early layers stop learning.
  • Convexity and Jensen’s inequality A function is convex when the straight line (chord) between any two points on its graph never goes below the graph. Think of the shape of a bowl.
  • Numerical stability: when maths meets floating point Floating point numbers (Chapter 1) have about 16 significant digits in float64 and about 7 in float32. Formulas that are perfect on paper can lose all their accuracy on a computer. Three situations come up again and again in machine learning.

6.3

Bayesian Inference, Markov Chains and Sampling

Open chapter

Handle uncertainty the way modern models do. Read joint, marginal and conditional distributions, measure distance with the multivariate normal, update beliefs with Bayes and conjugate priors, watch Markov chains settle, estimate with Monte Carlo, sample posteriors with Metropolis, and see how concentration inequalities guarantee how close an average is.

Key ideas

  • Joint, marginal and conditional distributions Real data has several variables at once: the weather and whether people carry umbrellas, a patient’s age and blood pressure, a pixel and its neighbour. The joint distribution P(X, Y) gives the chance of every combination. From it you can get everything else.
  • The multivariate normal and Mahalanobis distance The bell curve of Chapter 10 has a version for many variables at once, the multivariate normal . It is described by a mean vector μ (the centre) and a covariance matrix Σ (the spread of each variable and how pairs move together).
  • Bayesian inference: priors, posteriors and MAP Chapter 9 used Bayes’ rule for events. Bayesian inference uses it for an unknown parameter , such as the chance θ that a coin lands heads. Instead of one best guess, you keep a whole distribution of belief over θ and update it with data.
  • Markov chains and stationary distributions A Markov chain moves between states at random, and the chance of the next state depends only on the current one, not on the history. Weather from day to day, a shopper moving between web pages and the next word in a very simple text model are common examples.
  • Monte Carlo and inverse transform sampling Monte Carlo methods answer questions by simulation. To find an average, a probability or an integral, draw many random samples and average the results.
  • MCMC and the Metropolis algorithm Bayesian posteriors are often known only up to a constant: we can compute likelihood × prior, but not p(data), which needs an impossible integral. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) still produces samples.
  • Concentration inequalities and the law of large numbers The law of large numbers says that the average of many independent samples gets close to the true mean. Concentration inequalities say how close , with a guarantee rather than an approximation.
