Part 1

🎲Chance, outcomes and events

Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.5 means as likely as not. The list of everything that could happen is the sample space, and any group of outcomes you care about is an event.

🧮 Equally likely outcomes
P(A) = number of outcomes in Atotal number of outcomes
A fair die: P(even) = 3/6 = 0.5.
🧮 Complement
P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
Often the easy way round: P(at least one six in 4 rolls) = 1 − (5/6)⁴ ≈ 0.518.

Part 2

➕AND, OR and independence

Two rules combine simple probabilities into bigger ones. OR means "at least one happens". AND means "both happen".

🧮 Addition rule (OR)
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Subtract the overlap so it is not counted twice. If A and B cannot happen together (mutually exclusive), the overlap is 0.
🧮 Multiplication rule (AND)
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A)
If A and B are independent, P(B | A) = P(B) and this becomes P(A) × P(B).
WordsMeaningExample
mutually exclusivecannot both happen, P(A ∩ B) = 0rolling a 2 and rolling a 5 on one die

Mutually exclusive events are NOT independent. If A happens, B cannot, so knowing A tells you a lot about B.
OR adds (then subtract the overlap). AND multiplies (when independent).
Part 3

🔢Counting: permutations and combinations

When outcomes are equally likely, probability is just counting. Three tools do most of the counting.

🧮 Factorial
n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1
Ways to arrange n different things in a row. 5! = 120. By agreement 0! = 1.
🧮 Permutations (order matters)
P(n, k) = n!(n − k)!
Gold, silver and bronze from 10 runners: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720.
🧮 Combinations (order does not matter)
C(n, k) = n!k! (n − k)!
A team of 3 from 10 people: 720 ÷ 3! = 120. Also written ⁿCₖ or "n choose k".
Ask "does the order matter?" A podium, a PIN and a password are permutations. A team, a hand of cards and a lottery ticket are combinations.
Counting explains why brute force fails. A model choosing 10 features out of 100 has C(100, 10) ≈ 17 trillion subsets to try, which is why feature selection uses clever search instead.

🔍Conditional probability

A conditional probability answers "how likely is A, now that I know B happened?" Knowing B shrinks the world down to just the outcomes where B is true.

🧮 Conditional probability
P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B)

Part 5

🔄Bayes' theorem: updating beliefs

The theorem of Bayes turns P(B | A) around into P(A | B). It is the rule for updating what you believe when new evidence arrives: start with a prior, see evidence, and get a posterior.

🧮 Bayes' theorem
P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A)P(B)    with    P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | not A)P(not A)
posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ evidence.
Think in whole people, not percentages. Draw 10000 people, split them into sick and healthy, then split each group by test result. The answer is just one count divided by another.
Part 6

📧Naive Bayes: a classifier from counting

Naive Bayes uses the theorem of Bayes to classify, for example spam or not spam. It makes one bold (naive) simplification: given the class, every word is independent of the others. That is rarely exactly true, yet the classifier works surprisingly well and trains in one pass of counting.

🧮 Naive Bayes score
score(class) = P(class) × Π P(wordᵢ | class)
Compute it for every class and pick the biggest. Dividing by the total turns scores into probabilities.
🧮 Laplace (add one) smoothing
P(word | class) = count + 1total words in class + vocabulary size
Without the +1, one unseen word would make the whole product 0.
Multiplying many small probabilities underflows to 0 on a computer. Real implementations add logarithms instead: log score = log P(class) + Σ log P(wordᵢ | class). The biggest log score still wins.
