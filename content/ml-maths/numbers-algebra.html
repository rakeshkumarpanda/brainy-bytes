Part 1

🗺️Why machine learning needs maths

A machine learning model is a function with knobs. You give it numbers (the size of a house, the pixels of a photo, the words of a message) and it gives back a number (a price, the word "cat", the chance that a message is spam). Learning means turning the knobs until the answers come out right. Maths is the language that describes the knobs, the answers and the turning.

🏠 A tiny model

price = 3 × area + 50. The numbers 3 and 50 are the knobs. If a house of 100 square metres really sold for 340, the model says 3 × 100 + 50 = 350, so it is 10 too high. Training nudges 3 and 50 until such errors become small.

🧭 Four branches of maths

Algebra writes the model. Linear algebra handles thousands of inputs at once. Calculus tells each knob which way to turn. Probability and statistics measure how sure the model is.

This subject starts at school level and climbs to the level asked in machine learning interviews. Every idea comes with a picture or an animation, an everyday example, a program in four languages and problems with full working.

🔢Kinds of numbers and how computers store them

Numbers come in families. Each family is bigger than the one before and contains it.

FamilySymbolExamplesEveryday use
Natural numbersℕ1, 2, 3, 4Counting apples, number of rows in a dataset
Whole numbers0, 1, 2, 3Number of goals, zero included
Integersℤ−3, −1, 0, 2Temperature below zero, a bank balance
Rational numbersℚ½, 0.75, −4/3Any value you can write as one whole number over another
Irrational numbers√2, π, eDecimals that never end and never repeat
Real numbersℝall of the aboveEvery point on the number line. Model inputs live here

Computers round real numbers

A computer keeps a real number in a fixed number of binary digits, usually 64 bits. Most decimals, such as 0.1, cannot be written exactly in binary, just as ⅓ cannot be written exactly as a decimal (0.3333...). So the computer keeps the nearest value it can, and tiny rounding errors appear.

Never test two decimal results with ==. Test whether they are close: abs(a − b) < 1e-9. NumPy calls this np.isclose, and PyTorch calls it torch.allclose.
Neural networks often train with 32 bit or even 16 bit numbers to save memory and time. Fewer bits means bigger rounding, which is why tricks such as loss scaling and the log sum exp trick (Chapter 12) exist.
🔍 Going deeper: what is really inside a 64 bit number

A computer does not store 0.1 as "zero point one". It stores three pieces, like a number written in scientific notation, but in base 2. This standard is called IEEE 754, and almost every chip in the world follows it.

FormatSignExponent bitsFraction bitsCorrect decimal digitsWhere you meet it
float64 (double)11152about 15 to 16Python float, JavaScript number, Java double
float32 (single)1823about 7the default in PyTorch and on most GPUs
float16 (half)1510about 3mixed precision training, largest value only 65504
bfloat16187about 2 to 3Google TPUs and modern GPUs, same range as float32

Think of the fraction bits as the number of digits on a calculator screen. With 52 bits you get roughly 16 decimal digits, so anything after that is rounded away. That is why 0.1 + 0.2 prints 0.30000000000000004 in Python and JavaScript: 0.1 and 0.2 were each rounded a tiny bit when they were stored, and the tiny errors add up.

🧮 Machine epsilon
ε = 2⁻⁵² ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ for float64
The gap between 1 and the next number a double can hold. Any relative error smaller than this is invisible to the computer.

Three practical rules follow from this.

  1. Never compare decimals with ==. Compare with a tolerance, such as |a − b| < 10⁻⁹.
  2. Adding a tiny number to a huge one can do nothing at all. In float32, 100000000 + 1 is still 100000000, because 7 digits cannot show the 1. Summing millions of small losses in the wrong order can lose information this way.
  3. Fewer bits means a smaller range. float16 cannot hold anything above 65504, so a large logit or a big gradient becomes infinity. Mixed precision training multiplies the loss by a scale factor to keep small gradients from rounding to zero, then divides it back.
A bank never stores rupees and paise as floats. It stores whole paise as integers, because rounding even 0.000001 on millions of payments adds up to real money.
Part 3

➗Order of operations, fractions and percentages

When an expression mixes operations, everyone must work it out in the same order, or two people would get two different answers. The order is Brackets, Orders (powers and roots), Division and Multiplication from left to right, then Addition and Subtraction from left to right.

🧮 BODMAS (also called PEMDAS)
B → O → D·M → A·S
Division and multiplication have equal rank, so go left to right. The same is true for addition and subtraction.
🧮 Percentage
percent = partwhole × 100
Accuracy of 46 correct out of 50 is 46 ÷ 50 × 100 = 92%.
🧮 Percentage change
change = new − oldold × 100
Going from 80% to 92% accuracy is a 15% improvement, not 12%.

Fractions in one minute

A fraction ab means a parts out of b equal parts. To add fractions, make the bottoms (denominators) equal first: 12 + 13 = 36 + 26 = 56. To multiply, multiply tops and bottoms: 23 × 34 = 612 = 12. To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply.

A shop says "20% off, then another 10% off". That is not 30% off. You pay 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72 of the price, which is 28% off. Percentages that follow each other multiply.
Percent means "per hundred". To find 15% of anything, find 10% (move the decimal point once) and add half of it. 15% of 240 is 24 + 12 = 36.
🔍 Going deeper: percent versus percentage points, and chains of changes

Two words get mixed up in news headlines and in model reports all the time.

PhraseWhat it measuresExample: accuracy goes from 80% to 92%
percentage pointsthe plain difference between two percentages92 − 80 = 12 percentage points
percent changethe difference as a share of the starting value12 ÷ 80 × 100 = 15% better
error rate changeoften the most honest view for good modelserrors fell from 20% to 8%, a 60% cut in mistakes

The third row matters a lot in machine learning. Going from 98% to 99% accuracy sounds tiny, only 1 point. But the error rate falls from 2% to 1%, so the model makes half as many mistakes. For a fraud filter checking a million payments a day, that is 10000 fewer wrong answers every day.

Percentage changes that follow each other multiply. A rise of r% multiplies by (1 + r ÷ 100) and a fall of r% multiplies by (1 − r ÷ 100). A share that rises 50% and then falls 50% ends at 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 of where it began, a 25% loss, even though "+50 then −50" looks like it should cancel. Try it below with your own numbers.

Learning rate decay works the same way. Multiplying the learning rate by 0.9 every epoch for 10 epochs leaves 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.35 of it, not 0. Repeated percentage changes are powers, which the next lesson covers.
Part 4

⚖️Variables, expressions and equations

A variable is a box with a name, such as x, that holds a number we do not know yet. An expression such as 3x + 5 is a recipe that uses the box. An equation such as 3x + 5 = 20 says two things are equal, and solving it means finding what is in the box.

The golden rule: an equation is a balanced scale. Whatever you do to one side, do to the other side, and it stays balanced.

Two unknowns need two equations

Two burgers and one juice cost 250. One burger and three juices also cost 250. Write b for a burger and j for a juice: 2b + j = 250 and b + 3j = 250. Multiply the first equation by 3 to get 6b + 3j = 750, subtract the second one, and the juices cancel: 5b = 500, so b = 100 and j = 50.

🧮 Two equations, two unknowns (Cramer rule)
a₁x + b₁y = c₁ and a₂x + b₂y = c₂ give x = c₁b₂ − c₂b₁a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ and y = a₁c₂ − a₂c₁a₁b₂ − a₂b₁
The bottom part a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ is called the determinant. If it is 0, the two lines are parallel and there is no single answer. Chapter 4 meets it again.
Linear regression with one input solves exactly this kind of problem. Its two knobs, the slope and the intercept, come from two equations called the normal equations. With many inputs, the same idea becomes a matrix equation, which Chapter 4 solves.
Think of "undoing" the steps in reverse order, like taking off socks and shoes. The equation 3x + 5 = 20 was built by "times 3, then plus 5". So undo it with "minus 5, then divide by 3".
🔍 Going deeper: rearranging a formula to make any letter the subject

Most of the algebra used in machine learning is not "find x". It is "rearrange this formula so the thing I want is alone on one side". The balance rule is all you need. Here is the z-score formula from Chapter 10, rearranged to get back the original value.

A linear equation has the unknown only to the power 1 and never multiplied by itself, as in 3x + 5 = 20. Its graph is a straight line and it has one answer (or none, or infinitely many). A non linear equation, such as x² = 9 or eˣ = 5, can have several answers or need logs and roots to solve. Machine learning loves linear equations because they can be solved exactly and fast, even with millions of unknowns, which is why so many models start with a linear step.

Undo the operations in the reverse order they were done, like unwrapping a gift: the last layer of paper comes off first.

⚡Powers, roots and scientific notation

A power is repeated multiplication: 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. The small number 5 is the exponent and 2 is the base.

Scientific notation

Very big and very small numbers are written as a number between 1 and 10 times a power of 10. The distance to the Sun is about 1.5 × 10⁸ km. A typical learning rate is 3 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.0003. Programs write this as 3e-4, where e means "times ten to the power".

The exponent of 10 tells you how many places to move the decimal point. Positive moves right (bigger), negative moves left (smaller). 2.5e3 = 2500 and 2.5e-3 = 0.0025.
A virus that doubles every day turns 1 case into 2¹⁰ = 1024 cases in 10 days. Powers grow shockingly fast, which is why "exponential growth" became a household phrase in 2020.
Model sizes are counted with powers of ten: a model with 7 × 10⁹ parameters is a "7B" model. Learning rates such as 1e-3 and 1e-5 and the tiny epsilon 1e-8 that stops a division by zero are all scientific notation.
🔍 Going deeper: why the rules work, and how fast powers really grow

Every power rule comes from counting factors. 2³ × 2⁴ is (2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2 × 2 × 2), seven 2s in a row, so it is 2⁷. Dividing cancels factors, so 2⁵ ÷ 2³ leaves two 2s. Follow the pattern downwards and the strange rules explain themselves:

Power2⁴2³2²2¹2⁰2⁻¹2⁻²
Value168421½¼
How you got there÷ 2÷ 2÷ 2÷ 2÷ 2÷ 2

Each step left divides by 2, so 2⁰ must be 1 and 2⁻¹ must be one half. Nothing magic, just the pattern carried on. A fractional power is a root because (9^½)² = 9^(½ × 2) = 9¹ = 9, so 9^½ is the number whose square is 9.

The animation below races four kinds of growth. A straight line n, a square n², a cube n³ and a doubling 2ⁿ. For small n the cube is ahead. Past n = 10 the doubling leaves everything behind, and by n = 30 it is over a billion. Switch to the log scale to see all four at once: on a log scale, doubling becomes a straight line.

The cost of some algorithms grows like a power of n, which is fine, and some grow like 2ⁿ, which is hopeless for large n. Trying every subset of 50 features means 2⁵⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵ models. That is why exhaustive search is never used for feature selection.
Part 6

ΣSigma and Pi notation: the maths shorthand

Machine learning formulas add up many terms at once. Writing x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ... + xₙ is slow, so mathematicians use the Greek capital letter sigma, Σ, which means "sum".

🧮 Sigma means add them all
n
Σ xᵢ = x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ
i = 1
i is a counter that starts at the number below and stops at the number on top. Read it as "the sum of x i, for i from 1 to n".
🧮 Mean (average)
x̄ = 1n Σ xᵢ
Add everything and divide by how many there are. The bar on top of x is read "x bar".
🧮 Capital pi means multiply them all
Π xᵢ = x₁ × x₂ × ... × xₙ
Probabilities of independent events multiply, so Π appears in likelihoods (Chapter 11).
Σ is a for loop. The counter under the Σ is the loop variable, the numbers under and over it are where the loop starts and stops, and the expression after it is the loop body that gets added.
Gauss as a schoolboy added 1 + 2 + ... + 100 by pairing the first and last numbers: 1 + 100, 2 + 99 and so on make 50 pairs of 101, so the total is 5050. In general Σ i from 1 to n = n(n + 1) ÷ 2.
The mean squared error, the most common loss in regression, is (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)². It is only a sigma, a subtraction and a square. Once you can read Σ, half of every ML paper becomes readable.
🔍 Going deeper: the rules that let you move sums around

Formulas in papers often look scary only because a Σ was moved, split or combined. Three rules cover nearly all of it. Each one is just a fact about adding lists of numbers.

RuleIn symbolsWhy it is true
Split a sumΣ (aᵢ + bᵢ) = Σ aᵢ + Σ bᵢyou can add the numbers in any order
Pull out a constantΣ c·aᵢ = c · Σ aᵢc(a₁ + a₂) = ca₁ + ca₂, the distributive law
Sum of a constantΣ from 1 to n of c = n · cadding c to itself n times
Sum of a logΣ log aᵢ = log Π aᵢlogs turn products into sums (Chapter 2)

A double sum Σᵢ Σⱼ is two loops, one inside the other. Matrix multiplication (Chapter 4) is a double sum in disguise: entry (i, j) of AB is Σₖ aᵢₖbₖⱼ, and doing it for every i and j adds two more loops around it.

Part 7

📏Absolute value, inequalities and distance

The absolute value |x| is the distance of x from zero, so it is never negative: |5| = 5 and |−5| = 5. The distance between two numbers a and b is |a − b|, whichever is bigger.

🧮 Absolute value
|x| = x if x ≥ 0, and −x if x < 0
It removes the minus sign. |3 − 7| = |−4| = 4.
🧮 Mean absolute error
MAE = 1n Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|
y is the true value and ŷ (read "y hat") is the prediction. Average distance between them.

An inequality compares sizes: < less than, ≤ less than or equal, > greater than, ≥ greater than or equal. Solve it like an equation, with one twist: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number flips the sign. From −2x < 6 we get x > −3.

WrittenMeaningOn the number line
|x| < 3x is closer than 3 to zerobetween −3 and 3, written (−3, 3)
|x − 10| ≤ 2x is within 2 of 10from 8 to 10 inclusive, written [8, 12]
x ≥ 0x is not negativezero and everything to its right
|x − 10| ≤ 2 means 8 ≤ x ≤ 12, not 8 ≤ x ≤ 10. Students often forget the right half of the interval.
A weather app that says "25 °C, give or take 2" is saying |true temperature − 25| ≤ 2. A cricket bowler is allowed to bend the elbow by at most 15 degrees: |bend| ≤ 15.
ReLU, the most used activation in neural networks, is max(0, x). The L1 penalty in Lasso regression adds Σ|wᵢ| to the loss. Both are close cousins of the absolute value.
🔍 Going deeper: distance rules and why the kink at zero matters

The absolute value obeys one famous rule, the triangle inequality: |a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|. In words, a detour is never shorter than the direct route. If you walk 3 km east and then 5 km west, you end up only 2 km from home, even though you walked 8 km. The same rule holds for vector lengths in Chapter 3, and it is what makes a distance a "proper" distance.

🧮 Triangle inequality
|a + b| ≤ |a| + |b|    and    |a − c| ≤ |a − b| + |b − c|
Going from a to c directly is never longer than going through b.

The graph of |x| is a V with a sharp corner at 0. The slope is −1 on the left and +1 on the right, so at the corner there is no single slope. Calculus calls this not differentiable at 0. Gradient based training still works, because libraries simply pick a slope of 0 at the corner. But the corner has a useful side effect: L1 regularisation can push a weight to exactly 0 and keep it there, which squared penalties never do (Chapter 12).

Use the widget to play with the most common shape in error bars and tolerances, |x − c| ≤ r. It always means "x is within r of c", which is the interval from c − r to c + r.

A pharmacy label that says "500 mg ± 5%" means |dose − 500| ≤ 25, so any tablet from 475 mg to 525 mg passes the quality check.
