Part 1

🪜Taylor series: curves as polynomials

Computers can only add and multiply. To find e0.5 or sin 0.3, a calculator uses a polynomial that copies the function very closely near one point. That copy is a Taylor polynomial. The recipe is simple: match the value at the point, then the slope, then the bend, then the rate of change of the bend, and so on. Each matched derivative adds one more term.

🧮 Taylor polynomial around 0 (also called a Maclaurin polynomial)
f(x) ≈ f(0) + f′(0) x + f″(0)2! x² + f‴(0)3! x³ + …
n! means 1 × 2 × … × n. Around another point a, replace x by (x − a) and the derivatives at 0 by the derivatives at a.

🔍 Going deeper: why the terms shrink, and when they do not

The n-th term is f⁽ⁿ⁾(0) xⁿ ÷ n!. For eˣ every derivative is 1, so the terms are xⁿ ÷ n!. Factorials grow faster than any power. For x = 2 the terms are 1, 2, 2, 1.33, 0.67, 0.27, 0.09, … They climb a little at first, then fall fast. That is why the series for eˣ, sin x and cos x works for every x, it just needs more terms for larger x.

For ln(1 + x) the terms are xⁿ ÷ n with no factorial to help. When |x| > 1 the powers win and the terms grow, so adding more of them makes the answer worse. The interval where a series works is called its radius of convergence. Try x = 1.5 with ln(1 + x) in the animation and raise n to see the polynomial run away.

The leftover error after the order n polynomial is about the size of the first term you left out. This is Taylor’s remainder: error = f⁽ⁿ⁺¹⁾(c) xⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n + 1)! for some c between 0 and x. It explains why small x needs few terms.

Two Taylor orders run through all of machine learning. Order 1 (the tangent line, Chapter 7) is the reason gradient descent works: f(w + Δ) ≈ f(w) + ∇f · Δ, so stepping against the gradient lowers f. Order 2 adds the Hessian, ½ Δᵀ H Δ, and leads to Newton’s method in the next lesson. The GELU activation in transformers is often computed with a tanh based approximation for speed.
Your phone’s calculator does not store a table of every sine. It shrinks the angle into a small range, then adds up a handful of polynomial terms, which is fast and accurate to the last digit shown.
Part 2

🎯Newton’s method for roots and minimums

To solve f(x) = 0, Newton’s method draws the tangent line at your current guess and jumps to the point where that line crosses zero. The tangent line is the order 1 Taylor polynomial, so each step solves a simpler, straight line version of the problem.

🧮 Newton step for a root
xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f(xₙ)f′(xₙ)
Divide the height by the slope to find how far to slide along the tangent.
🧮 Newton step for a minimum
xₙ₊₁ = xₙ − f′(xₙ)f″(xₙ)
A minimum is a root of the slope f′, so use the same rule on f′. In many dimensions: x ← x − H⁻¹∇f.

That doubling of correct digits is called quadratic convergence. Gradient descent only gains a fixed number of digits per step, so near the answer Newton is much faster. It has costs, though.

  • It needs the second derivative, or in many dimensions the whole Hessian matrix: n² numbers for n weights. With a million weights that is a trillion numbers.
  • It can fail. A flat tangent (f′ = 0) sends the next guess to infinity, and some starting points make it jump back and forth forever. The third example in the animation does exactly that.
  • For minimising, it is attracted to any flat point, including maximums and saddle points, unless the Hessian is positive definite (Chapter 13).
Full Newton is used for small and medium models: logistic regression in statistics packages is fitted by a Newton method called iteratively reweighted least squares. For large models, quasi Newton methods such as L-BFGS build a cheap estimate of the Hessian from recent gradients. Adam’s division by the square root of recent squared gradients is a very rough, diagonal stand in for the curvature.
Newton in one sentence: replace the curve by its tangent (or the bowl by its quadratic), solve that easy problem, repeat.

🧮Matrix calculus: gradients without index pain

When a function takes a whole vector or matrix as input, we want its gradient as a vector or matrix of the same shape. Working through every index by hand is slow and error prone, so people learn a short list of rules, just like the derivative table for single numbers.

The gradient of a scalar function lives in the same space as its input and points uphill. The Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, tells how that gradient changes. For the bowl ½(x − m)ᵀA(x − m) the Hessian is simply A.

🔍 Going deeper: a safe way to derive any rule

When you meet a new expression, write the change in output for a tiny change dx and keep only the first order part. For f(x) = xᵀAx: f(x + dx) = xᵀAx + dxᵀAx + xᵀA dx + (tiny). Both middle terms are numbers, and a number equals its own transpose, so xᵀA dx = dxᵀAᵀx. The change is dxᵀ(A + Aᵀ)x, and whatever multiplies dxᵀ is the gradient.

Then always check the shapes. If x has n entries, the gradient must have n entries too. If the shapes do not match, a transpose is missing. Finally check one example numerically, as the program above does. Libraries do this in their own tests.

Every layer of a neural network is a matrix product followed by an activation. The rules ∂(Wx)/∂x = W and ∂L/∂W = (∂L/∂z) xᵀ are exactly what backpropagation computes for a dense layer. Getting the transposes right is the whole difficulty, and the shape check catches most mistakes.
Part 4

🤖Automatic differentiation: forward and reverse mode

There are three ways a computer can find derivatives. Numerical differences, (f(x + h) − f(x)) ÷ h, are easy but slow and slightly inaccurate. Symbolic differentiation, like a computer algebra system, produces exact formulas that can grow huge. Automatic differentiation (autodiff) is the third way. It applies the chain rule to each small operation as the program runs, and it is exact up to rounding.

Any program is a chain of tiny steps: multiply, add, sin, exp. Write each step down as a node in a graph. Each node knows its own local derivative. Autodiff combines those local pieces with the chain rule, and it can do so in two directions.

Forward modeReverse mode
directioninputs to output, together with the valuesvalues first, then output back to inputs
carriesthe derivative of every node with respect to one inputthe derivative of the output with respect to every node
costone pass per inputone backward pass per output
best whenfew inputs, many outputsmany inputs, one output (a loss)
also calleddual numbers, Jacobian vector productbackpropagation, vector Jacobian product
🔍 Going deeper: why deep learning uses reverse mode

A network has millions of weights (inputs to the loss) and one loss (the output). Forward mode would need one pass per weight, millions of passes. Reverse mode needs one forward pass to compute values and one backward pass, and the backward pass costs only a small multiple of the forward pass. This fact, sometimes called the cheap gradient principle, is why training large networks is possible at all.

The price of reverse mode is memory. The backward pass needs the values from the forward pass, such as x and y for the node x·y, so the whole tape must be kept until the backward pass is done. That is why training uses far more memory than prediction, and why tricks such as gradient checkpointing recompute some values instead of storing them.

When a value is used in two places, its derivative is the sum of the two contributions. Forgetting this is a classic bug in hand written backprop. In PyTorch, calling loss.backward() runs reverse mode over the graph recorded during the forward pass.

PyTorch, TensorFlow and JAX are autodiff engines with a GPU attached. JAX offers both modes directly: jax.grad uses reverse mode, jax.jvp uses forward mode, and the two can be combined to get Hessian vector products cheaply.
Part 5

📉Vanishing and exploding gradients

Backpropagation through a deep network multiplies many factors together, one per layer. Each factor is roughly (slope of the activation) × (weight). If those factors are a little below 1, their product shrinks towards 0 and the early layers stop learning. If they are a little above 1, the product blows up and training crashes with huge updates or NaN values.

🧮 Gradient reaching layer 1 of an L layer chain
∂L/∂h₁ = ∂L/∂h_L × ∏ (σ′(zₖ) wₖ)
One factor per layer. 0.9²⁰ ≈ 0.12, 0.5²⁰ ≈ 0.000001, 1.5²⁰ ≈ 3325.
FixWhat it does
ReLU and its relativesslope exactly 1 for positive inputs, so the activation does not shrink the signal
careful initialisationXavier (variance 1 ÷ n_in) or He (variance 2 ÷ n_in) keeps the size of signals about the same from layer to layer
batch or layer normalisationrescales the values inside the network so they stay in a healthy range
residual connectionsh + F(h) adds a "+1" path, so the gradient can skip layers unchanged
gradient clippingcaps the gradient length to stop explosions, common in recurrent networks
LSTM and GRU gateslet a recurrent network keep a memory path whose factor stays near 1
The residual connection of ResNets and transformers is the most important of these. Because the derivative of h + F(h) is 1 + F′(h), there is always a path with factor 1, and networks with hundreds of layers train well.
Many numbers multiplied together: slightly below 1 vanishes, slightly above 1 explodes. Keep each factor near 1.
Part 6

🥣Convexity and Jensen’s inequality

A function is convex when the straight line (chord) between any two points on its graph never goes below the graph. Think of the shape of a bowl. Convex functions are the friendly ones in optimisation: every local minimum is the global minimum, so gradient descent cannot get trapped.

🧮 Definition
f(t a + (1 − t) b) ≤ t f(a) + (1 − t) f(b) for all t in [0, 1]
The curve at the mixed point is at most the mix of the curve values.
🧮 Quick tests
f″(x) ≥ 0, or in many dimensions a positive semi definite Hessian
x², eˣ, |x| and −ln x are convex. ln x and √x are concave (the chord lies below).

Jensen’s inequality is the same idea with many points and any weights, in particular the probabilities of a random variable.

🧮 Jensen’s inequality
f(E[X]) ≤ E[f(X)] for convex f
The function of the average is at most the average of the function. For concave f the sign flips.
🔍 Going deeper: what Jensen proves in machine learning

KL divergence is never negative. −ln is convex, so E[−ln(q ÷ p)] ≥ −ln E[q ÷ p] = −ln 1 = 0, with the expectation under p. This is why cross entropy is always at least the entropy (Chapter 12).

The ELBO. Variational autoencoders want ln p(x), which is hard. Since ln is concave, ln E[ratio] ≥ E[ln ratio], which gives a lower bound that can be computed and maximised instead.

Which losses are convex. Squared error for linear regression and log loss for logistic regression are convex in the weights, so their training always reaches the best answer. A neural network with a hidden layer is not convex in its weights: swapping two hidden units gives the same function, so there are many equal minimums. In practice, large networks still train well, and their many minimums tend to be about equally good.

Averaging speeds is a Jensen trap. Drive 60 km at 30 km/h and 60 km at 90 km/h. The average of the speeds is 60 km/h, but the trip takes 2 + 0.67 = 2.67 hours for 120 km, an average of 45 km/h. Time is a convex function of speed, so the true average is lower.
Part 7

🧯Numerical stability: when maths meets floating point

Floating point numbers (Chapter 1) have about 16 significant digits in float64 and about 7 in float32. Formulas that are perfect on paper can lose all their accuracy on a computer. Three situations come up again and again in machine learning.

  1. Overflow: e¹⁰⁰⁰ is bigger than the largest float64 (about 1.8 × 10³⁰⁸), so it becomes infinity, and infinity ÷ infinity is NaN.
  2. Catastrophic cancellation: subtracting two nearly equal numbers wipes out the leading digits and leaves mostly rounding noise.
  3. Underflow: multiplying many small probabilities gives a number too small to store, which becomes exactly 0. Working with logs and adding them avoids it.
🧮 The log-sum-exp trick
ln Σ ezᵢ = m + ln Σ ezᵢ − m with m = max zᵢ
Every exponent is now 0 or below, so nothing overflows, and the biggest term is exactly e⁰ = 1, so nothing underflows to a sum of 0.

Finite differences show both errors at once. The step h in (f(x + h) − f(x)) ÷ h must be small to follow the curve, which is the truncation error, about h. But a tiny h makes f(x + h) − f(x) a cancellation, which is the rounding error, about ε ÷ h, where ε ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. The total is smallest in between.

🧮 Forward difference
error ≈ h + ε ÷ h, best h ≈ √ε ≈ 10⁻⁸
Gives about 8 correct digits at best.
🧮 Central difference
(f(x + h) − f(x − h)) ÷ 2h, error ≈ h² + ε ÷ h, best h ≈ 10⁻⁵
Gives about 10 to 11 correct digits.
Every deep learning library computes softmax and cross entropy together with the log-sum-exp trick, which is why you should pass raw logits to CrossEntropyLoss rather than probabilities. Gradient checking with central differences is the standard way to test a hand written backward pass. Mixed precision training in float16 multiplies the loss by a large "loss scale" so that small gradients do not underflow to zero.
Never compute e^big directly. Subtract the max first. Never multiply many probabilities. Add their logs.
