Part 1

🎯Random variables, PMF, PDF and CDF

A random variable is a number that comes out of a chance event: the face of a die, the number of heads in ten flips, or the time until the next bus. We write it with a capital letter such as X, and a value it can take with a small letter such as x.

A discrete random variable takes separate values you can list (0, 1, 2, 3 and so on). A continuous one can take any value in a range, such as 1.73 m or 1.7312 m. The two kinds are described in slightly different ways.

🧮 PMF (discrete)
p(x) = P(X = x)   with   Σ p(x) = 1
Probability mass function: the chance of each exact value. All the bars add up to 1.
🧮 PDF (continuous)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx   with total area 1
Probability density function: probability is the AREA under the curve between a and b.
🧮 CDF (both)
F(x) = P(X ≤ x)
Cumulative distribution function: it climbs from 0 on the far left to 1 on the far right and never goes down.

For a continuous variable, P(X = exactly 1.5) = 0, because a single point has no width and so no area. Only ranges have probability. That is also why a PDF can be taller than 1: its height is a density, not a probability.
The number of goals in a football match is discrete. The time until the next bus arrives is continuous.
🔍 Going deeper: build a PMF and a CDF from two dice

Roll two dice and add them. The total X is a discrete random variable with values 2 to 12. There are 36 equally likely pairs, so P(X = x) is just "how many pairs give x" divided by 36. Seven is the most likely total because six pairs make it: (1, 6), (2, 5), (3, 4), (4, 3), (5, 2) and (6, 1).

The CDF answers "at most" questions in one look. P(X ≤ 4) is the height of the CDF staircase at 4, which is (1 + 2 + 3) ÷ 36 = 6 ÷ 36. Questions about a range use a difference: P(5 ≤ X ≤ 9) = F(9) − F(4).

🧮 Quantile (the inverse of the CDF)
the q quantile is the smallest x with F(x) ≥ q
The median is the 0.5 quantile. The 0.95 quantile is the value that 95% of results stay at or below. Weather services and delivery apps quote these: "arrives within 30 minutes 95% of the time".
A classifier that outputs probabilities is giving a PMF over the classes. A regression model that outputs a mean and a spread is giving a PDF. Models that predict whole distributions, not just single numbers, are called probabilistic models.
Part 2

⚖️Expected value and variance

The expected value E[X] (also called the mean μ) is the long run average of X if you could repeat the experiment forever. The variance measures how far values usually land from that mean, and the standard deviation σ is its square root, which brings the units back to normal.

🧮 Expected value
E[X] = μ = Σ x · p(x)
Each value times its chance, all added up.
🧮 Variance
Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ²
The average squared distance from the mean. The second form is quicker by hand.
🧮 Standard deviation
σ = √Var(X)
Same units as X, so it is easier to picture.
🧮 Shifting and scaling
E[aX + b] = a E[X] + b    Var(aX + b) = a² Var(X)
Adding b moves the mean but not the spread. Multiplying by a stretches the spread by a.
🧮 Adding variables
E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]    Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)
The mean rule is always true. The variance rule needs X and Y to be independent.
The expected value does not have to be a possible result. Nobody ever rolls 3.5, yet 3.5 is where the average of many rolls ends up.
Insurance companies and casinos run on expected value. Each single policy or bet is a gamble, but over thousands of them the average is very predictable.
🔍 Going deeper: the mean is a balance point, and a worked game

Picture the PMF bars standing on a see saw. The expected value is exactly the point where the see saw balances. Pull probability towards the right and the balance point moves right. Spread the bars out and the variance grows, even if the balance point stays put. Try it below.

The expected value is also the best single guess if you are scored by squared error. That is why regression models trained with mean squared error learn to predict the conditional mean, E[Y | inputs].

Part 3

🪙Bernoulli and binomial: counting successes

A Bernoulli trial has two results: success (1) with chance p or failure (0) with chance 1 − p. A coin toss, a free throw and "did the user click?" are all Bernoulli trials. Repeat n independent Bernoulli trials and count the successes, and you get a binomial random variable.

🧮 Bernoulli(p)
P(X = 1) = p,   P(X = 0) = 1 − p
Mean p, variance p(1 − p).
🧮 Binomial(n, p)
P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ
C(n, k) counts the ways to place k successes among n tries. pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ is the chance of any one of those ways.
🧮 Binomial mean and spread
μ = n p    σ² = n p (1 − p)
Ten fair flips: mean 5 heads, variance 2.5, SD about 1.58.
A classifier that is right 90% of the time is tested on 200 new cases. The number correct is binomial with mean 200 × 0.9 = 180 and SD √(200 × 0.9 × 0.1) = √18 ≈ 4.24. So a score between about 172 and 188 is ordinary luck, not a better or worse model.
Part 4

📞Poisson, uniform and exponential

The Poisson distribution counts how many events happen in a fixed stretch of time or space when they arrive at random at an average rate λ (lambda). Calls to a help desk per hour, typos per page and goals per match are classic examples.

🧮 Poisson(λ)
P(X = k) = e−λ · λᵏk!
The mean and the variance are both λ.

Poisson is what the binomial becomes when n is huge and p is tiny, with n p = λ. Many people (huge n) each with a tiny chance of calling in this hour (tiny p) gives a Poisson number of calls.
Models that predict counts, such as the number of bike rentals per hour, often use Poisson regression, which assumes the count follows a Poisson distribution whose λ depends on the inputs.
🔍 Going deeper: play with λ, and see the binomial turn into Poisson

Move λ below to see the Poisson shape. Small λ piles up near 0 and is lopsided. Larger λ moves the peak to about λ and the shape becomes a bell, because a Poisson count with a big λ is like adding many small independent counts, and the central limit theorem takes over.

The exponential distribution is the Poisson’s partner. If events arrive at rate λ, the waiting time until the next one is exponential with mean 1 ÷ λ. It has a strange "memoryless" property: if you have already waited 10 minutes for a random event, the chance of waiting 5 more is the same as if you had just arrived. Buses on a timetable are not memoryless, but radioactive decays and many website requests are close to it.

Part 5

🔔The normal distribution and z-scores

The normal (or Gaussian) distribution is the famous bell curve. It is set by two numbers: the mean μ, which places the centre, and the standard deviation σ, which sets the width. Heights, measurement errors and exam scores are often close to normal.

🧮 Normal PDF
f(x) = 1σ √(2π) · e−(x − μ)² ÷ (2σ²)
The curve is symmetric about μ and its peak height is 1 ÷ (σ√(2π)).
🧮 z-score
z = x − μσ
How many standard deviations x is from the mean. Any normal becomes the standard normal N(0, 1) this way.

Remember 68, 95, 99.7. Within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean you find about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of a normal population.
Standardising a feature means turning every value into its z-score. Then every feature has mean 0 and SD 1, so no feature dominates just because of its units, and gradient descent runs much more smoothly. Neural network weights are also often started with small random normal values.
Part 6

📊The central limit theorem

The central limit theorem (CLT) explains why the bell curve is everywhere. Take many independent random values from almost any distribution (it only needs a finite variance), and their average is close to normally distributed. The original shape can be flat, lopsided or just 0 and 1. The average still ends up bell shaped.

🧮 Central limit theorem
X̄ = X₁ + X₂ + … + Xₙn   is roughly   N(μ, σ² ÷ n)
The average has the same centre μ as one value, but a narrower spread.
🧮 Standard error
SE = σ√n
The SD of an average of n values. It shrinks as the sample grows.
The √n rule: 4 times more data halves the error, and 100 times more data cuts it to a tenth. Precision gets expensive.
When a model scores 85% accuracy on 400 test cases, the CLT gives the error bar: SE = √(0.85 × 0.15 ÷ 400) ≈ 0.018, so the true accuracy is probably within about 85% ± 3.6%. Mini batch gradients in training are averages too, so they are noisy estimates of the full gradient, and bigger batches make that noise smaller.
Part 7

🔗Covariance, correlation and distributions in ML

So far each variable lived alone. Covariance asks whether two variables move together. If X is above its mean when Y is above its mean, the covariance is positive. Correlation rescales covariance to a number between −1 and 1 so it is easy to read.

🧮 Sample covariance
Cov(X, Y) = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)n − 1
Positive: they rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
🧮 Correlation
r = Cov(X, Y)sx · sy
Always between −1 and 1. r = 1 is a perfect rising straight line.
Correlation is not causation. Ice cream sales and sunburn are correlated because both rise in hot weather. Also, r only measures straight line relationships: for x = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 and y = x², r is exactly 0 even though y depends completely on x.

With several features, all the variances and covariances are collected into one square table, the covariance matrix Σ. Entry (i, j) is Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ). The diagonal holds the variances, and the table is symmetric because Cov(X, Y) = Cov(Y, X).

PCA (Chapter 5) is the eigen decomposition of exactly this matrix. The multivariate normal distribution of Chapter 15 uses Σ to describe tilted, stretched bell shaped clouds of data.
