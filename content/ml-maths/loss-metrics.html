Part 1

🔥Information and entropy

Some news tells you a lot, some tells you almost nothing. "The sun rose today" is no surprise. "It snowed in the Sahara" is a big surprise. Information theory measures surprise with a number, and entropy is the average surprise of a random event.

🧮 Surprise (information) of an outcome
I(x) = log₂ 1p(x) = −log₂ p(x)
Measured in bits. A fair coin flip carries 1 bit. A 1 in 8 event carries 3 bits.
🧮 Entropy
H(p) = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x)
The average surprise. It is 0 when one outcome is certain and largest when every outcome is equally likely.
Entropy counts the yes or no questions you need on average. A fair die needs log₂ 6 ≈ 2.585. Finding one person among a million needs about 20.
Decision trees choose each split to reduce entropy as much as possible. That drop is called information gain.
🔍 Going deeper: information gain and mutual information, worked

A decision tree asks one question at a time, such as "is income above 50000?". A good question leaves the groups below it purer, with lower entropy. The drop in entropy is the information gain.

Information gain is a special case of a more general idea, mutual information. It measures how much knowing one variable reduces your uncertainty about another.

🧮 Mutual information
I(X, Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) = Σ p(x, y) log [p(x, y) ÷ (p(x) p(y))]
It is 0 exactly when X and Y are independent, and it catches curved and non numeric relationships that correlation misses.
Feature selection often ranks features by their mutual information with the label (scikit learn has mutual_info_classif for this). Unlike correlation, it can spot a feature whose effect is U shaped, and it works for categories such as city or device type.
Part 2

🎯Cross entropy and KL divergence

Suppose reality follows p, but your model believes q. You will be surprised more often than necessary. Cross entropy measures your average surprise, and the extra over the unavoidable entropy is the KL divergence.

🧮 Cross entropy
H(p, q) = −Σ p(x) log q(x)
Reality p, belief q. It is smallest when q = p.
🧮 KL divergence
KL(p ‖ q) = Σ p(x) log p(x)q(x) = H(p, q) − H(p)
Always ≥ 0, and 0 only when q = p. Not symmetric: KL(p ‖ q) ≠ KL(q ‖ p).
In classification the true label is one-hot (p = 1 for the right class, 0 for the rest), so H(p) = 0 and cross entropy becomes just −log q(right class). Minimising cross entropy and minimising KL divergence are then the same thing. KL also appears in variational autoencoders and in keeping a fine-tuned language model close to its original version.
🔍 Going deeper: cross entropy and KL with real numbers

Move the model’s guess q below and watch the three bars. Entropy H(p) never moves, because it belongs to reality. Cross entropy is always at least as big, and the gap between them is the KL divergence. Press Play to let q walk towards p.

Why KL is not symmetric. KL(p ‖ q) punishes q heavily for giving tiny probability to something p says happens often. KL(q ‖ p) punishes the opposite. Generative models choose between them on purpose: one direction makes a model cover every possible output, the other makes it focus on the most common ones.

Part 3

🌡️Softmax, temperature and log-sum-exp

A classifier first produces raw scores called logits, which can be any real numbers. Softmax turns them into probabilities: all positive and adding to 1, with the biggest logit getting the biggest share.

🧮 Softmax
softmax(z)ᵢ = ezᵢ ÷ TΣⱼ ezⱼ ÷ T
T is the temperature. T = 1 is normal, small T sharpens the choice, large T flattens it.
🧮 Stable computation
softmax(z) = softmax(z − max z)
Subtracting the same number from every logit changes nothing, but keeps ez from overflowing.
🧮 Log-sum-exp
log Σ ezⱼ = m + log Σ ezⱼ − m,   m = max z
Cross entropy loss = log-sum-exp(z) − zcorrect.
Language models pick the next word by sampling from a softmax. The "temperature" setting in chatbots is exactly the T in this formula: low T gives safe, repeatable text, high T gives more varied text.
Part 4

📉Loss functions for regression

A loss function turns "how wrong was the model?" into one number that training then makes smaller. The choice of loss decides what kind of mistakes the model cares most about.

🧮 Mean squared error
MSE = 1n Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
Squares make big misses count a lot. Smooth and easy to differentiate. The best constant prediction is the mean.
🧮 Mean absolute error
MAE = 1n Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|
Every unit of error counts the same, so outliers matter less. The best constant prediction is the median.
🧮 Huber loss
½ e² when |e| ≤ δ,   δ(|e| − ½δ) otherwise
Squared for small errors, straight line for big ones: the best of both.
LossOutliersGradientTypical use
MSEvery sensitivesmooth, shrinks near 0default for regression
MAErobustconstant size, kink at 0when outliers are common
Huberrobustsmootha safe middle ground
MSE chases the mean, MAE chases the median. Pick the loss whose "best answer" matches what you want to predict.
🔍 Going deeper: compare the losses and their slopes side by side

The shape of a loss decides how hard the model is pushed by each error. Look at the slope (the gradient) as well as the loss itself. MSE pushes in proportion to the error, so one huge outlier gets a huge push. MAE pushes with the same strength whatever the size of the error. Huber behaves like MSE for small errors and like MAE for big ones, with δ choosing where it switches.

Another useful loss is the quantile (pinball) loss. It charges q × e for under predictions and (1 − q) × |e| for over predictions. With q = 0.9, under predicting costs nine times more, so the model learns the 90th percentile instead of the middle. Delivery apps and energy forecasters use it to predict "safe" upper estimates.

Part 5

🪵Log loss for classification

For a yes or no classifier that outputs a probability p, the standard loss is log loss, also called binary cross entropy. It is the cross entropy from earlier with only two classes.

🧮 Binary cross entropy
L = −[ y log p + (1 − y) log(1 − p) ]
If y = 1 only −log p counts. If y = 0 only −log(1 − p) counts.
True label yPredicted pLoss
10.9 (confident and right)0.105
10.6 (unsure but right)0.511
10.1 (confident and wrong)2.303
10.01 (very confident and wrong)4.605
Why not use MSE for classification? With a sigmoid output, MSE gives tiny gradients exactly when the model is confidently wrong, so learning stalls. Log loss punishes confident mistakes hard and keeps the gradient p − y large.
Minimising log loss is maximum likelihood for a Bernoulli model (Chapter 11). Clipping p to a range such as [10⁻⁷, 1 − 10⁻⁷] stops log 0 from turning into infinity.
🔍 Going deeper: the loss curve and the famous p − y gradient

Slide the predicted probability p below. When the true label is 1, the loss −ln p is gentle near p = 1 and shoots up towards infinity as p heads to 0. Being confidently wrong is punished far more than being unsure.

That cancellation is the real reason log loss and sigmoid are always used together. The gradient is just "prediction minus truth", which is large when the model is badly wrong and never gets stuck in the flat ends of the sigmoid. The same thing happens for softmax with cross entropy: the gradient for each class is pᵢ − yᵢ.

Part 6

🧲Regularisation: L2 and L1

A flexible model can fit noise (overfitting, Chapter 11). Regularisation adds a penalty for large weights to the loss, so the model only uses big weights when the data really demands them.

🧮 L2 (ridge, weight decay)
loss + λ Σ wⱼ²
Shrinks all weights smoothly toward 0. Rarely makes any weight exactly 0.
🧮 L1 (lasso)
loss + λ Σ |wⱼ|
Pushes some weights to exactly 0, so it also selects features.
🧮 One weight ridge solution
w = Σ xᵢ yᵢΣ xᵢ² + λ
For y ≈ w x with no intercept. Bigger λ makes w smaller.
λ is a dial between "trust the data" (λ = 0) and "keep it simple" (large λ). Pick it with a validation set, never with the test set.
Weight decay in neural network optimisers such as AdamW is L2 regularisation. Dropout and early stopping are other ways to regularise.
🔍 Going deeper: why L1 gives exact zeros: the picture

Regularisation can be seen as a budget on the weights. L2 says the weights must stay inside a circle, w₁² + w₂² ≤ t². L1 says they must stay inside a diamond, |w₁| + |w₂| ≤ t. The best allowed point is where the loss contours (the ellipses) first touch the shape. A diamond has sharp corners sitting exactly on the axes, and the ellipses very often touch at a corner, where one weight is exactly 0. A circle has no corners, so that almost never happens.

MethodPenaltyEffect
Ridge (L2)λ Σ wⱼ²shrinks all weights smoothly, keeps them all
Lasso (L1)λ Σ |wⱼ|sets some weights exactly to 0, choosing features
Elastic netλ₁ Σ |wⱼ| + λ₂ Σ wⱼ²a mix: sparse like L1 but stable when features are correlated
There is also a probability reading. L2 regularisation is the same as assuming, before seeing data, that each weight comes from a normal distribution centred on 0. L1 is the same as assuming a Laplace distribution, which has a sharp peak at 0. Chapter 15 shows this link through MAP estimation.

📊Classification metrics, ROC and AUC

Accuracy alone can fool you. If 99% of card payments are honest, a model that never flags fraud is 99% accurate and completely useless. The confusion matrix shows what really happened.

Really positiveReally negative
Predicted positiveTP (true positive)FP (false positive)
Predicted negativeFN (false negative)TN (true negative)
🧮 Precision
TPTP + FP
Of everything flagged, how much was right? Important when false alarms are costly.
🧮 Recall (sensitivity, TPR)
TPTP + FN
Of all real positives, how many did we catch? Important when misses are costly.
🧮 F1 score
F1 = 2 × precision × recallprecision + recall
The harmonic mean, high only when both are high.
🧮 ROC curve and AUC
TPR = TP ÷ (TP + FN) against FPR = FP ÷ (FP + TN) for every threshold
AUC is the area under that curve: the chance that a random positive gets a higher score than a random negative. 0.5 is guessing, 1 is perfect.
Precision asks "when I shout fraud, am I right?" Recall asks "did I catch all the fraud?" A doctor screening for cancer wants high recall. A spam filter that must not lose real email wants high precision.
🔍 Going deeper: choosing the threshold, PR curves and F beta

A classifier gives a score, and you choose the threshold that turns scores into yes or no. Raising the threshold flags fewer cases: precision usually goes up and recall goes down. There is no single right threshold. It depends on what each kind of mistake costs.

🧮 F beta score
Fβ = (1 + β²) × precision × recallβ² × precision + recall
β = 1 gives F1. β = 2 counts recall as more important (screening for disease). β = 0.5 counts precision as more important (spam filtering).

When positives are rare (fraud, rare diseases), a precision recall curve is often more honest than the ROC curve. ROC uses the false positive rate, whose bottom FP + TN is huge when negatives are plentiful, so even many false alarms look like a small rate. Precision shows those false alarms directly.
