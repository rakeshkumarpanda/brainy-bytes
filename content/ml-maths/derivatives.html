Part 1

🚗Rate of change and slope

A derivative answers one question: how fast is something changing right now? A car that travels 120 km in 2 hours has an average speed of 60 km/h, but the speedometer shows the speed at this exact moment. The derivative is the speedometer.

🧮 Average rate of change (slope of a secant)
f(b) − f(a)b − a
Rise over run between two points on the curve.
🧮 Instant rate of change (slope of the tangent)
f′(a) = limh→0 f(a + h) − f(a)h
Slide the second point closer and closer to the first. The slope settles on one number.

Your phone map shows "12 minutes to arrive". It divides the distance left by your current rate of change of position. When traffic slows you, the rate drops and the estimate rises.
Part 2

🔬Limits and the derivative from first principles

A limit says what value a formula approaches as the input gets close to some number, even if you cannot plug that number in. You cannot set h = 0 in (f(a + h) − f(a)) ÷ h because that divides by zero, but you can ask what happens as h shrinks.

The central difference (f(x + h) − f(x − h)) ÷ 2h looks both ways and is far more accurate than the one sided version. Machine learning engineers use it for gradient checking: comparing their hand written derivative with this numerical one to catch bugs.
Do not make h too tiny on a computer. With h = 10⁻¹⁵ the subtraction f(x + h) − f(x) loses almost all its digits to rounding. Something like h = 10⁻⁵ is a safe middle ground.
A derivative is just "rise over run" for a run so short you cannot see it.
🔍 Going deeper: one sided limits, gaps and the shrinking h experiment

A limit asks where a formula is heading, not what it equals at the point itself. Sometimes the formula cannot even be worked out at that point, and the limit still exists.

A limit only exists if you get the same answer coming from the left and from the right. The step function "0 for negatives, 1 for x ≥ 0" heads to 0 from the left and 1 from the right, so it has no limit at 0. A function with no jumps and no holes is called continuous, and continuous functions are the ones whose derivatives we can usually trust.

Now the computer experiment. The table below shrinks h by 10 each step and computes the derivative both ways, using real 64 bit numbers in your browser. At first the error falls, as calculus promises. Then, for very tiny h, rounding takes over (Chapter 1) and the error climbs again.

This is exactly why gradient checking uses the central difference with h around 10⁻⁵. It is the sweet spot where the maths error is small and rounding has not yet wrecked the subtraction.
Part 3

📏The rules of differentiation

Nobody works from first principles every time. A small set of rules covers almost every function you will meet in machine learning.

🧮 Power rule
d/dx xⁿ = n xⁿ⁻¹
Bring the power down, then lower it by one. x⁵ becomes 5x⁴.
🧮 Constant and sum rules
(c·f)′ = c·f′    (f + g)′ = f′ + g′
Constants ride along. Differentiate each term on its own.
🧮 Product rule
(f·g)′ = f′g + fg′
First times slope of second, plus second times slope of first.
🧮 Quotient rule
(fg)′ = f′g − fg′g²
"Low d high minus high d low, over low squared".

Part 4

⛓️The chain rule

Most functions in machine learning are functions inside functions. A neuron computes z = wx + b, then a = σ(z), then a loss L(a). The chain rule says how a small change at the start ripples through every link.

🧮 Chain rule
dydx = dydu × dudx
If y depends on u and u depends on x, multiply the slopes along the chain. Outside slope times inside slope.
Backpropagation is the chain rule applied over and over, from the loss back to every weight. Chapter 7 walks through it number by number. When many small slopes like 0.25 are multiplied along a deep chain, the product becomes tiny: the vanishing gradient problem. ReLU, whose slope is 1 for positive inputs, fixed much of this.
Peel an onion: differentiate the outside layer, keep the inside untouched, then multiply by the slope of the inside.
🔍 Going deeper: the chain rule with real numbers, and why deep chains fade

The chain rule is easiest to trust once you have checked it with numbers. Take y = (3x + 1)³ at x = 1.

Now chain many links. If each link has slope 0.25 (the largest a sigmoid can give), ten links multiply to 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 0.00000095. A weight at the start of the chain gets almost no signal, so it barely learns. This is the vanishing gradient problem. If each link has slope 1.5 instead, ten links give 1.5¹⁰ ≈ 57.7, and the gradient explodes. Chapter 14 looks at the fixes.

Part 5

⛰️Peaks, valleys and the second derivative

Where a smooth curve reaches a peak or the bottom of a valley, it is flat for a moment, so its slope is 0. Setting f′(x) = 0 finds these critical points. The second derivative f″(x), the slope of the slope, tells which kind it is.

SignWhat the curve doesPicture
f′(x) > 0rising↗
f′(x) < 0falling↘
f′(x) = 0 and f″(x) > 0local minimum, bends upward like a cup∪
f′(x) = 0 and f″(x) < 0local maximum, bends downward like a cap∩
f″(x) changes signinflection point, the bend flips∫
🧮 Convex function
f″(x) ≥ 0 everywhere
The curve is a single bowl, so any valley you find is THE lowest point. Mean squared error for linear regression is convex, which is why it always trains to the same answer.
A company finds that profit P(price) rises and then falls as the price goes up. Setting P′ = 0 finds the best price, and P″ < 0 there confirms it is a peak, not a dip.
Part 6

📐Integrals: adding up tiny pieces

An integral is the opposite job of a derivative. A derivative turns distance into speed. An integral turns speed back into distance, by adding up "speed × tiny time" over and over. In pictures, it is the area under a curve.

🧮 Definite integral as a sum of strips
∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx ≈ Σ f(xᵢ) Δx
Cut [a, b] into thin strips of width Δx and add up height × width. More strips, better answer.
🧮 Fundamental theorem of calculus
∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)
where F′ = f. Find a function whose slope is f, then subtract its values at the ends.
🧮 Power rule backwards
∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹n + 1 + C
Raise the power by one, divide by the new power (n ≠ −1).
Probabilities of continuous values are areas: the chance that a normal value lands between −1 and 1 is the area under the bell curve there, about 68%. Expected values (Chapter 10) are integrals, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC, Chapter 12) is an integral too.
🔍 Going deeper: a full worked integral, negative areas and averages

Two details trip people up. First, area below the x axis counts as negative. ∫ from −1 to 1 of x dx is 0, because the triangle below cancels the triangle above. Second, dividing an integral by the width of the interval gives the average value of the function there.

🧮 Average value of f on [a, b]
1b − a ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx
The average of x² from 0 to 2 is (8 ÷ 3) ÷ 2 = 4 ÷ 3 ≈ 1.333.
Expected value is an average, so for a continuous random variable it is an integral: E[X] = ∫ x f(x) dx. Most of these integrals are too hard to do by hand, so machine learning estimates them by averaging random samples instead (Monte Carlo, Chapter 15).
Part 7

🤖Why derivatives make machines learn

Training a model means making a loss number small. The derivative of the loss with respect to a weight says which way to nudge that weight and how strongly. If ∂L/∂w is positive, increasing w raises the loss, so decrease it. If it is negative, increase w.
