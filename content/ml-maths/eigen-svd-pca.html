Part 1

🧭Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix keeps

Chapter 4 showed that a matrix moves every arrow in the plane. Most arrows get turned to a new direction. A few special arrows only get longer, shorter or flipped, and stay on their own line. These are the eigenvectors of the matrix, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue, written λ (the Greek letter lambda).

🧮 The eigen equation
A v = λ v    with v ≠ 0
v is the eigenvector (the direction that survives). λ is the eigenvalue (how much it is stretched). λ = 2 doubles v, λ = 0.5 halves it, λ = −1 flips it.

🔍 How to check one

Multiply A by v. If the answer is a plain multiple of v, v is an eigenvector and the multiple is λ. With A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] and v = [1, 1], Av = [3, 3] = 3 × [1, 1], so λ = 3.

🚫 Not every matrix has real ones

A 90° rotation turns every arrow, so no direction is kept. Its eigenvalues are complex numbers, and you will see "no real eigenvectors" in the animation.

Spin a globe. Every point moves except the points on the axis through the poles. That axis is the eigenvector of the spin, with eigenvalue 1, because those points stay exactly where they are.
"Eigen" is German for "own". An eigenvector is a direction the matrix owns, the one it does not turn.
Part 2

🔎Finding eigenvalues by hand

Move everything to one side: Av − λv = 0, which is (A − λI)v = 0. A non zero v can only solve this when the matrix A − λI squashes space flat, which means its determinant is zero. That gives an ordinary equation in λ, called the characteristic equation.

🧮 Characteristic equation
det(A − λI) = 0
Solve for λ. A 2 × 2 matrix gives a quadratic, a 3 × 3 gives a cubic.
🧮 Shortcut for 2 × 2
λ² − (trace) λ + det = 0
trace = a + d, det = ad − bc. Then λ = trace ± √(trace² − 4 det)2
Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector. [1, 1], [2, 2] and [−5, −5] all point along the same line. Libraries usually scale it to length 1, so NumPy gives [0.7071, 0.7071] for [1, 1].
Triangular or diagonal matrix? The eigenvalues are simply the numbers on the diagonal. No equation needed.
🔍 Going deeper: from eigenvalues to eigenvectors, fully worked

Finding λ is only half the job. For each λ you then find the direction v by solving (A − λI)v = 0. Here is a full example with no steps skipped.

The characteristic equation is a quadratic in λ, so it is a parabola. Its roots, where it crosses zero, are the eigenvalues. Change the matrix below and watch the roots move. When the parabola floats above the axis there are no real roots: the matrix turns every direction, as a rotation does.

The two rows of A − λI are always multiples of each other (that is what det = 0 means). So you only ever need to read one row to find v.
Part 3

🧩Rules that save time

Eigenvalues come with a handful of rules that let you check your work in seconds and that explain why they are so useful.

RuleIn symbolsExample with [[2, 1], [1, 2]]
Sum of eigenvalues = traceλ₁ + λ₂ + … = a₁₁ + a₂₂ + …3 + 1 = 4 = 2 + 2
Product of eigenvalues = determinantλ₁ × λ₂ × … = det A3 × 1 = 3 = det A
PowersAⁿ v = λⁿ vA¹⁰ [1, 1] = 3¹⁰ [1, 1] = 59049 [1, 1]
InverseA⁻¹ v = (1/λ) veigenvalues of A⁻¹ are 1/3 and 1
Symmetric matrixA = Aᵀ gives real λ and perpendicular v[1, 1] · [1, −1] = 0
🧮 Diagonalisation (eigendecomposition)
A = P D P⁻¹    and    Aⁿ = P Dⁿ P⁻¹
P has the eigenvectors as its columns. D is diagonal with the eigenvalues. For a symmetric matrix P is orthogonal, so P⁻¹ = Pᵀ.

Why this matters: raising a matrix to the 100th power directly needs 99 matrix multiplications. With A = PDP⁻¹ you just raise each eigenvalue to the 100th power. It also tells the long run story. In a Markov chain the eigenvalue 1 belongs to the steady state, and every smaller eigenvalue fades away as its power shrinks to zero.

Training deep networks: the eigenvalues of the weight matrices decide whether signals blow up (|λ| > 1) or vanish (|λ| < 1) as they pass through many layers. This is the exploding and vanishing gradient problem in recurrent networks.
Two quick checks after finding eigenvalues: do they add up to the trace, and multiply to the determinant? If not, there is an arithmetic slip.
Part 4

🔁Power iteration and PageRank

Real matrices have thousands or millions of rows, so nobody solves a characteristic equation. A simple and famous method is power iteration: start with any vector, multiply by A, scale it back to length 1, and repeat. The direction with the biggest |λ| grows fastest, so the vector swings round to the top eigenvector.

🧮 Power iteration
v ← A v / ‖A v‖     λ ≈ vᵀ A v
The second formula (the Rayleigh quotient) reads off the eigenvalue once v has settled.

🌐 PageRank in one paragraph

The first Google ranking method treated the web as a Markov chain: a surfer clicks a random link on each page. The matrix of click chances has a top eigenvalue of 1, and its eigenvector gives every page a score, the share of time the surfer spends there. Power iteration on billions of pages found it.

Power iteration and its relatives (Lanczos, randomised SVD) are how libraries find the top few eigenvectors of huge matrices for PCA, spectral clustering and graph neural networks without computing all of them.
🔍 Going deeper: watch power iteration swing round

Here is why power iteration works. Write the starting vector as a mix of eigenvectors: x = c₁v₁ + c₂v₂. Multiplying by A k times gives c₁λ₁ᵏv₁ + c₂λ₂ᵏv₂. If |λ₁| is bigger than |λ₂|, the first term grows faster and soon swamps the second. Scaling back to length 1 each time only keeps the numbers from overflowing.

🧮 Speed of power iteration
error after k steps ≈ |λ₂ ÷ λ₁|ᵏ
With λ₁ = 5 and λ₂ = 2 the error shrinks by 0.4 each step. With λ₁ = 1 and λ₂ = 0.99 it shrinks by only 1% each step, which is very slow.

Press Play below. The orange arrow is the current guess. Each step multiplies by A (dashed) and then rescales. The guess swings round onto the blue eigenvector line, and the Rayleigh estimate of λ closes in on the true value.

Repeatedly asking "which pages do the important pages link to?" is power iteration. After a few rounds the answer stops changing, and that steady answer is the PageRank score.
Part 5

🧱Singular value decomposition (SVD)

Eigenvectors need a square matrix, and even then they might not exist. The SVD works for every matrix of any shape. It says each matrix is three simple moves in a row: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and another rotation.

🧮 Singular value decomposition
A = U Σ Vᵀ
For an m × n matrix: U is m × m and orthogonal, Σ is m × n with the singular values σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0 on its diagonal, and V is n × n and orthogonal.
🧮 Where the parts come from
σᵢ = √(eigenvalues of AᵀA)
The columns of V are the eigenvectors of AᵀA. Then uᵢ = A vᵢ / σᵢ.
🧮 Best rank k copy (Eckart and Young)
Aₖ = σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + … + σₖuₖvₖᵀ
Keeping the k biggest singular values gives the closest possible rank k matrix. The error is set by the singular values you dropped.
Use of SVDWhat it does
Image compressionkeep the top k singular values of the picture matrix
Recommendationsthe Netflix Prize matrix of users × films was filled in with low rank factorisations like the SVD
Search (latent semantic analysis)SVD of a words × documents table groups words with related meanings
Pseudo inverse and least squaresA⁺ = V Σ⁺ Uᵀ solves regression even when XᵀX has no inverse
Remember "rotate, stretch, rotate". Vᵀ turns the input, Σ stretches along the axes, U turns the result. The circle of all unit inputs always becomes an ellipse whose half axes are the singular values.
🔍 Going deeper: how much an SVD really saves, and how much it keeps

Keeping only the top k singular values gives a rank k copy of the matrix. Two simple formulas tell you what that costs and what it keeps.

🧮 Storage for a rank k copy
k × (m + n + 1) numbers
k columns of U (m each), k rows of Vᵀ (n each) and k singular values, instead of m × n.
🧮 Share of the "energy" kept
σ₁² + … + σₖ²σ₁² + … + σᵣ²
The squared singular values add up to the sum of all squared entries of the matrix. This share says how much of it the copy keeps.

Natural data almost always has fast falling singular values. A few directions carry the big structure, and the long tail is noise. That is why "throw away the small singular values" works so well for photos, ratings tables and text.

Part 6

🎯Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA finds the directions in which your data spreads out the most. Keep the top few and you can describe 100 features with, say, 10 numbers, while keeping most of the information. It is eigenvectors applied to data.

StepWhat you doWhy
1. Centresubtract the mean of every featureso the cloud sits around the origin
2. CovarianceC = XᵀX / (n − 1) for the centred XC says how features vary together
3. Eigenfind the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Ceigenvectors are the principal directions
4. Sort and keeporder by eigenvalue, keep the top keigenvalue = variance along that direction
5. Projectscores = X × (top k eigenvectors)each row becomes k numbers
🧮 Covariance of two features
cov(x, y) = Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)n − 1
Positive: they rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls. The diagonal of C holds the variances.
🧮 Explained variance ratio
share of PC k = λₖλ₁ + λ₂ + … + λₙ
Keep enough components to reach about 90 to 95 percent.
Scale your features first. If height is in millimetres and weight in tonnes, the millimetre column has a huge variance only because of its unit, and PCA will think it is the most important direction. Standardise each column (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) before PCA.
In practice PCA is computed with the SVD of the centred data matrix X = UΣVᵀ. The columns of V are the principal directions and the eigenvalues of C are σᵢ² / (n − 1). This is what scikit learn PCA does, and it is more accurate than forming C.
A clothing company measures 20 body sizes for each customer. PCA finds that one direction ("overall size") explains most of the spread, and a second ("tall and thin versus short and broad") explains much of the rest. Two numbers now guide the S, M, L sizing.
Part 7

🤖Where machine learning uses all this

Eigenvalues and the SVD appear wherever a model needs the "main directions" of something. Here is a quick map, which later chapters revisit.

AreaWhat the eigen idea gives
Dimensionality reductionPCA compresses features, speeds up training and lets you plot high dimensional data in 2D
Optimisation (Chapter 8)the eigenvalues of the Hessian describe the shape of the loss surface. The ratio λmax / λmin, the condition number, says how slow gradient descent will be
Spectral clusteringeigenvectors of a graph Laplacian split points into groups that ordinary k means misses
Recommendersa low rank factorisation of the users × items table predicts missing ratings
Large language modelsLoRA fine tuning adds a low rank update, and SVD is used to compress weight matrices
Stabilityeigenvalues of recurrent weight matrices above 1 make signals explode, below 1 make them vanish
🧮 Condition number
κ(A) = σmax / σmin
Large κ means a tiny change in the input can cause a large change in the answer. Near duplicate features make κ huge, which is why ridge regression adds λI to XᵀX.
Big eigenvalue = important direction. Tiny eigenvalue = direction you can usually drop. Zero eigenvalue = a direction the matrix throws away completely.
