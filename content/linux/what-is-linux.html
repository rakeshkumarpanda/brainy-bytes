Lesson 1 of 6

What an operating system does

A computer is a box of parts: a processor that does sums very fast, memory that remembers things for a short time, a disk that keeps files when the power is off, a screen, a keyboard and a network card. None of these parts knows how to share itself between the apps you run.

The operating system is the program that manages all of those parts for you. When your music app and your browser both want the processor, it gives each one a turn many times a second. When an app wants to save a file, it decides where on the disk the bytes go. When two people use the same computer, it keeps their files apart.

Part of the computerWhat the operating system does with itAn everyday example
Processor (CPU)Gives every running program a short turn, thousands of times a secondMusic keeps playing while you type a message
Memory (RAM)Gives each program its own space and stops programs reading each otherA crashed game does not break your browser
DiskOrganises bytes into files and foldersYour photos are still there after a restart
DevicesTalks to the keyboard, screen, printer and network card through driversA new mouse works as soon as you plug it in
UsersChecks who you are and what you may touchYour brother cannot read your files on a shared laptop

Windows, macOS, Android, iOS and Linux are all operating systems. This subject is about the Unix family, which includes Linux and macOS, and about Linux most of all, because it is the one you will meet on servers, in the cloud, in phones and in almost every company that writes software.

Real world scenario: your phone

If you have an Android phone, you already use Linux every day. Android is built on the Linux kernel. When you open a camera app, the Linux kernel is the part that talks to the camera chip and hands the picture to the app.

Every time you switch on a computer, a phone or even a smart television, a hidden program wakes up first and takes charge of everything. That program is the operating system. In the 1950s there was no such thing. People booked a computer for an hour, loaded their own program on punched cards, and nobody else could use the machine until they were finished. Operating systems were invented so that many programs, and later many people, could share one expensive machine fairly. Today the same idea runs in your pocket. Your phone has music, maps and messages running at once, and the operating system shares out the processor, the memory and the battery between them, many times every second. In this subject we study the operating system family called Unix, and its most famous member, Linux, which runs most of the servers behind the websites you use.
Lesson 2 of 6

The story of Unix and Linux

Unix was born in 1969 at Bell Labs, the research lab of the American phone company AT&T. Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie wanted a simple, friendly system for programmers, and they built it on a spare PDP-7 computer. In 1973 they rewrote Unix in a new language Ritchie had made, called C. That was a big idea: an operating system written in a high level language could be moved to a new kind of computer by recompiling it, instead of writing it again.

Universities got copies, and the University of California, Berkeley, built its own version called BSD. Companies made their own Unix too, so in 1988 the IEEE published the first POSIX standard, a list of what every Unix-like system must offer, so that programs work on all of them.

In 1983 Richard Stallman announced the GNU project to build a completely free Unix-like system. By 1991 GNU had a compiler, a shell and most tools, but no finished kernel. On 25 August 1991 a 21 year old student in Helsinki, Linus Torvalds, posted that he was making a free operating system kernel "just a hobby, won't be big and professional like gnu". He released Linux 0.01 in September 1991, and in 1992 he put it under the GNU General Public License. GNU tools plus the Linux kernel made a complete free system.

NameWhat it is
UNIX (in capitals)A trademark of The Open Group. Only systems that pass its tests (the Single UNIX Specification) may use the name. macOS is one of them.
Unix-likeAny system that behaves like Unix, certified or not. Linux and FreeBSD are Unix-like.
POSIXThe IEEE standard (IEEE 1003.1) for the commands, shell and C functions a Unix-like system offers. A POSIX shell script runs on Linux, macOS and BSD.
LinuxStrictly, only the kernel that Linus Torvalds started. In daily speech, the whole system built on it.
GNU/LinuxThe name the Free Software Foundation prefers for the whole system, because most of the tools come from GNU.
In the exam
Linux is not Unix code. Linus wrote the kernel from scratch. It is called Unix-like because it follows the same ideas and the POSIX standard.
Here is a story about a spare computer. In 1969, at Bell Labs in New Jersey, Ken Thompson found an old PDP-7 machine that nobody was using and wrote a small operating system on it, partly so he could play a space travel game he had written. That system became Unix. A few years later he and Dennis Ritchie rewrote it in the C language, which meant Unix could be copied to new computers easily, and it spread to universities all over the world. Twenty two years after that, in August 1991, a student called Linus Torvalds in Finland wrote a short message to an online group saying he was building a free kernel as a hobby. He expected a few friends to try it. Today Linux runs every one of the five hundred fastest supercomputers on Earth, most cloud servers, and billions of Android phones. A hobby project grew into one of the largest team efforts in history, with thousands of people adding code every year.
Lesson 3 of 6

Kernel, shell and tools: the layers of Linux

Linux is built in layers, and each layer only talks to the one below it.

LayerWhat it isExamples
HardwareThe real partsCPU, memory, disk, network card
KernelThe core program that controls the hardware. It starts first, never stops, and is the only part allowed to touch devices directlyThe Linux kernel, with its drivers
System librariesReady made functions programs call to ask the kernel for helpglibc, the GNU C library, or musl on Alpine
Shell and toolsThe programs you type commands into, and the small commands themselvesbash, zsh, ls, cp, grep from GNU coreutils and friends
ApplicationsEverything elseBrowsers, databases, web servers, games

The kernel runs in a protected mode of the processor called kernel space. Your programs run in user space. A user program that wants to read a file cannot read the disk itself. It asks the kernel with a system call, and the kernel checks that you are allowed, does the work and hands back the result. This is why one broken program cannot crash the whole computer.

Going deeper: Why a kernel version looks like 6.8.0

Linux versions are written major.minor.patch, for example 6.8.0. Since Linux 3.0 in 2011 the first number has no special meaning: Linus moves it up when the second number grows large. A new minor version comes out about every nine or ten weeks. Distributions add their own suffix, so Ubuntu shows something like 6.8.0-45-generic.

Think about ordering food in a restaurant. You do not walk into the kitchen and use the stove yourself. You tell the waiter, the waiter tells the kitchen, and the food comes back to your table. Linux works in the same safe way. A program never touches the disk or the network card directly. It asks the kernel through a system call, and the kernel checks the request first. This idea, called protected mode, arrived in ordinary personal computers with the Intel 80386 processor in 1985, and Linus Torvalds wrote the first Linux kernel for exactly that processor. Because of these layers, a program that crashes only hurts itself, and the rest of the system keeps working. When you learn the commands in this subject, remember that every one of them is just a small program in user space, asking the kernel for help.
Lesson 4 of 6

Distributions: many flavours, one kernel

Nobody installs only a kernel. A distribution (distro) is a complete package: the Linux kernel, the GNU tools, a package manager to install software, a desktop if you want one, and a team that tests and updates it all. They all run the same commands you learn here, with small differences in how software is installed.

Remember
An Ubuntu version number is the year and month of its release. 24.04 means April 2024. LTS versions come out in April of even years: 20.04, 22.04, 24.04.
Remember
Learn the commands, not the distribution. ls, grep and chmod work the same everywhere. Only the package tool (apt, dnf, pacman, apk) changes between families.
When Linux was young, installing it meant downloading the kernel and dozens of tools separately and putting them together by hand, which only experts could do. In 1993 two of the oldest distributions still alive today appeared. Slackware came first in July, and Debian followed in August, started by a student called Ian Murdock. A distribution does all the putting together for you. In 2004 a company called Canonical released Ubuntu, based on Debian, with the goal of making Linux easy for everyone, and it became very popular. Today there are hundreds of distributions, but most belong to a few families. If you use Ubuntu at home and your company uses Red Hat Enterprise Linux, nearly everything you type is the same. Only the way you install software changes, apt on one and dnf on the other.

Common mistake
Commands are case sensitive. ls works, LS gives "command not found". File names are case sensitive too: notes.txt and Notes.txt are two different files.
There is a famous saying among people who learn Linux. You do not learn it by reading, you learn it by typing. When the Windows Subsystem for Linux arrived in 2016, millions of Windows users suddenly had a real Linux shell one command away, and it became one of the most common ways students start. Many cloud companies also give a free small Linux server for learning. On this page you have the practice shell, a pretend Linux computer that lives inside the page. It has a user called learner, a home folder with a few files, and a list of missions. Try the missions now. Nothing you type there can harm your real computer, so be curious. Type a command, read what comes back, and if something looks strange, that is exactly the moment you learn the most.
Lesson 6 of 6

The Unix way of thinking, from school to research

Unix came with a way of building software that people still follow. Doug McIlroy, who invented Unix pipes, summed it up like this (as quoted by Peter Salus in 1994): write programs that do one thing and do it well, write programs to work together, and write programs to handle text streams, because that is a universal interface.

Unix ideaWhat it meansWhere you will see it
Small toolsEach command does one job, for example sort only sortsls, sort, wc, grep
Pipes join toolsThe output of one command becomes the input of the nextsort fruits.txt | uniq -c
Text everywhereSettings, logs and data are plain text you can read and edit/etc/passwd, /var/log, CSV files
Everything is a fileDevices, processes and even kernel settings appear as files/dev/null, /proc/cpuinfo
Silence is goldenA command that works prints nothing extracp and mkdir print nothing when they succeed
One idea, four depths

Linux is the boss program inside a computer. It shares the computer between all the apps, keeps everyone’s files safe, and lets you give orders by typing short words like ls (show my files) into a black window called a terminal.

Linux is a free, open source, Unix-like operating system kernel. Together with GNU tools and a package manager it forms a distribution. Users work through a shell, which runs small single purpose programs that can be combined with pipes, following the Unix philosophy and the POSIX standard.

Linux is a monolithic kernel with loadable modules. Processes in user space reach kernel services through system calls, with memory protection by virtual memory and privilege rings. Its interfaces follow POSIX and the Single UNIX Specification closely, so software is portable across Unix-like systems. Distributions differ in libc (glibc, musl), init system, packaging and release model.

Open problems include safer kernel code (the Rust for Linux effort, merged as experimental in Linux 6.1 in December 2022), real time behaviour (PREEMPT_RT was merged into the main kernel in Linux 6.12 in 2024), eBPF programs that run safely inside the kernel, scaling to thousands of cores, and studies of the kernel development process itself, one of the largest collaborations in software.

Real world scenario: a website you used today

When you watched a video or searched the web today, your request almost certainly landed on Linux servers. Most of them have no screen at all. Engineers manage them only through a shell over the network, using exactly the commands this subject teaches.

In 1978 the Bell System Technical Journal printed a special issue about Unix, and in it Doug McIlroy wrote down rules for building programs the Unix way. The best known summary is very short: do one thing well, work together, and use text. These rules explain why Linux feels the way it does. Instead of one giant program that does everything, you get hundreds of small commands, and you join them like building blocks. For example, one command lists files, a second one counts lines, and together they count your files. This idea has lasted more than fifty years and still shapes modern software, from small command line tools to the way big cloud systems are split into small services. As you go through this subject, notice how often a hard job becomes easy when you join two or three small tools.
