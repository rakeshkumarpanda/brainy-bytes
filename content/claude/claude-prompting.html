Prompt Engineering That Works

The techniques from Anthropic's own prompting guide, each with a real daily task: clear instructions, reasons, examples, XML tags, roles, output format, thinking, templates and prompt chains.

🧒 In plain words

A prompt is a message that tells Claude what you want. Good prompts are like good instructions to a new classmate: say what, for whom, why, how long, and show an example.

Part 1

🎯Be clear and direct

🧒 Beginner friendly

Anthropic's own advice is to treat Claude like a brilliant but new employee. It is very capable, but it does not know your customers, your style or what "good" means to you. The golden rule: if a colleague who knows nothing about the task would be confused by your prompt, Claude will be too.

😕 Vague
A vague prompt · 💬 Prompt to try
Write something about our new product.
What happens

Claude has to guess the product, the reader, the length and the purpose. You get a generic paragraph you cannot use.

😀 Clear
The same task, said clearly · 💬 Prompt to try
Write a 60 word Instagram caption for "Mango Lassi Ice Cream", our new flavour.

Reader: college students in Bengaluru who follow our cafe.
Goal: get them to visit this weekend for the 2 for 1 offer.
Tone: playful, one emoji at most.
End with the line: "Only at Scoops, Indiranagar."
What happens

A caption of about 60 words, aimed at students, that mentions the weekend offer and ends with the exact closing line.

A clear prompt answers five questions: what to make, for whom, why, how long and what it must include. When the order of steps matters, number them.

Part 2

💡Say why, not only what

🧒 Beginner friendly

Claude follows a rule better when it knows the reason. A bare rule covers one case. A reason lets Claude handle cases you did not think of.

Give the reason, not only the rule · 💬 Prompt to try
Summarise this lesson for my students.

Some of my students use a screen reader, which reads every symbol out loud.
So please write in full sentences and avoid tables, arrows and emoji.
What happens

Claude avoids tables, arrows and emoji, and also avoids other symbols you did not list, because it understands the reason.

Ask for what you want, not only what you do not want. "Write in flowing paragraphs" works better than "do not use bullet points".
Part 3

🧪Show examples

🧒 Beginner friendly

Examples are one of the most reliable ways to control format and tone. This is called few shot or multishot prompting. The docs suggest three to five examples that are close to your real task, different from each other, and wrapped in <example> tags so Claude can tell them apart from your instructions.

Teach the pattern with examples · 💬 Prompt to try
Sort each customer message into one of: refund, delivery, product_question, other.
Reply with the label only.

<examples>
<example>
<message>My parcel says delivered but I never got it</message>
<label>delivery</label>
</example>
<example>
<message>Is the steel bottle safe for hot tea?</message>
<label>product_question</label>
</example>
<example>
<message>I was charged twice, please return one payment</message>
<label>refund</label>
</example>
</examples>

<message>The lid of my lunch box cracked on day one, I want my money back</message>
What happens

Claude replies with the single word refund, in the same format as the examples.

If all your examples look alike, Claude may copy accidental details too, such as always starting with the same word. Vary them.
Part 4

🏷️Use XML tags to separate the pieces

A prompt often mixes instructions, background, examples and data. Put each kind in its own tag, such as <instructions>, <context> or <notes>. There are no magic tag names. Pick clear ones and use them the same way every time.

For long material, the docs give two tips: put the documents at the top and the question at the end (this improved answers by up to 30 percent in Anthropic's tests), and give each document its own tag with its source.

Several documents in XML tags · ▶ Runs
// Wrapping each piece of a prompt in its own tag stops Claude mixing them up.
// This helper builds a prompt with several documents, the way the docs recommend:
// long documents first, the question last.
function buildPrompt(docs, question) {
  const body = docs.map((d, i) =>
    `  <document index="${i + 1}">\n    <source>${d.source}</source>\n` +
    `    <document_content>\n${d.text}\n    </document_content>\n  </document>`).join("\n");
  return `<documents>\n${body}\n</documents>\n\n${question}`;
}

const prompt = buildPrompt(
  [{ source: "leave-policy.pdf", text: "Staff get 18 days of paid leave a year." },
   { source: "holiday-list-2026.xlsx", text: "Office closed on 15 Aug, 2 Oct and 25 Dec." }],
  "Using only the documents above, how many paid days off does a new hire get in 2026? Quote the lines you used.");

console.log(prompt);
Output
<documents>
  <document index="1">
    <source>leave-policy.pdf</source>
    <document_content>
Staff get 18 days of paid leave a year.
    </document_content>
  </document>
  <document index="2">
    <source>holiday-list-2026.xlsx</source>
    <document_content>
Office closed on 15 Aug, 2 Oct and 25 Dec.
    </document_content>
  </document>
</documents>

Using only the documents above, how many paid days off does a new hire get in 2026? Quote the lines you used.
Asking Claude to quote the lines it used before answering keeps it grounded in your documents instead of its general knowledge.
Part 5

🎭Give Claude a role

📄 system prompt

One sentence about who Claude is for this task changes tone and focus. In the API this goes in the system prompt. In claude.ai it goes in project instructions. In Claude Code the closest place is CLAUDE.md or an output style.

A role set in the system prompt (Python) · 🔑 Needs an API key, shown not run
import anthropic

client = anthropic.Anthropic()            # reads ANTHROPIC_API_KEY from the environment

message = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-opus-5-5",
    max_tokens=1024,
    system="You are a friendly maths tutor for children aged 9 to 11. "
           "Explain with small numbers and everyday objects. Ask one question back.",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": "Why is 3 x 4 the same as 4 x 3?"}],
)
for block in message.content:
    if block.type == "text":
        print(block.text)
What happens

Claude answers as a tutor, with something like rows of 3 apples and 4 apples, and ends with a small question for the child.

Part 6

📐Control the format of the answer

🧒 Beginner friendly

Tell Claude the exact shape: a numbered list, a table, JSON, a word limit, a closing line. Showing the shape works even better.

Ask for the exact shape you want · 💬 Prompt to try
Read the meeting notes below and return the action items.

Write your answer as a numbered list. Each line has the owner's name,
then the task, then the due date in the form 12 Nov.
If a task has no owner, write "Owner needed".
Respond directly, without an introduction.

<notes>
Priya will update the price sheet by Thursday 13 Nov.
We need someone to call the printer about the brochure.
Ravi to send the client the revised quote on 14 Nov.
</notes>
What happens

1. Priya, update the price sheet, 13 Nov 2. Owner needed, call the printer about the brochure, no date 3. Ravi, send the revised quote, 14 Nov

Older guides tell you to "prefill" the start of Claude's answer. Since the Claude 4.6 models, a prefilled final assistant message returns an error. Ask for the format in words, use tags, or use structured outputs in the API instead.
Part 7

🤔Let Claude think before answering

For sums, comparisons and decisions, ask Claude to work through the problem first. Tags make it easy to keep the working separate from the final answer, so a script can read just the answer.

Ask Claude to think first · 💬 Prompt to try
Our shop sold 1,240 items in March and 1,550 in April.
Returns were 62 in March and 93 in April.

Before you answer, work through it step by step inside <thinking> tags.
Then give the final answer inside <answer> tags.

Question: did the return rate go up or down, and by how many percentage points?
What happens

Inside thinking: 62 ÷ 1,240 = 5.0% and 93 ÷ 1,550 = 6.0%. Inside answer: the return rate went up by 1 percentage point.

In the API, current models also have adaptive thinking: Claude decides how much to think on its own. You switch it on with thinking: {type: "adaptive"}. Chapter 12 shows it.

Part 8

🧩Prompt templates for daily work

🧒 Beginner friendly

When you send the same kind of prompt every day, turn it into a template with blanks in double braces. Fill the blanks each time. Later, the same template becomes a skill (chapter 7) or a line in a script (chapter 11).

Fill a reusable prompt template · ▶ Runs
// A prompt template: write the prompt once, fill in the details every day.
const TEMPLATE = `You are helping {{name}}, who works as {{job}}.

<task>
{{task}}
</task>

Rules:
- Keep the answer under {{words}} words.
- Use simple English.`;

function fill(template, values) {
  return template.replace(/{{(\w+)}}/g, (whole, key) => {
    if (!(key in values)) throw new Error("missing value for " + key);
    return values[key];
  });
}

console.log(fill(TEMPLATE, {
  name: "Anita", job: "an HR executive", words: 120,
  task: "Draft a polite reminder to staff to submit timesheets by Friday 5pm.",
}));
Output
You are helping Anita, who works as an HR executive.

<task>
Draft a polite reminder to staff to submit timesheets by Friday 5pm.
</task>

Rules:
- Keep the answer under 120 words.
- Use simple English.
Daily inbox triage prompt · 💬 Prompt to try
<role>You are my executive assistant.</role>

<inbox>
(paste today's unread emails here)
</inbox>

For each email give me:
1. One line summary
2. Urgency: today, this week or no rush
3. A two sentence reply draft in my voice: friendly and short

Then list the three things I should do first today.
What happens

A short block per email with a summary, an urgency tag and a reply draft, then a top three list for the day.

A simple prompt checker · ▶ Runs
// A tiny prompt checker. It looks for the things good prompts usually have.
function checkPrompt(p) {
  const tips = [];
  if (p.split(/\s+/).length < 12) tips.push("Too short: add who it is for and why.");
  if (!/\d+\s*(words|lines|bullet|items|sentences)|table|list|json/i.test(p)) tips.push("Say the format or length you want.");
  if (/\b(something|stuff|good|nice|etc)\b/i.test(p)) tips.push("Replace vague words with specifics.");
  if (p.length > 400 && !/<\w+>/.test(p)) tips.push("Long input: wrap data in tags like <notes>.");
  return tips.length ? tips : ["Looks clear."];
}

console.log(checkPrompt("Write something nice about our product"));
console.log(checkPrompt("Write a 60 word caption for college students about our 2 for 1 lassi offer this weekend, playful tone."));
Output
[
  'Too short: add who it is for and why.',
  'Say the format or length you want.',
  'Replace vague words with specifics.'
]
[ 'Say the format or length you want.' ]
Part 9

⛓️Chain prompts: draft, review, refine

Big jobs go better in steps. The most useful chain is self correction: one prompt drafts, one checks the draft against a list, one rewrites. You can read each step and stop if something is off.

A three step chain: draft, review, refine · 💬 Prompt to try
Step 1 prompt:  "Draft a 150 word apology email to a customer whose order was late. <order>...</order>"
Step 2 prompt:  "Review this draft against the checklist: polite, gives a reason, gives a new date,
                 offers the 10% voucher, under 150 words. List what is missing. <draft>...</draft>"
Step 3 prompt:  "Rewrite the draft so it passes every item. <draft>...</draft> <review>...</review>"
What happens

Each step is a separate request, so you can read the review before the rewrite and catch problems early.

🏢 Real scenario: a monthly reportAn operations lead pastes raw numbers. Prompt 1 writes the summary. Prompt 2 checks every number against the table. Prompt 3 fixes the two numbers that did not match. Errors that once reached the director now stop at step 2.
Part 10

📋A recipe card for any prompt

🧒 Beginner friendly
  1. Role: who Claude is for this task.
  2. Context: who it is for and why it matters.
  3. Data: pasted inside tags, long material first.
  4. Task: numbered steps if order matters.
  5. Format: shape, length, what to include.
  6. Examples: three to five, inside example tags, when format matters.
  7. Check: ask for quotes or a short self check when facts matter.
You do not need all seven every time. A quick question needs one line. A task you will repeat a hundred times deserves all seven.
